\title [mode = title]{Mesh-to-Mesh Transfer Errors in BDF2 Flow Simulations}
\input{submission/author_details.tex}

\begin{abstract}
A mesh change alters the fields from which an unsteady flow calculation resumes. We relate the accuracy, bounds, and conservation of transferred fields to the subsequent solution in vertex-centered finite-volume calculations. For the second-order backward differentiation formula (BDF2), we derive the weighted contribution of the two stored fields to the next state and to an aerodynamic load. Controlled airfoil restarts recover the predicted lift ratios when the same discrepancy is applied to different histories. A discrete mass balance separates transfer changes from flow evolution in a shock tube. For control-volume transfer, numerical influx through the open ends explains the mass increase, while the final shock position differs from the fixed-mesh result by $0.19$ of the original cell spacing. Restoring total mass leaves the smeared profile from first-order nearest-neighbor transfer largely unchanged. Airfoil calculations show a delayed near-wall pressure response to a remote mesh change. In nominally steady viscous flow, all three tested methods produce similar load departures, including control-volume transfer that preserves the mean-flow integrals. The departures persist at smaller time steps and decrease on the refined mesh. These results connect transfer properties to the history, boundary fluxes, and spatial disturbances that govern the restarted flow.
\end{abstract}

\begin{highlights}
\item Analysis and airfoil tests quantify the separate effects of both BDF2 histories.
\item Galerkin and control-volume transfer can preserve different mass integrals.
\item Numerical boundary influx explains mass gain in the conservative shock-tube run.
\item Remote remeshing produces a delayed pressure response near the airfoil.
\item Airfoil lift and drag change even when transfer preserves mean-flow integrals.
\end{highlights}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Time-dependent mesh adaptation changes the spatial resolution of a flow calculation as the solution evolves~\cite{AlauzetLoseilleOlivier2018,SauvageAlauzetDervieux2024}. Resuming the calculation on each new mesh requires transferring a discrete solution. The resulting errors can affect the subsequent flow and the aerodynamic loads, even when the transfer enforces a domain integral.

On smooth fields, transfer accuracy describes how well the target values approximate the field and how that error decreases under refinement. Bounds constrain the range of target values, and conservation constrains selected integrals. Intersection-based methods integrate a reconstructed source field over target cells~\cite{DukowiczKodis1987,MargolinShashkov2003,AlauzetMehrenberger2010,Alauzet2016}. Galerkin projection uses element intersections to match moments against target basis functions~\cite{JiaoHeath2004,FarrellMaddison2011} and has been used for conservative transfer in time-accurate discontinuous Galerkin calculations~\cite{LevyMay2024,LevyMay2025}. Which integral a method preserves matters when the flow solver uses different weights to compute its conserved totals. The distribution of the transferred field matters as well. Perturbations concentrated near a shock can alter its speed~\cite{Guglielmi2026}, and the loss of a discrete balance can excite disturbances, as illustrated by equilibrium-preserving interpolation for shallow water~\cite{MaddisonCotterFarrell2011,MaddisonCotterFarrell2013}.

Here, a \emph{remeshing event} is the replacement of the mesh and the transfer of the fields needed to resume time integration. It takes place between physical time steps. The second-order backward differentiation formula (BDF2) uses the latest field and the field from one step earlier. At an event we apply the transfer separately to the source values at each stored time. Both results live on the same replacement mesh and retain their original times. The older field can therefore affect the next step even when the latest field agrees with a target-mesh reference.

We compare nearest-neighbor Taylor interpolation (NN), barycentric interpolation (BAR), continuous Galerkin projection (GP), and conservative control-volume transfer (CV) for a vertex-centered finite-volume discretization. Before limiting and other corrections, CV preserves the solver's control-volume sum and GP preserves the integral of its finite-element field. The analysis connects the two BDF2 history discrepancies to the first-step response and derives the propagation of mass changes under BDF2.

The numerical tests follow that connection from transfer to flow evolution. Analytic fields isolate accuracy, bounds, and conservation at transfer. Controlled airfoil restarts test the predicted history weights for the next-step lift. A shock tube then compares wave shape and position and accounts for mass changes over repeated events. The final airfoil cases measure the time for a disturbance away from the body to affect near-wall pressure and the sensitivity of viscous loads to repeated remeshing. We prescribe the mesh changes on fixed two-dimensional domains and use fixed-mesh references to measure the combined effects of transfer and subsequent evolution on the changed meshes.

\section{Transferring a solution between meshes}
\label{sec:numerical-framework}

The four methods differ in how they compute values on the target mesh: NN and BAR interpolate from nearby source values, whereas GP and CV solve coupled systems built from integrals. Each method can then apply limiting, boundary conditions, and positivity corrections, and we distinguish the raw field from the corrected field passed to the flow solver. Method suffixes give the nominal order of accuracy, not the polynomial degree: BAR2 is second order, and GP2 and CV2 use piecewise-linear fields. For CV2, the element geometry can reduce the order below two (Section~\ref{sec:cv-transfer}).

\subsection{Stored variables and reconstructed derivatives}
\label{sec:derivative-reconstruction}

The source mesh $A$ and the target mesh $B$ cover the same discretized domain $\Omega$ in $d$ space dimensions. A mesh $M$ has $n_M$ solver vertices; vertex $i$ has position $\boldsymbol x_i^M$ and a median-dual control volume $C_i^M$ of positive measure $w_i^M=|C_i^M|$. The solver state $U_M$ stacks $n_U$ transported variables per vertex. For one scalar component $\phi$, the vector $\phi_M=(\phi_{M,1},\ldots,\phi_{M,n_M})$ collects the vertex values, and the solver computes its integral as
\begin{equation}
 Q_M(\phi_M)=\sum_{i=1}^{n_M}w_i^M\phi_{M,i}.
 \label{eq:fv-discrete-integral}
\end{equation}
For the full state, $Q_M(U_M)$ denotes the vector of these integrals, one per transported component. The numerical tests measure the change of an integral across transfer by the signed relative change $e_Q(\phi_A,\phi_B)=[Q_B(\phi_B)-Q_A(\phi_A)]/Q_A(\phi_A)$, defined for a nonzero source integral.

The mean-flow variables are density $\rho$, momentum density $\rho\boldsymbol u$, and total energy per unit volume $\rho E$, where $\boldsymbol u$ is the velocity, with Cartesian components $(u,v)$ in two dimensions, and $E$ is the total energy per unit mass. All transfer methods return values at the solver vertices.

The higher-order methods also need gradients and Hessians, which weighted least squares reconstructs from neighboring source values:
\begin{equation}
 \phi_j-\phi_i\simeq \boldsymbol g_i\cdot\boldsymbol r_{ij}
              +\tfrac12\boldsymbol r_{ij}^{T}\mathcal H_i\boldsymbol r_{ij},
 \qquad \boldsymbol r_{ij}=\boldsymbol x_j-\boldsymbol x_i.
 \label{eq:wls-polynomial}
\end{equation}
Here $\boldsymbol r_{ij}$ joins source vertices $i$ and $j$, and $\boldsymbol g_i$ and $\mathcal H_i$ approximate the gradient and Hessian of $\phi$ at $i$; a linear fit omits the Hessian. For smooth data on a well-conditioned, full-rank scaled stencil with characteristic local mesh spacing $h$, the linear fit has an $O(h)$ gradient error, and the quadratic fit has $O(h^2)$ gradient and $O(h)$ Hessian errors. Anisotropy, weighting, and poor conditioning can reduce this accuracy~\cite{BarthFrederickson1990,Barth1993,Mavriplis2003,DiskinThomas2008}. The third-order methods use the quadratic fit, and their achieved order is checked numerically.

\subsection{Nearest-neighbor and barycentric interpolation}
\label{sec:point-transfer}
\label{sec:barycentric-transfer}

We write $\calI_{AB}$ with a method label for the raw interpolation, before limiting and state corrections. Let $i_{\mathrm{NN}}(j)$ be the source vertex nearest to the target point $\boldsymbol x_j^B$, and let $\boldsymbol r_j=\boldsymbol x_j^B-\boldsymbol x_{i_{\mathrm{NN}}(j)}^A$ be the displacement between them. Nearest-neighbor Taylor transfer is
\begin{align}
 (\calI^{\mathrm{NN1}}_{AB}\phi_A)_j
 &=\phi_{A,i_{\mathrm{NN}}(j)},\label{eq:nn1}\\
 (\calI^{\mathrm{NN2}}_{AB}\phi_A)_j
 &=\phi_{A,i_{\mathrm{NN}}(j)}
       +\boldsymbol g_{i_{\mathrm{NN}}(j)}\cdot\boldsymbol r_j,\label{eq:nn2}\\
 (\calI^{\mathrm{NN3}}_{AB}\phi_A)_j
 &=\phi_{A,i_{\mathrm{NN}}(j)}
       +\boldsymbol g_{i_{\mathrm{NN}}(j)}\cdot\boldsymbol r_j
       +\tfrac12\boldsymbol r_j^T\mathcal H_{i_{\mathrm{NN}}(j)}\boldsymbol r_j.
 \label{eq:nn3}
\end{align}
NN1 copies the nearest source value, NN2 adds the local slope, and NN3 adds the curvature. For $\|\boldsymbol r_j\|_2=O(h)$, where $\|\cdot\|_2$ is the Euclidean norm, and sufficiently accurate derivatives, the smooth-field errors are $O(h)$, $O(h^2)$, and $O(h^3)$. Because the nearest source vertex changes abruptly with the target position, the interpolated value can be discontinuous.

Barycentric interpolation uses the source simplex (triangle or tetrahedron) that contains the target point; other element types are first subdivided into simplices without adding vertices. In dimension $d$, let the containing simplex have vertices $\boldsymbol x_{i_r}^A$, $r=1,\ldots,d+1$. The barycentric coordinates $\lambda_r$ of the target point are the nonnegative weights that satisfy
\begin{equation}
 \boldsymbol x_j^B=\sum_{r=1}^{d+1}\lambda_r \boldsymbol x_{i_r}^A,
 \qquad \sum_r\lambda_r=1,
 \qquad \lambda_r\geq0.
 \label{eq:barycentric-coordinates}
\end{equation}
They define BAR2 and its gradient-corrected extension BAR3:
\begin{align}
 (\calI^{\mathrm{BAR2}}_{AB}\phi_A)_j
 &=\sum_r\lambda_r\phi_{A,i_r},\label{eq:bar2}\\
 (\calI^{\mathrm{BAR3}}_{AB}\phi_A)_j
 &=\sum_r\lambda_r\left[\phi_{A,i_r}
       +\tfrac12\boldsymbol g_{i_r}\cdot(\boldsymbol x_j^B-\boldsymbol x_{i_r}^A)\right].\label{eq:bar3}
\end{align}
The barycentric coordinates in Eq.~\eqref{eq:barycentric-coordinates} make BAR2 exact for affine fields. Their nonnegativity and unit sum also keep the result within the range of the containing simplex's vertex values. BAR3 adds half of each vertex gradient correction, which makes it third-order consistent, as shown next. Values and gradients are shared between neighboring simplices, so both interpolants are continuous across conforming simplex interfaces. Neither method constrains the source-to-target integral.

The factor of one half follows from a Taylor expansion. Let $q$ be a quadratic field with constant Hessian $\mathcal H_q$, let $\boldsymbol x=\boldsymbol x_j^B$, and set $\boldsymbol d_r=\boldsymbol x-\boldsymbol x_{i_r}^A$. Then
\begin{align*}
q(\boldsymbol x_{i_r}^A)&=q(\boldsymbol x)-\nabla q(\boldsymbol x)\cdot\boldsymbol d_r+\tfrac12\boldsymbol d_r^T\mathcal H_q\boldsymbol d_r,\\
\nabla q(\boldsymbol x_{i_r}^A)&=\nabla q(\boldsymbol x)-\mathcal H_q\boldsymbol d_r.
\end{align*}
Substituting these expressions into Eq.~\eqref{eq:bar3} gives $q(\boldsymbol x)-\tfrac12\nabla q(\boldsymbol x)\cdot\sum_r\lambda_r\boldsymbol d_r=q(\boldsymbol x)$, because $\sum_r\lambda_r\boldsymbol d_r=0$ by Eq.~\eqref{eq:barycentric-coordinates}. BAR3 is therefore exact for quadratic fields with exact vertex gradients. For a smooth field on a shape-regular simplex, the Taylor remainder is $O(h^3)$, and an $O(h^2)$ gradient error multiplied by the $O(h)$ displacement contributes at the same order.

\subsection{Galerkin projection and cross-mesh integration}
\label{sec:galerkin-projection}
\label{sec:simplicial-representation}

Galerkin projection chooses the target field closest to the reconstructed source field in the $L^2$ norm~\cite{FarrellMaddison2011,JiaoHeath2004}. The transfer uses finite-element basis functions, although the flow equations here remain finite volume, and it solves for all target values together instead of interpolating each vertex independently. Let $\phi_A^h$ be the piecewise-polynomial field built from the source vertex values and, for GP3, their reconstructed derivatives. Let $\mathcal V_B^\ell$ be the space of continuous Lagrange functions of polynomial degree $\ell$ on the target simplicial mesh, and write $P^\ell$ for polynomials of total degree at most $\ell$. With the inner product $(f_1,f_2)_{L^2(\Omega)}=\int_\Omega f_1f_2\dd x$, the target field $\phi_B^h\in\mathcal V_B^\ell$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
 (\phi_B^h-\phi_A^h,\xi_B)_{L^2(\Omega)}=0
 \quad\text{for every }\xi_B\in\mathcal V_B^\ell.
 \label{eq:galerkin-orthogonality}
\end{equation}
The projection difference is orthogonal to every target test function. This makes $\phi_B^h$ the best $L^2$ approximation of $\phi_A^h$ in $\mathcal V_B^\ell$~\cite{FarrellMaddison2011,JiaoHeath2004}. Expanding $\phi_B^h=\sum_jz_{B,j}N_j^B$ in the nodal basis functions $N_j^B$ of $\mathcal V_B^\ell$ and taking $\xi_B=N_i^B$ gives the linear system
\begin{equation}
 H_Bz_B=b,\qquad
 (H_B)_{ij}=\int_\Omega N_i^BN_j^B\dd x,
 \qquad b_i=\int_\Omega N_i^B\phi_A^h\dd x.
 \label{eq:galerkin-system}
\end{equation}
The consistent Galerkin mass matrix $H_B$ is sparse and positive definite. It couples the basis functions that share a target element. The vector $b$ is the cross-mesh load vector. For GP2, the source field $\phi_A^h$ on each source triangle is the barycentric interpolation of its three vertex values. The target basis function $N_i^B$ is the hat function of target vertex $i$: it equals one at that vertex and decreases linearly to zero on the opposite edge of each triangle that contains it.

The load vector integrates the product of two fields that are piecewise polynomial on different meshes. A target element generally overlaps several source elements, across which the source polynomial changes, so the integral is assembled over the element intersections:
\begin{equation}
 b_i=\sum_{K_B\ni i}\sum_{K_A:\,|K_A\cap K_B|>0}
      \int_{K_A\cap K_B}N_i^B\phi_A^h\dd x.
 \label{eq:galerkin-load-intersections}
\end{equation}
Here $K_A$ is a source simplex, $K_B$ is a target simplex incident to $i$, and $|K_A\cap K_B|$ is the measure of their intersection. The intersections form a common refinement of the two meshes (Figure~\ref{fig:transfer-geometry}a) and must cover the domain without gaps or overlap. On each intersection both fields are single polynomials, so quadrature is exact: degree two suffices for $P^1$/$P^1$ transfer and degree four for $P^2$/$P^2$ transfer on affine simplices~\cite{Dunavant1985,Keast1986}. This geometric integration and the coupled solve distinguish GP from NN and BAR, which evaluate a source reconstruction at each target vertex independently.

GP2 uses piecewise-linear ($P^1$) source and target fields. GP3 reconstructs a quadratic ($P^2$) source field and solves for a quadratic target field. At a source edge midpoint, the reconstruction uses the mean of the two vertex values minus $\boldsymbol d^T(\mathcal H_i+\mathcal H_j)\boldsymbol d/16$, where $\boldsymbol d=\boldsymbol x_i^A-\boldsymbol x_j^A$. This midpoint formula is exact for a quadratic with its exact Hessian. The solver receives only the original-vertex values. Mixed-element meshes use $P^\ell$ on simplicial subdivisions. Taking the test function equal to one in Eq.~\eqref{eq:galerkin-orthogonality} shows that the projection matches the source and target integrals of the complete finite-element fields. The flow solver forms $Q_M$ from the original vertex values and their median-dual weights. The finite-element integral equals $Q_M$ for a piecewise-linear field on a triangular mesh. On quadrilaterals the simplicial subdivision can give different weights, and for GP3 the finite-element integral also involves edge values. We therefore measure $Q_B$ on the final solver values in the numerical tests. The smooth-field tests check the accuracy of these stored vertex values. Joining them linearly across each target triangle gives a different field from the quadratic one used in the GP3 solve.

\subsection{Conservative control-volume transfer}
\label{sec:cv-transfer}

The CV method matches the integrals of the source and target fields over each target control volume. This requires a field that can be integrated over parts of cells. The domain integral of that field must also equal the solver total, which is the sum of the vertex values multiplied by their control-volume measures. We construct such a field as follows. Both meshes represent the field as $\phi_M^h=\sum_k\phi_{M,k}\psi_k^M$. On triangles and tetrahedra, $\psi_k^M=N_k^M$ is the usual $P^1$ hat function. A quadrilateral is divided into triangles by joining its vertices to their average center (Figure~\ref{fig:transfer-geometry}b), and the value at that center is a weighted combination of the vertex values, not an independent unknown. Nonsimplicial three-dimensional elements use analogous centered subdivisions. The weights are chosen so that
\begin{equation}
 \int_K\psi_k^M\dd x=|C_k^M\cap K|
 \qquad\text{for every element $K$ and vertex $k$ of $M$,}
 \label{eq:cv-lumped-exact}
\end{equation}
where $C_k^M\cap K$ is the part of the control volume of vertex $k$ inside element $K$. For a quadrilateral, this gives vertex $k$ the center weight $|C_k^M\cap K|/|K|$. Multiplying Eq.~\eqref{eq:cv-lumped-exact} by the stored value $\phi_{M,k}$ and summing over the vertices of $K$ gives $\int_K\phi_M^h\dd x=\sum_{k\in K}|C_k^M\cap K|\phi_{M,k}$, and summing over all elements gives exactly the solver total $Q_M(\phi_M)$. The reconstructed field and the solver therefore have the same domain integral.

On parallelograms, the center weights give the arithmetic vertex average and reproduce affine fields. On distorted quadrilaterals the center weights can differ from the arithmetic average. For an affine field the center-value error is the gradient dotted with the difference between the weighted average of the vertex positions and the arithmetic mean of the vertex positions. The weights enforce Eq.~\eqref{eq:cv-lumped-exact} at the expense of affine reproduction.

The $n_B$ target vertex values are defined by requiring that the reconstructed target and source fields have the same integral over each target control volume:
\begin{equation}
 \int_{C_j^B}\left(\phi_B^h-\phi_A^h\right)\dd x=0,
 \qquad j=1,\ldots,n_B.
 \label{eq:cv-condition}
\end{equation}
A control volume usually overlaps several source elements, which supply its source contributions.

Conservative remapping can instead match integrals over whole target elements. The intersection-based $P^1$ methods of Alauzet and Mehrenberger~\cite{AlauzetMehrenberger2010} and Alauzet~\cite{Alauzet2016} take that route: they limit an element-wise linear reconstruction and recover vertex values by volume-weighted averaging over the incident elements. Because the flow solver here stores values at vertices and forms its total with median-dual weights, the CV construction uses the target control volumes directly. Each integral in Eq.~\eqref{eq:cv-condition} involves several target vertex values, which gives the coupled system
\begin{equation}
 \widetilde H_B\phi_B=\tilde b,\qquad
 (\widetilde H_B)_{jk}=\int_{C_j^B}\psi_k^B\dd x,
 \qquad \tilde b_j=\int_{C_j^B}\phi_A^h\dd x.
 \label{eq:cv-system}
\end{equation}
The functions $\psi_k^B$ sum to one, so the row sums are $w_j^B$, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:cv-lumped-exact} gives column sums $w_k^B$. The entries are nonnegative, depend only on the target geometry, are evaluated in closed form, and couple vertices within each target element. On triangles and tetrahedra, vertex permutation symmetry makes the element matrix symmetric. On a convex quadrilateral $K$, write $w_{j,K}=|C_j^B\cap K|$. Before eliminating the center value, direct integration of the piecewise-linear basis gives a vertex block with diagonal $w_{j,K}/2$ and off-diagonal entry $a/12$ for vertices joined by an edge, where $a$ is the area of the triangle from that edge to the center. The center basis contributes $w_{j,K}/3$ to row $j$. Substituting the center weights $w_{k,K}/|K|$ adds the symmetric entry $w_{j,K}w_{k,K}/(3|K|)$. The resulting diagonal is greater than half the row sum $w_{j,K}$. For simplices the diagonal divided by the row sum is $11/18$ on triangles and $25/48$ on tetrahedra, from averaging the corner basis over each median-dual piece. Assembly thus gives a symmetric strictly diagonally dominant matrix with a positive diagonal for the element types considered here.

Like the Galerkin load vector, the right-hand side is assembled from intersections, here of source subelements with the median-dual pieces of the target elements:
\begin{equation}
 \tilde b_j=\sum_{K_B\ni j}\ \sum_{K_A:\,|K_A\cap K_B|>0}
      \int_{K_A\cap K_B\cap C_j^B}\phi_A^h\dd x,
 \label{eq:cv-load-intersections}
\end{equation}
where $K_B$ ranges over the target elements incident to $j$ and $K_A$ over the source subelements. The source mesh must cover every part of $C_j^B$, as it does in the numerical tests, where both meshes cover the same polygonal domain.

Summing Eq.~\eqref{eq:cv-condition} over the target control volumes, which tile $\Omega$, and using Eq.~\eqref{eq:cv-lumped-exact} gives
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 Q_B(\phi_B)&=\sum_{j=1}^{n_B}\int_{C_j^B}\phi_B^h\dd x
 =\sum_{j=1}^{n_B}\tilde b_j\\
 &=\int_\Omega\phi_A^h\dd x=Q_A(\phi_A)
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:cv-global}
\end{equation}
to within integration and solve tolerances. Equation~\eqref{eq:cv-condition} concerns the integral of the reconstructed field over each target control volume, $(\widetilde H_B\phi_B)_j$. This is not the product $w_j^B\phi_{B,j}$ that the solver attributes to vertex $j$, because integrating a continuous field over a control volume generally differs from multiplying that volume by the value at its vertex. The two quantities have the same sum over the domain but differ vertex by vertex. Clipping vertex values and redistributing the resulting change can preserve the domain sum while changing the individual integrals in Eq.~\eqref{eq:cv-condition}.

If $B=A$, the system has $\tilde b=\widetilde H_A\phi_A$ and recovers the source vertex values. If both reconstructions reproduce affine fields, uniqueness also makes transfer exact for those fields. On shape-regular mesh families with comparable local spacings and a uniformly stable recovery, affine reproduction gives second-order accuracy on smooth fields. On quadrilaterals, a center-value error of order $h$ introduces a first-order contribution. The same geometry term is of second order when the weighted center and the arithmetic center lie within $O(h^2)$ of one another.

\subsection{Corrections applied to the transferred state}
\label{sec:global-correction}
\label{sec:galerkin-limiter}
The methods above determine target vertex values, but those values must also be suitable for restarting the flow solver. Near a discontinuity, a reconstruction can create new extrema. Bounded values can still change a conserved integral or fail to satisfy the boundary conditions, and for compressible flow the conserved variables must give positive density and pressure. We therefore treat limiting, integral correction, boundary treatment, and a positivity fix as parts of the complete transfer. These operations interact: clipping or replacing boundary values changes an integral, and restoring that integral modifies local values. Their combined effect must therefore be assessed on the final state supplied to the solver.

Higher-order NN and BAR reconstructions can use the Barth--Jespersen (BJ) limiter~\cite{BarthJespersen1989}. At each source vertex $i$ it supplies a factor $0\leq\theta_i\leq1$ that reduces the linear reconstruction increment wherever the predicted values would leave the range of the neighboring source values. The same factor multiplies the Taylor correction in NN2 and NN3 and the gradient correction in BAR3, so $\theta_i=1$ retains the full correction and $\theta_i=0$ gives NN1 or BAR2. The smooth-field tests use $\theta_i=1$. Because the factor is computed from the linear slope, it does not bound the quadratic term of NN3, and extrema are therefore measured on the final transferred field.

GP and CV are limited after the solve. At each target vertex, the limiter uses extrema from the contributing source elements. For GP, these elements overlap target simplices incident to the vertex. For CV, they overlap the target control volume. Hard limiting clips to these bounds. Relaxed limiting widens them first by $\tau$ times the source range, where $\tau$ is dimensionless. Redistribution then restores the integral to its value before clipping. For CV this total is $Q_B$. For GP it is the integral of the piecewise-linear interpolant of the retained vertex values on the target simplices. In GP3 this limiter acts after the quadratic field has been sampled at the vertices. The redistribution can change the Galerkin orthogonality conditions or the individual CV integrals. In the flow tests, GP2 and CV2 use $\tau=2\times10^{-3}$.

Bounding a field does not restore an integral that transfer has changed. Bounded global correction (GC) can enforce the integral in Eq.~\eqref{eq:fv-discrete-integral} for any base transfer. It lets us test whether restoring the domain integral improves a transfer that otherwise changes it. The tests below compare NN with and without this correction. Given an uncorrected target field $\phi_B^{\mathrm{pre}}$, GC adds a correction $\chi=(\chi_1,\ldots,\chi_{n_B})$ that satisfies
\begin{equation}
 \sum_jw_j^B\chi_j=Q_A(\phi_A)-Q_B(\phi_B^{\mathrm{pre}}),
 \qquad \phi_j^{\min}\leq\phi_{B,j}^{\mathrm{pre}}+\chi_j\leq \phi_j^{\max},
 \label{eq:global-correction-constraint}
\end{equation}
where $\phi_j^{\min}$ and $\phi_j^{\max}$ are the lower and upper bounds at target vertex $j$. The corrected field is $\phi_B^{\mathrm{GC}}=\phi_B^{\mathrm{pre}}+\chi$. After clipping, let $v_j$ be the clipped value and let $D=Q_A(\phi_A)-\sum_i w_i^B v_i$ be the remaining difference. When $D>0$, the available room at vertex $j$ is $\kappa_j=\phi_j^{\max}-v_j$. When $D<0$, it is $\kappa_j=v_j-\phi_j^{\min}$. With finite bounds and sufficient capacity, the increment is $D\kappa_j/\sum_i w_i^B\kappa_i$. It restores the total by distributing the correction in proportion to the available room~\cite{ShashkovWendroff2004,KucharikShashkovWendroff2003}. A vertex can participate whenever it has room in the required direction.

Integral preservation must also be reconciled with the boundary conditions. In the standard treatment, boundary values are interpolated from the source solution on the matching physical boundary; this replaces the unconstrained transfer at those vertices and can change the local GP or CV identities. The flow tests retain the original boundary vertices and surface discretization. The boundary treatment at transfer supplies wall values from the same physical locations on the source mesh. During the subsequent flow solve the solver evaluates its boundary fluxes from the numerical state and the prescribed boundary data.

Finally, the conserved variables must form a physical gas state. For the ideal gas used here, the pressure is $p=(\gamma-1)[\rho E-|\rho\boldsymbol u|^2/(2\rho)]$, with heat-capacity ratio $\gamma>1$. Positive density and positive internal energy are therefore coupled requirements: separate bounds on the conserved components do not guarantee positive pressure.

These requirements determine the final treatment before the solver restarts. The standard GP, CV, and globally corrected transfers apply boundary values and scalar lower bounds, and then redistribute the resulting integral change. A positivity fix is applied to any remaining state with nonpositive density or pressure, while preserving the totals of the affected patch. The final state can consequently differ from the solution of the original GP or CV equations. We therefore measure conservation and bounds on the state passed to the flow solver. Global redistribution can reach any vertex with room inside its relaxed bounds, including vertices in a uniform far field; Section~\ref{sec:results-local-conservation} measures the consequence.

\section{Effect of remeshing on multistep time integration}
\label{sec:restart-consistency}

BDF2 uses two stored solutions. If either changes during transfer, the next time-step equation changes. To quantify this change, we compare two calculations on the same target mesh $B$. The \emph{reference run} continues from states computed on $B$. The \emph{restarted run} continues from states transferred from the source mesh $A$. From the first step on $B$ onward, both runs use the same discrete equations. Each run starts from its own two stored fields. At each physical step, the implicit BDF2 equation is solved iteratively while the stored states are held fixed; the calculations in this paper use dual-time stepping, whose pseudo-time iterations are called inner iterations below. An iterative solve stops at a finite tolerance, and the analysis keeps the residual that it leaves.

\subsection{Time discretization and stored-state errors}
\label{sec:unsteady-discretization}
\label{sec:native-transfer-decomposition}

Let $V_M=\operatorname{diag}(w_i^M)\otimes I_{n_U}$ be the diagonal matrix of control volumes, where $I_{n_U}$ is the identity on the state components and $\otimes$ denotes the Kronecker product: each vertex weight is repeated for its $n_U$ components. This finite-volume matrix differs from the coupled Galerkin matrix $H_B$. Between remeshing events, the semi-discrete equations are
\begin{equation}
 V_M\dot U_M+F_M(U_M,t)=0.
 \label{eq:semidiscrete}
\end{equation}
Here $t$ is physical time, $\dot U_M=\mathrm dU_M/\mathrm dt$, and $F_M$ is the assembled spatial residual, including fluxes, source terms, and boundary contributions. The state includes the RANS turbulence variables when they are advanced in time.

Fix the event at $t^n$, after the solver has computed the latest state on $A$ and before it advances to $t^{n+1}$. The index $n$ stays fixed in this analysis. We use $k$ for a physical time index that may vary. At constant step size $\Delta t=t^n-t^{n-1}>0$, the reference run on $B$ computes $U_B^{n+1}$ from
\begin{equation}
 \frac{V_B}{\Delta t}\left(\tfrac32U_B^{n+1}-2U_B^n+\tfrac12U_B^{n-1}\right)
   +F_B(U_B^{n+1},t^{n+1})=0.
 \label{eq:bdf2}
\end{equation}
We denote the left-hand side by $\Phi_B(U_B^{n+1};U_B^n,U_B^{n-1})$ and call it the BDF2 residual. Its first argument is the new state. The other two are the stored states, which are fixed during the step. Each mesh is stationary during a time step. Continuously moving meshes would require compatible geometric histories and a discrete geometric conservation law~\cite{KoobusFarhat1999,GeuzaineGrandmontFarhat2003}; Eq.~\eqref{eq:bdf2} does not cover that case.

Let $U_A^n$ be the latest field and $U_A^{n-1}$ the older field stored on $A$. At the event at $t^n$, we perform two separate transfers onto the common target mesh $B$ as follows.
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 \widehat U_B^n &= \mathcal T_{AB}(U_A^n),\\
 \widehat U_B^{n-1} &= \mathcal T_{AB}(U_A^{n-1}).
 \end{aligned}
\]
Each transfer uses the source values at its own time level. The map $\mathcal T_{AB}$ includes the raw transfer and any limiting, boundary treatment, and positivity corrections. The two results retain the times $t^n$ and $t^{n-1}$ and enter the corresponding BDF2 history slots for the step to $t^{n+1}$.

A hat identifies the restarted run. Let $U_B^k$ be the field at $t^k$ from the reference run on $B$. At either stored time or after advancing on $B$, the difference between the two runs is
\begin{equation}
\eps^k=\widehat U_B^k-U_B^k.
 \label{eq:history-defects}
\end{equation}
At $k=n-1,n$, this difference includes the effects of the transfer and of computing the histories on different meshes. The static tests use an analytic reference to measure transfer error alone.

\subsection{Response of the next time step to stored-state errors}
\label{sec:state-response}

Both runs solve Eq.~\eqref{eq:bdf2} iteratively, each with its own stored states. When the iterations stop, the computed new states leave the residuals
\[
 \mathfrak r_B^{n+1}=\Phi_B(U_B^{n+1};U_B^n,U_B^{n-1}),\qquad
 \widehat{\mathfrak r}_B^{n+1}=\Phi_B(\widehat U_B^{n+1};\widehat U_B^n,\widehat U_B^{n-1})
\]
in the reference run and in the restarted run, respectively. Both residuals are zero when the equations are solved exactly. Subtracting the first definition from the second gives, without approximation,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 &\frac{3V_B}{2\Delta t}\eps^{n+1}
   +F_B(\widehat U_B^{n+1},t^{n+1})-F_B(U_B^{n+1},t^{n+1})
 =\frac{V_B}{\Delta t}\eta^n
   +\widehat{\mathfrak r}_B^{n+1}-\mathfrak r_B^{n+1},\\
 &\eta^n=2\eps^n-\tfrac12\eps^{n-1}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:nonlinear-bdf2-response}
\end{equation}
The stored states appear only in the time-derivative term of Eq.~\eqref{eq:bdf2}, with coefficients $-2$ and $1/2$. They do not appear in $F_B$, which depends on the new state alone. The explicit history term in Eq.~\eqref{eq:nonlinear-bdf2-response} therefore depends on the weighted discrepancy $\eta^n$, and the difference of the two spatial residuals stays on the left-hand side. When both equations are solved exactly, $\mathfrak r_B^{n+1}=\widehat{\mathfrak r}_B^{n+1}=0$, and the history term $V_B\eta^n/\Delta t$ is balanced by the change in the new state and in its spatial residual.

\paragraph{Linear response.}
Let $J_B=\partial_U F_B(U_B^{n+1},t^{n+1})$ be the spatial Jacobian at the reference new state, where $\partial_U$ denotes differentiation with respect to the state vector. Define the remainder $\mathcal N_B$ as the part of the spatial-residual difference that is not linear in $\eps^{n+1}$:
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal N_B(\eps^{n+1})
 =F_B(\widehat U_B^{n+1},t^{n+1})-F_B(U_B^{n+1},t^{n+1})-J_B\eps^{n+1}.
\label{eq:spatial-remainder}
\end{equation}
This is a definition and involves no approximation. Appendix~\ref{app:remainders} derives an integral expression for $\mathcal N_B$ and a bound on its size. Substituting Eq.~\eqref{eq:spatial-remainder} into Eq.~\eqref{eq:nonlinear-bdf2-response} and multiplying by $\Delta t$ gives
\begin{equation}
 A_B\eps^{n+1}
 =V_B\eta^n+\Delta t\bigl(\widehat{\mathfrak r}_B^{n+1}-\mathfrak r_B^{n+1}\bigr)
                 -\Delta t\,\mathcal N_B(\eps^{n+1}),
 \qquad A_B=\tfrac32V_B+\Delta t J_B.
 \label{eq:linearized-bdf2-response}
\end{equation}
The matrix $A_B$ is the Jacobian of the BDF2 residual with respect to the new state, multiplied by $\Delta t$. For exact solves, the implicit function theorem~\cite{OrtegaRheinboldt2000} gives a locally unique restarted solution near the reference for small $\eta^n$, provided that $F_B$ is continuously differentiable there and $A_B$ is invertible. If the Jacobian of $F_B$ is also Lipschitz continuous near the reference, the remainder is quadratic (Appendix~\ref{app:remainders}), and for exact solves
\begin{equation}
 \eps^{n+1}=R_B\eta^n+O(\Delta t\|\eta^n\|_2^2),
 \qquad R_B=A_B^{-1}V_B.
 \label{eq:state-response}
\end{equation}
The response matrix $R_B$ maps the weighted history error linearly to the leading term of the next-state difference. Section~\ref{sec:naca-fixed-methodology} uses this property to test the relative effects of the two stored states. When $\|\Delta t V_B^{-1}J_B\|_2$ tends to zero, $R_B$ tends to $\tfrac23I$ and the leading term becomes $\tfrac43\eps^n-\tfrac13\eps^{n-1}$. Otherwise the Jacobian term changes both the size and the spatial distribution of the response.

The Lipschitz condition is an assumption on the spatial discretization, not a consequence of BDF2. Slope limiters and upwind switches are not differentiable where they change branch, so the quadratic estimate can fail there. When the iterative solves stop early, the term $\Delta t A_B^{-1}(\widehat{\mathfrak r}_B^{n+1}-\mathfrak r_B^{n+1})$ must also be retained; Eq.~\eqref{eq:linearized-bdf2-response} contains it.

\subsection{Response of an aerodynamic load}
\label{sec:delayed-response}

This subsection relates the state difference $\eps^{n+1}$ to the difference in an aerodynamic load, such as the lift or drag coefficient. On a fixed airfoil in inviscid flow, the load coefficients are weighted sums of wall pressure, with weights fixed by geometry, force direction, and reference quantities. In viscous flow, wall shear also contributes, through velocity gradients reconstructed near the wall. An error confined outside the vertices used in the load evaluation has no immediate contribution. Its later effect depends on how it propagates into the wall pressure or near-wall velocity gradients.

Let $G_B$ be one scalar load evaluated on mesh $B$, and let $\eps_G^{n+1}=G_B(\widehat U_B^{n+1})-G_B(U_B^{n+1})$ be its difference between the two runs. Let $\nabla_U G_B$ be the gradient of the load with respect to the state, evaluated at $U_B^{n+1}$. As in Eq.~\eqref{eq:spatial-remainder}, define the load remainder $\mathcal N_G^{n+1}$ as the part of the load difference that is not linear in $\eps^{n+1}$:
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal N_G^{n+1}=\eps_G^{n+1}-(\nabla_U G_B)^T\eps^{n+1}.
 \label{eq:output-remainder}
\end{equation}
The remainder is quadratic in $\eps^{n+1}$ when the load gradient is Lipschitz continuous (Appendix~\ref{app:remainders}). Under the exact-solve and smoothness assumptions of Eq.~\eqref{eq:state-response}, substitution gives
\begin{equation}
 \eps_G^{n+1}=(\nabla_U G_B)^TR_B\eta^n+O(\|\eta^n\|_2^2).
 \label{eq:force-response}
\end{equation}
The first-step load difference is thus, to leading order, a weighted sum of the entries of $\eta^n$, with weights given by the row vector $(\nabla_U G_B)^TR_B$. The controlled airfoil test compares ratios of lift differences for proportional history discrepancies, so the common load sensitivity cancels at leading order.

Equation~\eqref{eq:force-response} also shows why preserving a global integral does not by itself preserve a load. Consider mass at a stored time level $k=n-1$ or $n$. Let $\rho_A^k$ be the source density in the selected history slot, $\rho_B^k$ the target-mesh reference density at that time, and $\widehat\rho_B^k$ the density obtained by transferring $\rho_A^k$. A mass-preserving transfer satisfies $Q_B(\widehat\rho_B^k)=Q_A(\rho_A^k)$. Subtracting the target-reference mass gives
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{j=1}^{n_B} w_j^B(\widehat\rho_{B,j}^k-\rho_{B,j}^k)
 =Q_A(\rho_A^k)-Q_B(\rho_B^k).
 \label{eq:gc-native-mismatch}
\end{equation}
Preserving the source mass therefore need not give zero mass difference from the target reference. Even when the two reference masses agree, mass can be added at one vertex and removed at another. These changes cancel in the mass sum. Their contributions to a load carry different weights in Eq.~\eqref{eq:force-response}, however, and need not cancel. The tests therefore measure integral changes and load changes separately.

Finally, the first-step load difference need not capture the later response. At the following step the stored-state errors are $\eps^{n+1}$ and $\eps^n$, so the weighted history error is $\eta^{n+1}=2\eps^{n+1}-\eps^n/2$. The newly computed error thus enters the subsequent steps, during which the flow can carry a disturbance toward the wall. Section~\ref{sec:delayed-airfoil-test} tracks the pressure response near the airfoil after one localized remeshing event.

\subsection{Response of total mass}
\label{sec:mass-response}

We now follow the total-mass difference after the single remeshing event at $t^n$. The first step on $B$ starts at $k=n$ and computes the field at $k+1=n+1$. No further transfer is made, and both runs evolve on the same fixed mesh $B$. Define
\[
 \delta^k=Q_B(\widehat\rho_B^k)-Q_B(\rho_B^k).
\]
Comparing each mapped field with the target-mesh reference at its own time gives the two starting mass differences $\delta^{n-1}$ and $\delta^n$. For conservative transfer, Eq.~\eqref{eq:gc-native-mismatch} relates these differences to the source and reference masses.

Summing the density equations over all control volumes cancels interior face fluxes in pairs. Let $f_\rho^{k+1}$ denote the net outward numerical mass flux and $r_\Sigma^{k+1}$ the signed sum of the density entries of the BDF2 residual defined in Section~\ref{sec:unsteady-discretization}. Subtracting the summed reference equation from that of the restarted run gives
\[
 \frac{\tfrac32\delta^{k+1}-2\delta^k+\tfrac12\delta^{k-1}}{\Delta t}
 =-(\widehat f_\rho^{k+1}-f_\rho^{k+1})
   +(\widehat r_\Sigma^{k+1}-r_\Sigma^{k+1}),\qquad k\ge n .
\]
For exact inner solves, both residual sums vanish. Equal net numerical boundary fluxes also cancel in this difference, as they do on a domain with impermeable walls.
Solving for the new mass difference yields
\begin{equation}
 \delta^{k+1}=\tfrac43\delta^k-\tfrac13\delta^{k-1},\qquad k\ge n.
 \label{eq:mass-first-step}
\end{equation}
Subtracting $\delta^k$ from both sides isolates the increment between consecutive steps:
\[
 \delta^{k+1}-\delta^k=\tfrac13(\delta^k-\delta^{k-1}).
\]
The first increment, from $n$ to $n+1$, is therefore $(\delta^n-\delta^{n-1})/3$. Each later increment is one third of the preceding one, so repeated application gives
\[
 \delta^{k+1}-\delta^k=3^{-(k-n+1)}(\delta^n-\delta^{n-1}),\qquad k\ge n.
\]
To obtain the mass difference after $k-n$ physical steps, add these increments to $\delta^n$. For $k\ge n+1$, the finite geometric sum gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
 \delta^k
 &=\delta^n+\sum_{j=n}^{k-1}(\delta^{j+1}-\delta^j)\\
 &=\delta^n+(\delta^n-\delta^{n-1})\sum_{j=n}^{k-1}3^{-(j-n+1)}\\
 &=\delta^n+\tfrac12\left(1-3^{-(k-n)}\right)(\delta^n-\delta^{n-1}).
\end{aligned}
\]
As $k\to\infty$ with $n$ fixed, the decaying term $3^{-(k-n)}$ vanishes. Hence
\begin{equation}
 \delta^\infty:=\lim_{k\to\infty}\delta^k
 =\delta^n+\tfrac12(\delta^n-\delta^{n-1})
 =\tfrac32\delta^n-\tfrac12\delta^{n-1}.
 \label{eq:mass-long-run}
\end{equation}
If both stored masses differ from the reference by $\delta$, the mass difference stays equal to $\delta$ at every subsequent step. If only the older mass differs by $\delta$, so that $\delta^{n-1}=\delta$ and $\delta^n=0$, the difference tends to $-\delta/2$. Adding the two stored errors would predict the wrong result in either example.

Equation~\eqref{eq:mass-long-run} describes the unforced evolution after one event. For repeated events, each separately measured transfer change enters the corresponding mass history at the time of remeshing. The shock-tube budget in Section~\ref{sec:results-local-conservation} uses the stepwise balance with those changes and with the measured flux and residual terms.

\section{Numerical tests and results}
\label{sec:methodology}
\label{sec:results}

Static fields measure the error introduced by a single field transfer. Controlled restarts isolate the next-step response to perturbing each BDF2 history slot. After repeated remeshing in the shock tube, we compare contact smearing, shock width and position, and signed mass changes. The CV2 budget identifies the source of the mass increase in that run. Airfoil calculations extend the observation from the next-step load to the later pressure response and persistent viscous load departures. Each flow test uses a matching fixed-mesh reference, and the viscous case includes time-step and mesh-resolution controls.

Method selection follows the purpose of each comparison. The smooth-field verification retains all eight variants to assess their approximation orders. Paired corrections isolate bounds and integral preservation. The shock tube uses NN1 as a baseline and compares four nominally second-order methods, with detailed flux instrumentation for CV2. The airfoil tests retain the subsets needed to examine history weighting, disturbance propagation and load sensitivity.

\subsection{Single-transfer tests}
\label{sec:static-verification}

\subsubsection{Observed accuracy for smooth fields}

The smooth-field test measures the observed approximation orders and their dependence on element geometry. Prescribing an exact density gives a reference at every vertex on both meshes, without contamination by a flow solve. We refine structured quadrilateral and unstructured mixed meshes separately to test how element geometry affects the observed order. Figure~\ref{fig:reference-densities} also introduces the discontinuous field used in the following boundedness test. For the smooth case the exact density is

\begin{equation}
 \rho(x,y)=\rho_\infty\left[2+0.3e^{0.8x/L_x+0.5y/L_y}
              \sin(2\pi x/L_x)\sin(2\pi y/L_y)\right].
 \label{eq:asymmetric-manufactured-field}
\end{equation}
Here $\rho_\infty=1.2$~kg/m$^3$ and $L_x,L_y$ are the side lengths of the square domain. The experiment uses three source-to-target resolution pairs, $32\to64$, $64\to128$, and $128\to256$. On the structured meshes these numbers count vertices along each side, so the first pair transfers values from $32^2$ to $64^2$ vertices. On the independently generated meshes of triangles and quadrilaterals, the numbers specify the nominal refinement levels. The actual vertex counts determine the effective spacing used below.

 e_{\rho,2}^{\mathrm{FV}}=

 \label{eq:static-error-norms}
\end{equation}

To compare rates of convergence, we define the effective spacing $h_{\mathrm{eff}}=n_B^{-1/2}$ using the target vertex count $n_B$. If $e_{\mathrm c}$ and $e_{\mathrm f}$ are the errors in the same norm on two successive levels, with effective spacings $h_{\mathrm{eff},\mathrm c}$ and $h_{\mathrm{eff},\mathrm f}$, their observed order is
\begin{equation}
 p^{\mathrm{obs}}=
 \frac{\log(e_{\mathrm c}/e_{\mathrm f})}{\log(h_{\mathrm{eff},\mathrm c}/h_{\mathrm{eff},\mathrm f})}.
 \label{eq:pairwise-observed-order}
\end{equation}

\label{sec:results-asymmetric}

In the volume-weighted norm, NN1 converges at first order, NN2, BAR2 and GP2 at about second order, and NN3 at third order. BAR3 and GP3 have fitted orders above three, with orders near 3.2 between the two finest refinement levels.

The errors in Eq.~\eqref{eq:static-error-norms} are evaluated at solver vertices. A high order in these samples does not imply the same order for a field reconstructed between vertices.

CV2 is sensitive to element shape. Its volume-weighted order is 2.04 on the structured quadrilaterals and 1.49 on the mixed mesh, falling to 1.34 on the finest mixed pair. This behavior is consistent with the loss of affine accuracy on quadrilaterals that remain distorted under refinement (Section~\ref{sec:cv-transfer}). CV2 still preserves total mass. The relative mass-change magnitude $|e_Q|$ stays below $4\times10^{-15}$ on both mesh families. On the finest meshes, the corresponding GP2 values are $4.3\times10^{-12}$ (structured) and $2.5\times10^{-7}$ (mixed).

The geometry comparison therefore distinguishes preservation of an integral from reproduction of the underlying field.

The second test separates the preservation of bounds from the preservation of mass. We transfer a discontinuous field once, without advancing the flow, so the comparison isolates the transfer and its corrections. The disk in Figure~\ref{fig:reference-densities} is centered in the domain and has radius $\min(L_x,L_y)/4$. Its boundary cuts across mesh cells. \label{sec:results-boundedness}

\subsection{Lift response to perturbing the BDF2 history states}
\label{sec:naca-fixed-methodology}

We use steady flow over a NACA~0012 airfoil to test the lift response to the same prescribed discrepancy in either BDF2 history slot. The steady solution gives identical source values at both stored times, so changing the slot changes the BDF2 weight while keeping the discrepancy fixed.

We compute steady Euler solutions independently on two triangular meshes. Both use the same 370 vertices on the airfoil and 63 on the outer boundary, $100$ chords away. Their interiors were generated independently. The source mesh has 16,255 vertices in all. The Delaunay target mesh has 22,508 and a smaller minimum first off-wall spacing ($4.3\times10^{-4}c$ versus $5.9\times10^{-4}c$). \subsection{Shock-tube wave profiles and mass balance}

\subsubsection{Setup and error measures}
\label{sec:riemann-methodology}

The shock tube distinguishes preservation of total mass from preservation of the evolving wave profiles. Its contact and shock provide local features whose position and width can be checked against an exact solution. We first compare NN1 with and without global mass correction to isolate what restoring an integral can recover. We then compare limited NN2, BAR2, GP2 and CV2 at a common nominal order, separating shock sharpness from positional accuracy. Mass histories compare the five methods, while an instrumented CV2 budget distinguishes changes introduced at transfer from boundary exchange during flow evolution. The fixed-mesh calculation identifies the broadening already produced by the same flow solver. We use Roe fluxes with MUSCL reconstruction and the Venkatakrishnan limiter to solve the two-dimensional Euler equations on $[0,5]\times[0,1]$. The initial uniform mesh has $501\times101$ vertices at spacing $h=0.01$. With nondimensional gas parameters $\gamma=1.4$ and $R_{\mathrm g}=1$, the initial state is

\begin{equation}
 (\rho,u,v,p)=
 \begin{cases}
 (1,0,0,1), & x<2,\\
 (0.125,0,0,0.1), & x\geq2,
 \end{cases}
 \label{eq:riemann-initial-state}
\end{equation}
which gives left and right temperatures of 1 and 0.8. The upper and lower boundaries are inviscid slip walls. Characteristic conditions at $x=0$ and $x=5$ prescribe total pressure and temperature $(p_0,T_0)=(1,1)$ and $(0.1,0.8)$, respectively, with direction $(1,0)$ at both ends.

During the 400 physical steps of size $\Delta t=10^{-3}$, remeshing displaces interior vertices in the streamwise direction. At each event, we draw new displacements relative to the original mesh and keep the boundary vertices fixed. The displacement of vertex $i$ is at most $a_{i,\min}/s$, where $a_{i,\min}$ is the smallest altitude of its incident corner triangles. The following comparisons use $s=2$.

The main comparison replaces the mesh after computing the states at time indices $n=3,5,\ldots,399$. At each event we transfer the two BDF2 fields separately from their source values at $t^n$ and $t^{n-1}$. The 199 mesh replacements therefore give 398 individual field transfers by the measurement time. NN1 is used as a diffusive baseline for the correction test. BAR2 supplies a second-order barycentric comparison for GP2 and CV2, whose integral constraints differ. For the shock-width comparison at $s=2$, we run NN1, BAR2, GP2 and CV2 with three random seeds. Each seed generates a different sequence of displaced meshes. Within each sequence, all methods use the same meshes. We call these realizations sequences 1--3. The fixed-mesh run uses the same initial state and boundary conditions. Differences from it include evolution on displaced meshes as well as the transfer itself. Limited NN2 joins the profile and mass-history comparisons for sequence 1.

At $t=0.399$ we sample density on eight equally spaced lines from $y=0.2$ to $y=0.8$. Averaging the eight samples at each $x$ gives the streamwise density profile. We locate the shock at the halfway point of the density jump, using the plateau densities estimated at the ends of $2.40\le x\le3.00$. For the width we use $2.55\le x\le2.85$, which contains the shock and excludes the tail of the broadened contact. Within that interval we estimate the two plateau densities and measure the distance between the 10\% and 90\% crossings of the jump. We report changes in position and width relative to the fixed-mesh solution in units of the original cell spacing $h$.

Let $I_\rho(t)=Q_{M}(\rho_{M}(t))$ denote the latest field mass on mesh $M$ at time $t$. At a remeshing time we evaluate it on the source mesh before transfer and on the target mesh after transfer. The exact solution retains its initial mass $I_{\rho,0}=2.375$ through the measurement time because the fluid remains at rest at both open ends. Section~\ref{sec:results-local-conservation} compares this behavior with the measured mass drift after conservative remeshing.

\subsubsection{Mass changes during transfer and flow evolution}
\label{sec:results-local-conservation}

Figure~\ref{fig:riemann-mass-history} compares the signed change of the latest field mass $I_\rho(t)$ relative to the initial mass $I_{\rho,0}$ for NN1, NN2 with BJ limiting, BAR2, GP2 and CV2 at $s=2$, sequence 1. The final values are $+7.27\times10^{-4}$, $+4.34\times10^{-4}$, $+1.37\times10^{-4}$, $-3.88\times10^{-6}$ and $+5.49\times10^{-6}$, respectively. The enlarged scale resolves GP2 and CV2 against the fixed-mesh control. At an event at $t^n$, the two values plotted at that time are the mass of the latest field before and after mapping it to the new mesh. Mapping the older field changes the history used to compute the mass at $t^{n+1}$.

Standard NN1, NN2 and BAR2 impose no integral constraint at transfer. We measure the mass change across each transfer separately for the fields at $t^n$ and $t^{n-1}$. We add each change to the corresponding history slot and propagate its contribution through Eq.~\eqref{eq:mass-first-step} between events. The resulting transfer contribution accounts for the final mass drift of these three methods to within $2.4\times10^{-14}$ of the initial mass. GP2 also changes the solver's mass integral across transfer on this quadrilateral mesh, although it preserves the finite-element integral described in Section~\ref{sec:galerkin-projection}. Its accumulated transfer contribution is $-7.15\times10^{-6}$, partly offset by a net flow-evolution contribution of $+3.27\times10^{-6}$. The evolution term for GP2 is the measured drift minus the transfer contribution.

For CV2, the accumulated contribution from transferring both histories is $8.1\times10^{-15}$ relative to the initial mass. The measured relative mass increase is $5.49\times10^{-6}$ at $t=0.399$. The fixed-mesh reference ends with a relative change of $-5.8\times10^{-15}$. We use the numerical flux and signed residual terms in the summed BDF2 balance to account for the CV2 increase.

The end conditions prescribe total pressure and temperature, not zero normal velocity or zero mass flux. At each residual evaluation, the solver combines those prescribed data with characteristics from the current interior state to construct the boundary state and its numerical flux. Reapplying the boundary condition therefore does not reset the fluid at the ends to rest. In the exact solution, the ends remain at rest until the physical waves arrive. A transferred numerical state can instead disturb those regions immediately, so a nonzero boundary flux can persist during the subsequent flow solve even before wave arrival.

We evaluate the flux and residual at the saved solution of each physical step, before any transfer at that time. A positive boundary flux sum denotes outward mass flow. At the final physical step the sum is $-6.44\times10^{-5}$, indicating net influx, while the signed density residual sum is $9.28\times10^{-15}$. In the mass recurrence, the outward flux sum enters with coefficient $-2\Delta t/3$ and the signed residual sum with coefficient $+2\Delta t/3$. Propagating the measured flux through the recurrence gives a relative mass contribution of $5.49\times10^{-6}$, matching the observed drift (Figure~\ref{fig:riemann-budget}). The accumulated residual contribution is $-2.51\times10^{-16}$. Including the measured transfer changes and the numerical interior-flux cancellation error, the budget closes to $1.42\times10^{-13}$ of the initial mass. The measured numerical influx during the flow solve accounts for the mass gain.

Global redistribution after limiting can alter the far-field state without changing total mass. When the GP or CV limiter clips a value, the conservative correction is distributed among all vertices with available capacity. The relaxed bounds ($\tau=2\times10^{-3}$) give even initially uniform regions nonzero capacity. Such corrections can disturb the open ends before the physical waves arrive. The measured exchange follows the complete transfer and flow solve.

CV2's final shock position and width differ from the fixed-mesh values by $+0.19h$ and $+0.009h$, respectively. Guglielmi et al.~\cite{Guglielmi2026} relate shock speed to perturbations imposed near the shock. Here the domain mass increase comes from influx at the open ends and coexists with a small change in the shock profile.

\subsection{Load response in nominally steady viscous flow}
\label{sec:rans-methodology}
\label{sec:results-rans-new}

The viscous test measures the lift and drag response to repeated remeshing of a nominally steady flow. The geometry and freestream remain fixed, so departures from a matching fixed-mesh calculation measure the disturbance produced by remeshing and subsequent flow evolution. We compare NN2, GP2 and CV2, then use time-step and mesh refinement to assess whether the load response persists. The stationary NACA~0012 has Mach number $0.1$, incidence $6^\circ$ and chord Reynolds number $Re_c=\rho_\infty U_\infty c/\mu_\infty=6\times10^6$. Turbulence is modeled with Spalart--Allmaras (SA)~\cite{SpalartAllmaras1994}.

The coarse mesh is a mixed unstructured grid with structured boundary-layer rows and an unstructured connection to the farfield. It has 23,271 vertices and 36,537 elements. The first off-wall spacing is approximately $5\times10^{-6}c$ and the farfield boundary is at $100c$. Every ten physical steps we perturb interior vertices at wall distances from $0.01c$ to $20c$. The random displacement of vertex $i$ has magnitude at most $a_{i,\min}/4$, where $a_{i,\min}$ is its smallest incident corner-triangle altitude. We reduce the displacements where necessary so that each corner triangle retains at least 20\% of its original area. Both boundaries stay fixed.

The mean-flow equations use HLLC fluxes with MUSCL reconstruction and a van Albada slope limiter. The SA equation uses scalar upwinding without MUSCL. The airfoil wall is no-slip and adiabatic. A steady calculation supplies the state at $t=0$. The RMS density residual reaches $10^{-11.2}$, and both force coefficients are stable to $10^{-7}$ over the last 2,000 iterations. Ten first-order substeps of length $\Delta t_0/10$ supply the state at $t=\Delta t_0$. BDF2 then advances from these two fields. At each event we map the latest and older fields separately. Each field contains the mean-flow state and the SA working variable $\widetilde\nu$ at its own stored time. Wall distance and eddy viscosity are recomputed on the new mesh. The fixed-mesh reference starts from the same two fields.

The nominal step is $\Delta t_0=10^{-3}$~s, with $c=1$~m and $U_\infty=34.72$~m/s. The record spans about 13.9 chord convection times $c/U_\infty$, compared with 0.047 in the inviscid airfoil tests.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

Remeshing in a BDF2 calculation changes the two fields used to compute the next state. The analysis predicts different signs and weights for discrepancies in the older and latest fields. In controlled airfoil restarts, inserting the same discrepancy into different history slots changes the first-step lift in the predicted ratios. Both stored fields therefore matter to the accuracy of the restart.

The static tests show that distorted quadrilaterals reduce CV2's observed order and that mass conservation and bounds impose separate constraints. The shock tube extends this distinction to wave accuracy. Global correction recovers the total mass while leaving the first-order profile smeared. Limited second-order nearest-neighbor transfer produces a narrow shock with a downstream shift relative to the fixed-mesh solution. In the instrumented CV2 case, the discrete balance attributes the mass rise to numerical influx through the open ends. Its shock position changes by less than one fifth of the original cell spacing. A global balance and local wave measurements are therefore both needed to assess the remeshed solution.

The airfoil tests connect the remeshing disturbance to the later load response. A localized mesh change produces a delayed near-wall pressure response consistent with acoustic travel. In nominally steady viscous flow, the three methods give load departures of comparable magnitude, including CV2 with mean-flow integrals preserved to roundoff. The disturbances persist at smaller time steps and are smaller on the refined mesh under the tested displacement rule.

Two developments follow from these results. First, local conservative redistribution after limiting could protect uniform regions and retain boundary-compatible states. The shock-tube tests provide a way to assess its effect on wave profiles and numerical boundary exchange. Second, the transfer assessment could be extended to three-dimensional unsteady flows, including fluid--structure interaction. In these coupled problems, structural motion changes the flow and the resulting loads drive the motion. The analysis of the BDF2 histories provides a starting point for studying how transfer errors affect both the fluid loads and the structural response over repeated remeshing events.

\section{Remainder bounds for the linear response}
\label{app:remainders}

This appendix derives integral expressions for the remainders $\mathcal N_B$ and $\mathcal N_G^{n+1}$ defined in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:spatial-remainder} and \eqref{eq:output-remainder}, and gives the conditions under which they are quadratic. Norms are the Euclidean vector norm $\|\cdot\|_2$ and its induced matrix norm, applied to nondimensional states.

\paragraph{Spatial remainder.}
Join the reference and restarted new states by the straight path
\[
 U(\sigma)=U_B^{n+1}+\sigma\eps^{n+1},\qquad 0\leq\sigma\leq1,
\]
so that $U(0)=U_B^{n+1}$ and $U(1)=\widehat U_B^{n+1}$. Consider the spatial residual along this path, $f(\sigma)=F_B(U(\sigma),t^{n+1})$. Since $\mathrm dU/\mathrm d\sigma=\eps^{n+1}$, the chain rule gives
\[
 \frac{\mathrm df}{\mathrm d\sigma}=\partial_U F_B(U(\sigma),t^{n+1})\,\eps^{n+1}.
\]
Integrating from $\sigma=0$ to $\sigma=1$, and using $f(1)-f(0)=F_B(\widehat U_B^{n+1},t^{n+1})-F_B(U_B^{n+1},t^{n+1})$, gives
\[
 F_B(\widehat U_B^{n+1},t^{n+1})-F_B(U_B^{n+1},t^{n+1})
 =\int_0^1\partial_U F_B(U(\sigma),t^{n+1})\,\eps^{n+1}\dd\sigma.
\]
The linear part can be written in the same form, because $J_B$ and $\eps^{n+1}$ do not depend on $\sigma$:
\[
 J_B\eps^{n+1}=\int_0^1J_B\eps^{n+1}\dd\sigma.
\]
Subtracting the second expression from the first and using the definition in Eq.~\eqref{eq:spatial-remainder} gives
\[
 \mathcal N_B(\eps^{n+1})
 =\int_0^1\big[\partial_U F_B(U(\sigma),t^{n+1})-J_B\big]\eps^{n+1}\dd\sigma.
\]
The integrand vanishes at $\sigma=0$, where the two Jacobians coincide. Assume that the Jacobian is Lipschitz continuous along the path, with a finite constant $L_F$. Since $\|U(\sigma)-U_B^{n+1}\|_2=\sigma\|\eps^{n+1}\|_2$, this means
\begin{equation}
 \|\partial_U F_B(U(\sigma),t^{n+1})-J_B\|_2
 \leq L_F\sigma\|\eps^{n+1}\|_2,
 \quad 0\leq\sigma\leq1.
 \label{eq:jacobian-variation-bound}
\end{equation}
The norm of an integral is at most the integral of the norm, so
\begin{equation}
 \|\mathcal N_B(\eps^{n+1})\|_2
 \leq\int_0^1\|\partial_U F_B(U(\sigma),t^{n+1})-J_B\|_2\,\|\eps^{n+1}\|_2\dd\sigma
 \leq L_F\|\eps^{n+1}\|_2^2\int_0^1\sigma\dd\sigma
 =\tfrac12 L_F\|\eps^{n+1}\|_2^2.
 \label{eq:spatial-remainder-bound}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{State response.}
For exact solves and invertible $A_B$, multiplying Eq.~\eqref{eq:linearized-bdf2-response} by $A_B^{-1}$ gives
\[
 \eps^{n+1}=R_B\eta^n-\Delta t A_B^{-1}\mathcal N_B(\eps^{n+1}).
\]
Taking norms and using Eq.~\eqref{eq:spatial-remainder-bound},
\[
 \|\eps^{n+1}\|_2
 \leq\|R_B\|_2\|\eta^n\|_2
 +\tfrac12\Delta t\|A_B^{-1}\|_2L_F\|\eps^{n+1}\|_2^2.
\]
Suppose that the state difference is small enough that $\tfrac12\Delta t\|A_B^{-1}\|_2L_F\|\eps^{n+1}\|_2\leq\tfrac12$. The last term is then at most $\|\eps^{n+1}\|_2/2$, and moving it to the left-hand side gives
\[
 \|\eps^{n+1}\|_2\leq2\|R_B\|_2\|\eta^n\|_2.
\]
Inserting this bound into the remainder term gives
\[
 \|\Delta t A_B^{-1}\mathcal N_B(\eps^{n+1})\|_2
 \leq\tfrac12\Delta t\|A_B^{-1}\|_2L_F\|\eps^{n+1}\|_2^2
 \leq2\Delta t\|A_B^{-1}\|_2L_F\|R_B\|_2^2\|\eta^n\|_2^2,
\]
which is the $O(\Delta t\|\eta^n\|_2^2)$ term in Eq.~\eqref{eq:state-response}. The constant contains $\|A_B^{-1}\|_2$ and $\|R_B\|_2$ and therefore depends on the mesh and the time step.

\paragraph{Load remainder.}
The same steps apply to a load. Along the path, let $g(\sigma)=G_B(U(\sigma))$. The chain rule gives $\mathrm dg/\mathrm d\sigma=\nabla_U G_B(U(\sigma))^T\eps^{n+1}$, and integrating from zero to one gives
\[
 \eps_G^{n+1}=G_B(\widehat U_B^{n+1})-G_B(U_B^{n+1})
 =\int_0^1\nabla_U G_B(U(\sigma))^T\eps^{n+1}\dd\sigma.
\]
Subtracting $(\nabla_U G_B)^T\eps^{n+1}=\int_0^1(\nabla_U G_B)^T\eps^{n+1}\dd\sigma$ and using the definition in Eq.~\eqref{eq:output-remainder} gives
\[
 \mathcal N_G^{n+1}=\int_0^1
 [\nabla_U G_B(U(\sigma))-\nabla_U G_B]^T\eps^{n+1}\dd\sigma.
\]
If the load gradient is Lipschitz continuous along the path, with a finite constant $L_G$,
\begin{equation}
 \|\nabla_U G_B(U(\sigma))-\nabla_U G_B\|_2
 \leq L_G\sigma\|\eps^{n+1}\|_2,
 \quad 0\leq\sigma\leq1,
 \label{eq:output-gradient-variation-bound}
\end{equation}
then, as for the spatial remainder, $|\mathcal N_G^{n+1}|\leq\tfrac12L_G\|\eps^{n+1}\|_2^2\leq2L_G\|R_B\|_2^2\|\eta^n\|_2^2$. Together with the state-response bound, this gives the $O(\|\eta^n\|_2^2)$ term in Eq.~\eqref{eq:force-response}. Ideal-gas wall pressure, for example, is a differentiable function of the conserved variables for positive density, and a finite bound requires the density to stay away from zero over the states considered. For a viscous load, the Lipschitz condition also covers the dependence of the wall-gradient reconstruction and constitutive model on the flow state.
