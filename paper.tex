\begin{abstract}
Mesh replacement perturbs the spatial discretization and the solution histories used to advance an unsteady flow. This study analyzes solution transfer as a restart operator for the second-order backward differentiation formula and separates approximation order, boundedness, and integral preservation. Nearest-neighbor Taylor interpolation, barycentric interpolation, continuous Galerkin projection, and a control-volume transfer posed as a Petrov--Galerkin condition on the solver's median-dual cells are compared through static fields, repeated Euler remeshing, and stationary-airfoil calculations. Smooth tests recover the intended first- to third-order behavior, except for the control-volume transfer on distorted quadrilaterals. Under repeated Euler remeshing, globally corrected first-order nearest-neighbor Taylor interpolation (NN1) still broadens the density transition despite roundoff-level cumulative integral error, whereas the second-order control-volume transfer (CV2) holds that integral at roundoff with at most 0.07 reference cells of broadening, comparable to second-order Galerkin projection (GP2). An exact history-forcing identity is proved and verified for every transfer, slip-wall vertices included, and controlled restarts isolate the lift response to an older-history error. In Reynolds-averaged Navier--Stokes calculations with two turbulence closures, an application test of these criteria, GP2 reduces turbulence-integral defects by factors of approximately 37--247 relative to second-order nearest-neighbor Taylor interpolation (NN2), and CV2 keeps every monitored integral defect below $5\times10^{-12}$. The aerodynamic comparison remains unresolved under time-step refinement and, for one closure, incomplete inner convergence. These results support assessing a restart through errors in every transferred history, the admissibility of the returned state, integral defects at stated processing stages, and output differences relative to the receiving calculation's numerical sensitivity.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Adaptive remeshing creates a discrete restart whenever the computational mesh is replaced. Before time integration can continue, the numerical solution must be represented on the new mesh. Repeated transfers can accumulate error, so time-accurate adaptation frameworks treat solution transfer together with spatial and temporal discretization~\cite{AlauzetLoseilleOlivier2018,SauvageAlauzetDervieux2024,LevyMay2025}. A multistep restart requires more than the current state: every solution level used by the time integrator must be supplied consistently on the receiving mesh.

Transfer accuracy, bounds, and integral preservation answer different questions. Interpolation order describes approximation of smooth fields; limiting controls new extrema; and conservative projection constrains selected integrals or moments. Common-refinement and Galerkin approaches provide accurate conservative transfer between nonmatching meshes~\cite{JiaoHeath2004,FarrellMaddison2011}, while constrained formulations impose additional requirements such as bounds~\cite{MaddisonHiester2017}. Conservative remapping instead transfers integrals of a reconstructed source field to individual receiving cells~\cite{DukowiczKodis1987,MargolinShashkov2003,AlauzetMehrenberger2010,Alauzet2016}. Conservative Galerkin transfer also supports time-accurate anisotropic adaptation, including high-order implicit formulations with bounded transfer near discontinuities~\cite{LevyMay2024,LevyMay2025}. Preserving a domain integral leaves the spatial distribution undetermined, and enforcing bounds can alter the raw approximation or projection.

A multistep restart makes these distinctions consequential. The receiving discretization advances from transferred histories instead of states generated by its own previous steps. An older-history error can perturb the next residual even when the latest history is exact. Limiting, representation changes, positivity enforcement, and boundary conditions can further modify the transferred field before the solver uses it. A latest-state interpolation norm or a conservation defect alone therefore leaves part of the restart disturbance unmeasured.

The study analyzes solution transfer as a restart operator for a vertex-centered finite-volume discretization advanced with the second-order backward differentiation formula (BDF2), and asks which properties of the operator govern the subsequent flow evolution and aerodynamic loads. Four transfer families---nearest-neighbor Taylor interpolation (NN), barycentric interpolation (BAR), continuous Galerkin projection (GP), and conservative control-volume transfer (CV)---are compared, together with a bounded global correction (GC) that isolates the effect of enforcing a domain integral; numerical suffixes, as in NN1 or GP2, give the nominal smooth-field order. The control-volume transfer is posed as a Petrov--Galerkin condition on the solver's median-dual cells: before limiting and away from the boundary trace, it preserves the integral of the transferred field over every receiving control volume, and it preserves the solver integral globally. It thus serves as a locally conservative finite-volume comparator for the Galerkin projection, which constrains integrals against continuous basis functions.

Smooth and discontinuous static fields isolate approximation order, boundedness, and integral behavior. Repeated Euler remeshing tests accumulation; independent airfoil meshes separate the BDF2 history contributions; and localized transfers track the subsequent load response. Reynolds-averaged Navier--Stokes (RANS) calculations then apply the same criteria as a stress test with turbulence transport and viscous loads, with temporal, iterative, and mesh-sensitivity checks. The flow experiments use prescribed mesh replacements on fixed domains and native discrete references, with three Riemann deformation realizations per amplitude, two in each repeated Euler- and RANS-airfoil comparison, and one in the RANS sensitivity tests.

The contributions are as follows.
\begin{itemize}
\item A history-forcing identity for transferred multistep histories (Proposition~\ref{prop:history-forcing}): at a common trial state, the BDF2 residual changes exactly by $-M_B\eta^n/\Delta t$, where $M_B$ is the receiving finite-volume mass matrix and $\eta^n=2\eps^n-\eps^{n-1}/2$ combines the discrepancies of the two transferred histories, for a nonlinear spatial residual and without a small-error assumption. A common-trial residual test confirms the history-forcing identity for every transfer, including the slip-wall rows, and retaining only the older BDF2 discrepancy reverses the sign of the next-step lift change relative to perturbing both histories.
\item Counterexamples that separate interpolation order, integral preservation, and profile preservation (Secs.~\ref{sec:results-boundedness} and~\ref{sec:results-riemann}, \ref{app:quadrature-mismatch}). After vertex restriction, an exact quadratic projection can carry a second-order error in the solver integral; unlimited CV2 preserves the integral while exceeding the source bounds; and with globally corrected NN1, the density transition widens by 18.43--26.33 reference cells while the cumulative integral defect remains at roundoff.
\item A control-volume $P^1$ transfer posed as a Petrov--Galerkin condition on the median-dual cells (Sec.~\ref{sec:cv-transfer}), with a source representation whose integral equals the solver integral on every supported element type. Its recovery matrix has the control-volume measures as row and column sums and is symmetric positive definite on two-dimensional and tetrahedral meshes. CV2 is globally conservative and holds the repeated-remeshing integral at roundoff with at most 0.07 cell of broadening.
\item A stage-wise assessment of conservation and accuracy (Sec.~\ref{sec:global-correction}), in which integral defects are reported at stated processing stages---construction, limiting, boundary trace, and admissibility conditioning---rather than inferred from the unconstrained operator.
\end{itemize}
The RANS application tests these criteria. GP2 and CV2 leave smaller turbulence-integral defects than NN2, and the paired aerodynamic difference between methods is compared with the numerical sensitivity of the receiving calculation, which prevents a resolved aerodynamic comparison at the tested settings. Section~\ref{sec:conclusions} develops practical requirements from these findings.


\section{Solution-transfer operators}
\label{sec:numerical-framework}

Each transfer consists of a raw target-field construction and constraints on the state returned to the flow solver. The construction families are nearest-neighbor Taylor interpolation (NN), barycentric interpolation (BAR), continuous Galerkin projection (GP), and conservative control-volume transfer (CV). Numerical suffixes denote nominal smooth-field accuracy, not polynomial degree: BAR2 is second-order barycentric interpolation, whereas GP2 and CV2 use piecewise-linear fields. A bounded global correction (GC) adjusts the integral of an existing target field and is treated separately from these constructions.

\subsection{Representation and derivative reconstruction}
\label{sec:derivative-reconstruction}

Source and target meshes, $A$ and $B$, cover the same discretized domain $\Omega$. The nodal state on mesh $M$ is $U_M$, and its finite-volume mass matrix is $M_M=\operatorname{diag}(w_i^M)\otimes I$, where $w_i^M=|C_i^M|$ is the positive measure of the median-dual control volume $C_i^M$ of vertex $i$, $I$ is the identity on the state components, and $\otimes$ denotes the Kronecker product. For one scalar component $\phi$, the solver integral is
\begin{equation}
 Q_M(\phi_M)=\sum_i w_i^M\phi_{M,i}.
 \label{eq:fv-discrete-integral}
\end{equation}
All methods return values at the flow-solver vertices. Mean-flow density, momentum, and total energy are transferred directly in conserved-variable form; $E$ denotes specific total energy, so the energy inventory is $\rho E$. Nodal interpolation is not control-volume averaging, even when Eq.~\eqref{eq:fv-discrete-integral} is used to assess its conservation.

Higher-order transfer requires derivatives recovered from neighboring values. A weighted least-squares fit uses
\begin{equation}
 \phi_j-\phi_i\simeq g_i\cdot r_{ij}+\tfrac12r_{ij}^{T}H_i r_{ij},
 \qquad r_{ij}=x_j-x_i.
 \label{eq:wls-polynomial}
\end{equation}
Here $g_i$ and $H_i$ approximate the gradient and Hessian of $\phi$ at source vertex $i$, and $r_{ij}$ is the displacement to a neighboring vertex. A degree-one fit omits the Hessian. With smooth data, a full-rank scaled stencil, and controlled mesh geometry, the linear fit gives an $O(h)$ gradient error; the quadratic fit gives $O(h^2)$ gradient and $O(h)$ Hessian errors. Anisotropy, weighting, and stencil conditioning can degrade these estimates~\cite{BarthFrederickson1990,Barth1993,Mavriplis2003,DiskinThomas2008}. Third-order variants use the quadratic reconstruction, and their achieved order is verified numerically.

\subsection{Nearest-neighbor and barycentric interpolation}
\label{sec:point-transfer}
\label{sec:barycentric-transfer}

Let $n(j)$ be the source vertex closest to target point $x_j^B$, and $r_j=x_j^B-x_{n(j)}^A$. Nearest-neighbor Taylor transfer gives
\begin{align}
 (\calI^{\mathrm{NN1}}_{AB}\phi_A)_j&=\phi_{A,n(j)},\label{eq:nn1}\\
 (\calI^{\mathrm{NN2}}_{AB}\phi_A)_j&=\phi_{A,n(j)}+g_{n(j)}\cdot r_j,\label{eq:nn2}\\
 (\calI^{\mathrm{NN3}}_{AB}\phi_A)_j&=\phi_{A,n(j)}+g_{n(j)}\cdot r_j
              +\tfrac12r_j^TH_{n(j)}r_j.\label{eq:nn3}
\end{align}
The variants respectively copy the donor value, include its local slope, and include its curvature. For $|r_j|=O(h)$ and sufficiently accurate derivatives, their smooth-field errors are $O(h)$, $O(h^2)$, and $O(h^3)$. The donor changes across source-node Voronoi boundaries, so the transferred value need not vary continuously with target position.

Barycentric interpolation instead uses the source simplex containing the target point. A conforming triangulation or tetrahedralization of the original mesh provides the search geometry, without adding control-volume vertices. In spatial dimension $d$, a simplex has vertices $x_{i_r}^A$, $r=1,\ldots,d+1$. Its barycentric coordinates satisfy
\begin{equation}
 x_j^B=\sum_{r=1}^{d+1}\lambda_r x_{i_r}^A,
 \qquad \sum_r\lambda_r=1,
 \qquad \lambda_r\geq0.
 \label{eq:barycentric-coordinates}
\end{equation}
The same geometric weights define the second-order interpolant and its gradient-corrected extension:
\begin{align}
 (\calI^{\mathrm{BAR2}}_{AB}\phi_A)_j
 &=\sum_r\lambda_r\phi_{A,i_r},\label{eq:bar2}\\
 (\calI^{\mathrm{BAR3}}_{AB}\phi_A)_j
 &=\sum_r\lambda_r\left[\phi_{A,i_r}
       +\tfrac12g_{i_r}\cdot(x_j^B-x_{i_r}^A)\right].\label{eq:bar3}
\end{align}
BAR2 reproduces affine fields and is bounded by the containing-simplex values. For a quadratic field with exact vertex gradients, the half-gradient term cancels its interpolation remainder; BAR3 is consequently third-order consistent. Shared values and gradients give continuity across conforming simplex interfaces. Neither method imposes a source-to-target integral constraint.

Derivative limiting changes these properties independently of nominal order. Multiplying the Taylor corrections by $0\leq\Phi_i\leq1$ reduces NN2 or NN3 to NN1 when $\Phi_i=0$, but reduces BAR3 to BAR2. The smooth verification is unlimited; discontinuous tests also use Barth--Jespersen (BJ) limiting~\cite{BarthJespersen1989}. A limiter derived from linear increments does not automatically bound a quadratic reconstruction at every evaluation point. Extrema must therefore be checked for the actual returned field.

\subsection{Galerkin projection and cross-mesh integration}
\label{sec:galerkin-projection}
\label{sec:simplicial-representation}

Continuous Galerkin projection determines a coupled target field rather than evaluating target vertices independently. Let $\phi_A^h$ be the piecewise-polynomial source representation and $V_B^p$ the continuous degree-$p$ Lagrange space on the target simplicial mesh. The projected field $\phi_B^h\in V_B^p$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
 (\phi_B^h-\phi_A^h,v_B)_{L_2(\Omega)}=0
 \quad\text{for every }v_B\in V_B^p.
 \label{eq:galerkin-orthogonality}
\end{equation}
This is the best $L_2$ approximation of the source representation $\phi_A^h$ in $V_B^p$~\cite{FarrellMaddison2011,JiaoHeath2004}. Writing $\phi_B^h=\sum_jv_jN_j^B$, where $N_j^B$ are target nodal basis functions and $v_j$ their coefficients, gives
\begin{equation}
 H_Bv=b,\qquad
 (H_B)_{ij}=\int_\Omega N_i^BN_j^B\dd x,
 \qquad b_i=\int_\Omega N_i^B\phi_A^h\dd x.
 \label{eq:galerkin-system}
\end{equation}
The sparse positive-definite matrix $H_B$ couples basis functions sharing target elements. It differs from the diagonal finite-volume matrix $M_B$ used for time integration.

A target element can cross several source elements. Although a target basis function has one polynomial expression there, the source field changes expression at each source-element boundary. The cross-mesh load must therefore be integrated as
\begin{equation}
 b_i=\sum_{K_B}\sum_{K_A:\,|K_A\cap K_B|>0}
      \int_{K_A\cap K_B}N_i^B\phi_A^h\dd x.
 \label{eq:galerkin-load-intersections}
\end{equation}
Here $K_A$ and $K_B$ are source and target simplices, and $|K_A\cap K_B|$ is their intersection measure. On each intersection, both expressions are fixed polynomials. These intersections form the common refinement and permit polynomial-exact quadrature: degree two for $P^1$/$P^1$ transfer and degree four for $P^2$/$P^2$ transfer on affine simplices~\cite{Dunavant1985,Keast1986}. Complete, nonoverlapping coverage is required. The intersection geometry and coupled solve distinguish projection from the point searches used by NN and BAR.

GP2 uses $P^1$ source and target spaces. GP3 constructs a $P^2$ source representation, including internally reconstructed edge values, and solves for a complete quadratic target field. Only original-vertex values are then supplied to the flow solver; the edge construction and the consequence of this restriction are detailed in \ref{app:quadrature-mismatch}. On mixed-element meshes the spaces are $P^p$ on conforming simplicial subdivisions, not $Q^p$ on the original elements. Nominal approximation orders are two and three under smoothness and mesh-stability assumptions; accuracy after vertex restriction is assessed separately.

\subsection{Conservative control-volume transfer}
\label{sec:cv-transfer}

Conservative control-volume transfer (CV) constrains the transferred field over the finite-volume cells of the receiving mesh instead of against continuous basis functions. Let $C_i^M$ be the median-dual control volume of vertex $i$ on mesh $M$, so that $w_i^M=|C_i^M|$, and let $C_i^M\cap K$ be its piece in element $K$. Both meshes carry a continuous field $\phi_M^h=\sum_k\phi_{M,k}\psi_k^M$. On triangles and tetrahedra, $\psi_k^M=N_k^M$ is the $P^1$ hat function. A quadrilateral is divided into four triangles joining its edges to its vertex average; hexahedra, prisms, and pyramids are divided into tetrahedra joining each face triangle to the element vertex average, after quadrilateral faces are divided about their own vertex average. Each $\psi_k^M$ is linear on every subelement, equal to one at vertex $k$ and zero at the other vertices. The value at an added element center is a fixed weighted average of the element vertex values, with weights chosen such that
\begin{equation}
 \int_K\psi_k^M\dd x=|C_k^M\cap K|
 \qquad\text{for every element $K$ and vertex $k$ of $M$.}
 \label{eq:cv-lumped-exact}
\end{equation}
For a quadrilateral, the center weight of vertex $k$ is $|C_k^M\cap K|/|K|$. Summation over elements gives $\int_\Omega\psi_k^M\dd x=w_k^M$, and hence $\int_\Omega\phi_M^h\dd x=Q_M(\phi_M)$: the field integral equals the solver integral, Eq.~\eqref{eq:fv-discrete-integral}, on every supported element type. The source field is thus the continuous interpolant of the nodal solver values rather than a piecewise-constant or piecewise-linear reconstruction on the source control volumes. It reproduces the source integral $Q_A$, but not the individual contents $w_i^A\phi_{A,i}$ of the source control volumes: before limiting and boundary processing, CV transfer preserves the domain integral and the field integral over each receiving control volume, not the content of each source control volume. On parallelograms the weights reduce to the arithmetic vertex average, and the representation reproduces affine fields. On quadrilaterals that remain distorted it does not, because Eq.~\eqref{eq:cv-lumped-exact} is imposed in preference to affine reproduction.

The $n_B$ target vertex values are defined by
\begin{equation}
 \int_{C_j^B}\left(\phi_B^h-\phi_A^h\right)\dd x=0,
 \qquad j=1,\ldots,n_B.
 \label{eq:cv-condition}
\end{equation}
This is a Petrov--Galerkin condition with trial functions $\psi_k^B$ and the indicator functions of the receiving control volumes as test functions. Intersection-based $P^1$-conservative interpolation~\cite{AlauzetMehrenberger2010,Alauzet2016} instead preserves the integral over each receiving element through an element-wise linear reconstruction, limits its gradient, and recovers vertex values by explicit volume-weighted averaging over the elements incident to each vertex. Equation~\eqref{eq:cv-condition} imposes the integrals over the solver control volumes directly and therefore requires the solution of a sparse system. In matrix form,
\begin{equation}
 \widetilde H_B\phi_B=\tilde b,\qquad
 (\widetilde H_B)_{jk}=\int_{C_j^B}\psi_k^B\dd x,
 \qquad \tilde b_j=\int_{C_j^B}\phi_A^h\dd x.
 \label{eq:cv-system}
\end{equation}
Because the functions $\psi_k^B$ sum to one, the row sums of $\widetilde H_B$ are $w_j^B$; by Eq.~\eqref{eq:cv-lumped-exact}, its column sums are $w_k^B$. The matrix has nonnegative entries and the element sparsity of $H_B$. It is symmetric on meshes of triangles and strictly convex quadrilaterals and on tetrahedral meshes: on a simplex, the vertex permutations exchange the median-dual pieces, and on a quadrilateral, the center weight of each vertex is proportional to its median-dual piece. Its entries depend only on target geometry and are evaluated in closed form.

The source field changes expression at the boundaries of the source subelements, and the test function at the boundaries of the target median-dual pieces. The load is therefore integrated over three-way intersections,
\begin{equation}
 \tilde b_j=\sum_{K_B\ni j}\ \sum_{K_A:\,|K_A\cap K_B|>0}\
      \int_{K_A\cap K_B\cap C_j^B}\phi_A^h\dd x,
 \label{eq:cv-load-intersections}
\end{equation}
where $K_B$ ranges over the target elements incident to vertex $j$ and $K_A$ over the source simplices of the subdivision above. Each median-dual piece is convex; in a triangle or tetrahedron, the piece of vertex $i$ is the region in which its barycentric coordinate is largest. Every intersection is a convex polygon or polyhedron obtained by exact clipping. The source field is linear there and the test function constant, so the intersection measure times the source value at its centroid integrates each contribution exactly, without the higher-degree quadrature of Eq.~\eqref{eq:galerkin-load-intersections}. The source simplices overlapping each target element are located by a straight-line walk through the source adjacency followed by an advancing front. Any part of a target piece not covered by source simplices receives the convex combination of donor values at the nearest point of the closest exposed source boundary face, evaluated at the centroid of the uncovered part and restricted to the matching boundary marker when boundary matching is requested. This content enters $\tilde b_j$, so the transferred integral then differs from the source integral by the content assigned to the uncovered measure. In the calculations reported here, source and target meshes cover the same polygonal domain, and the uncovered measure remains below $10^{-20}$ of the domain.

The solution of Eq.~\eqref{eq:cv-system} consists of the nodal coefficients of $\phi_B^h$, which the flow solver receives as vertex values. The control-volume averages $\tilde b_j/w_j^B$ are not returned: for a smooth field they approximate the value at the control-volume centroid, which differs from $x_j^B$ by $O(h)$ at boundaries and on nonuniform meshes. They serve instead as the initial iterate of a Jacobi-preconditioned stabilized biconjugate-gradient (BiCGSTAB) iteration, terminated at a relative residual of $10^{-15}$. On triangles and tetrahedra, each element contributes to a row of $\widetilde H_B$ with an off-diagonal-to-diagonal ratio of $7/11$ and $23/25$, respectively, independently of element shape. On simplicial meshes, the eigenvalues of the Jacobi-preconditioned matrix therefore lie in a disc of radius $7/11$ (triangles) or $23/25$ (tetrahedra) about one, independently of resolution and aspect ratio. Quadrilateral contributions are also strictly diagonally dominant. A symmetric matrix with a positive, strictly dominant diagonal is positive definite, so $\widetilde H_B$ is symmetric positive definite on two-dimensional meshes and on tetrahedral meshes. Hexahedra, prisms and pyramids are not diagonally dominant (on the reference cube the diagonal entry is $15/32$ of the dual-piece volume and the off-diagonal sum $17/32$), and their contributions are symmetric only on affine cells.

Summing Eq.~\eqref{eq:cv-condition} over the receiving control volumes, which tile $\Omega$, and using the column sums of $\widetilde H_B$ gives
\begin{equation}
 Q_B(\phi_B)=\bm1^T\widetilde H_B\phi_B=\bm1^T\tilde b
   =\int_\Omega\phi_A^h\dd x=Q_A(\phi_A),
 \label{eq:cv-global}
\end{equation}
to within the solver tolerance and any fallback content. The local statement, Eq.~\eqref{eq:cv-condition}, concerns the field: $\int_{C_j^B}\phi_B^h\dd x$ equals the source content of $C_j^B$. The nodal content $w_j^B\phi_{B,j}$ represented by $M_B$ differs from it by $(\widetilde H_B\phi_B)_j-w_j^B\phi_{B,j}$, which sums to zero over the mesh but does not vanish individually. For $B=A$, $\tilde b=\widetilde H_A\phi_A$, and the transfer returns $\phi_A$. If both representations reproduce affine fields, uniqueness of the solution of Eq.~\eqref{eq:cv-system} makes the transfer exact for them. CV2 is consequently nominally second order on simplicial meshes and on quadrilaterals that remain parallelograms under refinement; persistent quadrilateral distortion reduces the attainable order. No higher-order CV variant is used.

On simplicial meshes, CV2 and GP2 seek the target field in the same space $V_B^1$, from the same $P^1$ source field and common refinement, and both solve a sparse system with the element coupling of $H_B$. They differ in the test space and therefore in the integrals they preserve. GP2 tests against the hat functions: $H_B$ is the symmetric consistent mass matrix, the result is the $L_2$ best approximation, and the integral of the field and its coordinate moments are preserved over $\Omega$, but not over individual control volumes. CV2 tests against the control-volume indicators: before limiting and away from the boundary trace, the field integral over every receiving control volume is preserved, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:cv-global} holds for the solver integral, but the result is not a best approximation, and coordinate moments are not preserved in general. CV2 is thus a coupled transfer with local conservation constraints, not an explicit cell-by-cell remap: $\widetilde H_B^{-1}$, like $H_B^{-1}$, couples all target vertices. On meshes containing quadrilaterals, GP2 uses a conforming triangulation without added vertices, whereas CV2 uses the centered subdivision above with dependent center values.

Limiting follows the GP rule of Sec.~\ref{sec:galerkin-limiter}. The bounds of vertex $j$ are the extrema of the source vertex values on the source simplices intersecting $C_j^B$, together with any fallback value assigned there, widened by $\tau$ times the range of these bounds over the target mesh. Values outside the interval are clipped, and the clipped content is redistributed among vertices with remaining capacity so that $Q_B(\phi_B)=\sum_j\tilde b_j$. The flow calculations use $\tau=2\times10^{-3}$, as for GP2; CV2-u denotes the unlimited transfer. Mean-flow and turbulence variables are transferred in the same form as for GP2, and the returned state is conditioned as described in Sec.~\ref{sec:global-correction}.

The cost is dominated by the cross-mesh integration. Relative to the Galerkin load, each source--target intersection is additionally clipped against the median-dual pieces of the target element, while a one-point rule replaces the degree-two quadrature. Work scales with the number of intersections, and memory with the number of elements; in three dimensions, target elements are processed in blocks, which bounds memory without affecting the result. Every target element is intersected, including elements that coincide with source elements. The recovery solve adds a few sparse matrix--vector products per field, with an iteration count that the diagonal dominance above bounds independently of resolution on simplicial meshes. The construction applies to tetrahedra, hexahedra, prisms, and pyramids; the calculations reported here are two-dimensional.

\subsection{Integral preservation, bounds, and the returned state}
\label{sec:global-correction}
\label{sec:galerkin-vertex-output}
\label{sec:galerkin-limiter}

When the unconstrained target space contains constants, Eq.~\eqref{eq:galerkin-orthogonality} preserves the integral of the complete finite-element field. It also preserves consistent coordinate moments represented by the test space. These identities do not imply conservation over arbitrary finite-volume cells, whose indicator functions generally lie outside a continuous finite-element space. CV2 uses these indicators as test functions; Eq.~\eqref{eq:cv-condition} preserves the field integral over each receiving control volume, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:cv-global} the solver integral. Neither the Galerkin mass-matrix inverse nor $\widetilde H_B^{-1}$ is a local operator: unchanged geometry in a subregion does not guarantee unchanged transferred values there.

For GP2, consistent and solver-lumped integrals coincide when $w_i^M=\int_\Omega N_i^M\dd x$ on both meshes. This condition holds on simplicial meshes; on the conforming triangulation of a quadrilateral, the hat-function integrals generally differ from the median-dual measures. The CV2 representation satisfies the corresponding condition, Eq.~\eqref{eq:cv-lumped-exact}, on every supported element type. For GP3, source reconstruction and target vertex restriction introduce additional representation differences. An exact quadratic projection can acquire a second-order lumped-integral error after vertex restriction (\ref{app:quadrature-mismatch}). A third-order pointwise approximation therefore does not imply a third-order conservation defect in the solver representation.

The bounded global correction can enforce Eq.~\eqref{eq:fv-discrete-integral} for any base transfer. For a raw target field $\phi_B^r$, the correction $c$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
 \sum_jw_j^Bc_j=Q_A(\phi_A)-Q_B(\phi_B^r),
 \qquad \ell_j\leq\phi_{B,j}^r+c_j\leq u_j.
 \label{eq:global-correction-constraint}
\end{equation}
The nodal lower and upper bounds are $\ell_j$ and $u_j$. The defect is redistributed among nodes with available capacity, with saturated nodes fixed~\cite{ShashkovWendroff2004,KucharikShashkovWendroff2003}. The requested integral must lie within the weighted bounds. A small integral defect does not bound each correction, and bounds imposed separately on conserved components do not ensure positive pressure.

GP and CV limiting use source-vertex extrema in overlapping elements: for GP, the source elements overlapping the target simplices incident to a vertex; for CV, the source simplices intersecting its receiving control volume. The interval is widened by a dimensionless tolerance $\tau$ times the global source range, followed by clipping and redistribution preserving a selected lumped total, the simplicial lumped total for GP and $Q_B$ for CV. Hard limiting has $\tau=0$; soft limiting permits a finite excursion. Once active, limiting need not preserve Galerkin orthogonality, moments, or best approximation. Because the clipped content is redistributed among all vertices with remaining capacity, it also perturbs the per-volume identity of Eq.~\eqref{eq:cv-condition} while retaining $Q_B$. Boundary processing and positivity corrections can change the field further. For every method, each vertex on a boundary marker receives the full conserved state of the source trace: the convex combination of donor values at the closest point of the source boundary faces with the same marker. This step keeps values from crossing between physical boundaries, such as a wall and the far field, and gives wall vertices a state that already satisfies the wall condition imposed strongly in the source solution. The trace replaces the boundary values of the transfer method, so Galerkin orthogonality and the per-volume condition of Eq.~\eqref{eq:cv-condition} do not hold at boundary vertices and their neighbors. The trace also satisfies the receiving wall condition only where the source and target boundary vertices coincide: where the discretized surface changes between the meshes, the trace carries the source wall orientation, and its momentum has a component normal to the target wall that grows with the change of the surface discretization. For GP, CV, and globally corrected transfers, the free neighbors of the changed vertices absorb the change of the lumped total, scalar solver floors are imposed by bounded redistribution of the lumped total, and any remaining inadmissible states are blended toward patch means on patches of adjacent vertices, with each patch lumped total held fixed and, when limiting is active, within the widened global source bounds. These stages retain $Q_B$ but not the per-volume integrals inside the affected patches. Conservation and accuracy are consequently assessed at specified stages, rather than assigned to the final state from properties of the unconstrained projection or control-volume condition.

\section{Multistep response to a mesh change}
\label{sec:restart-consistency}

The receiving time integrator combines the transferred histories to determine the restart forcing. The standard constant-step BDF2 weights are used here as a controlled diagnostic of transferred-history error. With the native target-mesh trajectory as a common reference, the analysis separates the exact residual forcing from the conditional response of the solved state and aerodynamic outputs.

\subsection{Native and transferred histories}
\label{sec:unsteady-discretization}
\label{sec:native-transfer-decomposition}

Between mesh replacements the semi-discrete equations are
\begin{equation}
 M_M\dot U_M+F_M(U_M,t)=0.
 \label{eq:semidiscrete}
\end{equation}
Here $t$ is physical time and $F_M$ is the assembled spatial residual, including fluxes, source terms, and the imposed boundary treatment. The state includes every transported variable required by the time integrator, including turbulence variables for RANS. Boundary constraints must be imposed consistently on the histories and on the equations advanced in time. At a constant time step, the target BDF2 equation is
\begin{equation}
 \Phi_B(Y;V,W)=\frac{M_B}{\Delta t}
        \left(\tfrac32Y-2V+\tfrac12W\right)+F_B(Y,t^{n+1})=0.
 \label{eq:bdf2}
\end{equation}
Here $V$ and $W$ are the latest and older histories. The mesh is stationary during each time step. A continuously moving mesh would require compatible geometric histories and a discrete geometric conservation law~\cite{KoobusFarhat1999,GeuzaineGrandmontFarhat2003}; Eq.~\eqref{eq:bdf2} is not intended as that general formulation.

Let $U_A^k$ and $U_B^k$ denote native trajectories for the same physical problem on two meshes. The transferred histories and their target-referenced discrepancies are
\begin{equation}
 \widehat U_B^k=\calI_{AB}(U_A^k),\qquad
 \eps^k=\widehat U_B^k-U_B^k,\qquad k=n,n-1.
 \label{eq:history-defects}
\end{equation}
The effective map $\calI_{AB}$ includes the processing needed to form admissible histories. The discrepancy $\eps^k$ combines interpolation error with differences between the native source and target discretizations. The native target state $U_B^k$ supplies a common receiving evolution operator, not an exact continuum reference; \ref{app:reference-decomposition} gives the nonlinear decomposition.

\subsection{Exact history forcing and conditional state response}

At a common trial state, the transferred and native BDF2 equations differ only through their histories, and this difference is exact.

\begin{proposition}[History forcing of transferred histories]
\label{prop:history-forcing}
Let $\Phi_B$ be the constant-step BDF2 residual of Eq.~\eqref{eq:bdf2}, and let the transferred histories differ from the native target histories by $\eps^n$ and $\eps^{n-1}$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:history-defects}. Then, for every trial state $Y$,
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 &\Phi_B(Y;U_B^n+\eps^n,U_B^{n-1}+\eps^{n-1})
       -\Phi_B(Y;U_B^n,U_B^{n-1})\\
 &\hspace{2cm}=-\frac{M_B}{\Delta t}\eta^n,
 \qquad \eta^n=2\eps^n-\tfrac12\eps^{n-1}.
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:history-residual-identity}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Both residuals are evaluated on mesh $B$ at the same trial state $Y$ and time $t^{n+1}$, so the terms $\tfrac32M_BY/\Delta t$ and $F_B(Y,t^{n+1})$ cancel in the difference. The remaining terms of Eq.~\eqref{eq:bdf2} are linear in the histories, with coefficient $-2M_B/\Delta t$ for $V$ and $M_B/(2\Delta t)$ for $W$. Their difference at $V=U_B^n+\eps^n$, $W=U_B^{n-1}+\eps^{n-1}$ and at $V=U_B^n$, $W=U_B^{n-1}$ is $(M_B/\Delta t)(-2\eps^n+\tfrac12\eps^{n-1})=-(M_B/\Delta t)\eta^n$.\qed
\end{proof}

The proof uses no linearity of the spatial residual and no small-error assumption: Eq.~\eqref{eq:history-residual-identity} holds for a nonlinear $F_B$ and for discrepancies of any size. It requires the same trial state, boundary treatment, and residual sign convention in both evaluations; a solver that reports $-\Phi_B$ reverses the sign of both sides. Where the boundary treatment modifies residual rows, the predicted forcing must undergo the same operation before the comparison, and these rows are assessed separately (Sec.~\ref{sec:results-bdf2-new} and \ref{app:history-checks}).

The solved-state difference $\delta^{n+1}=\widehat U_B^{n+1}-U_B^{n+1}$ obeys a different relation. Let $r$ and $\widehat r$ be the algebraic residuals remaining after the native and perturbed physical steps. Subtraction and expansion about the native target state give
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 A_B\delta^{n+1}&=M_B\eta^n+\Delta t(\widehat r-r)
                 -\Delta t\,\mathcal N_B(\delta^{n+1})\\
 A_B&=\tfrac32M_B+\Delta t J_B,
 \qquad J_B=\frac{\partial F_B}{\partial U}.
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:linearized-bdf2-response}
\end{equation}
The Jacobian $J_B$ is evaluated at the native target solution at $t^{n+1}$, and $\mathcal N_B$ is the nonlinear Taylor remainder. A locally Lipschitz Jacobian gives $\mathcal N_B(\delta)=O(\|\delta\|^2)$. If the step is locally invertible, the perturbation is small, and the algebraic errors are negligible, then
\begin{equation}
 \delta^{n+1}=R_B\eta^n+O(\Delta t\|\eta^n\|^2),
 \qquad R_B=A_B^{-1}M_B.
 \label{eq:state-response}
\end{equation}
The bound is local to the mesh, state, time step, and norm. Limiter switching and incomplete inner convergence can invalidate this smooth-response approximation without invalidating Eq.~\eqref{eq:history-residual-identity}.

A superscript $\star$ denotes a computed steady reference state. Table~\ref{tab:naca-history-decomposition} assigns the same spatial discrepancy $\eps^\star=\calI_{AB}U_A^\star-U_B^\star$ to the two histories independently.

\label{tab:naca-history-decomposition}

Writing the corresponding responses as $\delta_f$, $\delta_n$, and $\delta_{n-1}$, local linearity predicts
\begin{equation}
 \delta_n\simeq\tfrac43\delta_f,\qquad
 \delta_{n-1}\simeq-\tfrac13\delta_f,\qquad
 \delta_f\simeq\delta_n+\delta_{n-1}.
 \label{eq:naca-history-ratios}
\end{equation}
The older-only case tests whether a latest-state diagnostic misses a finite forcing. These collinear perturbations test directional response; they do not determine $R_B$ for arbitrary history fields.

\subsection{Aerodynamic response}
\label{sec:delayed-response}

For a differentiable target-mesh output $G_B$, define $\Delta G^{n+1}=G_B(\widehat U_B^{n+1})-G_B(U_B^{n+1})$. With $g_B^{n+1}$ the gradient of $G_B$ with respect to the state at the native target solution, Eq.~\eqref{eq:state-response} yields
\begin{equation}
 \Delta G^{n+1}=(g_B^{n+1})^TR_B\eta^n+O(\|\eta^n\|^2).
 \label{eq:force-response}
\end{equation}
The load response depends on the spatial discrepancy weighted by the target dynamics and output sensitivity, not just its integral. In particular, preserving the source integral imposes, componentwise,
\begin{equation}
 \bm1^TM_B\eps^k=Q_A(U_A^k)-Q_B(U_B^k).
 \label{eq:gc-native-mismatch}
\end{equation}
The right-hand side need not vanish because the native discrete states can have different integrals. Global correction therefore need not reduce the target-referenced state or load discrepancy.

After the first step, the new solution difference and remaining older history affect subsequent evolution, so the initial load difference need not bound the later excursion. Disturbances away from the airfoil can produce delayed responses on acoustic or convective scales. Because neither the Galerkin mass-matrix inverse nor an implicit step has compact spatial support, the experiments identify arrival through finite-amplitude thresholds.


\section{Numerical experiments}
\label{sec:methodology}


\subsection{Static transfer verification}
\label{sec:static-verification}

Static transfer tests exclude flow-solver evolution so that operator properties can be measured directly. The smooth asymmetric field is
\begin{equation}
 \phi=\rho_\infty\left[2+0.3e^{0.8x/L_x+0.5y/L_y}
              \sin(2\pi x/L_x)\sin(2\pi y/L_y)\right].
 \label{eq:asymmetric-manufactured-field}
\end{equation}
Here $\rho_\infty$ is the reference density, and $L_x$ and $L_y$ are the coordinate length scales. Structured quadrilateral and independently generated mixed triangular/quadrilateral target meshes have nominal resolutions 64, 128, and 256; source resolution is half the target resolution. Relative nodal density errors are measured against the analytic field at target vertices. The effective spacing $h_{\mathrm{eff}}=N_{\mathrm{nodes}}^{-1/2}$ defines refinement ratios on the fixed domain. Pairwise orders are
\begin{equation}
 p_{k,k+1}=\frac{\log(E_k/E_{k+1})}{\log(h_k/h_{k+1})}.
 \label{eq:pairwise-observed-order}
\end{equation}
Here $E_k$ is the relative nodal error and $h_k$ the effective spacing at refinement level $k$. A least-squares fit of $\log E$ against $\log h$ summarizes each three-level sequence. The discontinuous field has density 1.2 outside and 3.6 inside a circular inclusion of radius $0.25\min(L_x,L_y)$, whose interface crosses the mesh. Unlimited and limited transfers are compared through overshoot, undershoot, and the source-to-target lumped-integral defect.

\subsection{Repeated Euler remeshing}
\label{sec:riemann-methodology}

The repeated-remeshing calculation asks whether the one-transfer properties measured above predict cumulative spatial fidelity. It uses a two-dimensional Euler calculation on $[0,5]\times[0,1]$. The uniform reference mesh has $501\times101$ vertices and spacing $h=0.01$. The gas is ideal, with heat-capacity ratio $\gamma=1.4$ and unit gas constant. Density, pressure, and temperature are denoted by $\rho$, $p$, and $T$, and Cartesian velocity components by $u$ and $v$. Initially,
\begin{equation}
 (\rho,u,v,p)=
 \begin{cases}
 (1,0,0,1), & x<2,\\
 (0.125,0,0,0.1), & x\geq2,
 \end{cases},
 \label{eq:riemann-initial-state}
\end{equation}
so the left and right temperatures are 1 and 0.8. The upper and lower boundaries are inviscid slip walls. At $x=0$ and $x=5$, characteristic boundary conditions prescribe total pressure and temperature $(p_0,T_0)=(1,1)$ and $(0.1,0.8)$, respectively, with direction $(1,0)$ at both ends.

The calculation advances 400 physical steps with $\Delta t=10^{-3}$. Interior vertices receive fresh random displacements in $x$ relative to the original mesh; boundaries remain fixed, and geometric distortion does not accumulate between successive meshes. For vertex $i$, let $a_{i,\min}$ be the smallest altitude of the incident corner triangles. The admissible streamwise displacement is bounded by $a_{i,\min}/s$, where the dimensionless deformation parameter $s$ controls mesh perturbation amplitude. Values $s=4$ and $s=2$ are used; $s=2$ permits twice the maximum displacement of $s=4$.

Mesh replacement intervals of ten and two physical steps compare low- and high-frequency transfer. Both BDF2 histories are transferred at each mesh change. The low-frequency comparison contains 80 transfer evaluations; the high-frequency integral record contains 398. The fixed-mesh numerical reference uses the same initial and boundary conditions; differences from it include transfer error and the change in spatial discretization.
% TODO(author): Clarify the high-frequency counting convention: data/riemann_frequency_full.csv records cycles=400, whereas the manuscript reports 398 transfer evaluations. Preserve the recorded values pending clarification.

Density profiles are averaged in $y$ with a common sampling procedure. The transition position is its 50\% crossing, and its width is the distance between the 10\% and 90\% crossings. The differences $\Delta x$ and $\Delta w$ denote the transferred-calculation position and width minus their fixed-mesh reference values, expressed in cells of the $h=0.01$ reference mesh. They describe a resolved density transition, without presuming a separately identified sharp shock. Density and energy integral changes are evaluated immediately across each transfer, separately from physical evolution. With $I_\rho=Q_M(\rho_M)$, each signed increment $\Delta I_\rho$ is the target integral minus the source integral; $\sum\Delta I_\rho$ accumulates these signed increments. NN1, NN1+GC, BAR2, GP2, and CV2 are compared at both amplitudes; additional methods extend the frequency comparison at $s=4$.

\subsection{Euler airfoil and controlled BDF2 histories}
\label{sec:naca-fixed-methodology}

The airfoil calculations test consequences that cannot be isolated in the repeated Riemann problem: the independent contribution of the two BDF2 histories and the evolution of a transfer disturbance into an aerodynamic output. Euler flow over the National Advisory Committee for Aeronautics (NACA) 0012 airfoil is calculated at freestream Mach number $M_\infty=0.1$ and angle of attack $\alpha=6^\circ$, with $p_\infty=\rho_\infty=T_\infty=1$, $a_\infty=\sqrt{1.4}$, $U_\infty=0.118322$, and $\Delta t=10^{-3}$. Here the subscript $\infty$ denotes freestream quantities, $a_\infty$ is the sound speed, and $U_\infty$ is the speed. The airfoil chord is denoted by $c$. The source and independent target meshes contain 16,255 and 22,508 vertices, respectively, with 32,077 and 44,583 triangles. Both retain the same 370 airfoil vertices and 63-vertex farfield polygon at $100c$, so the discretized surface is unchanged. Forces are pressure-only, with unit reference chord and area and a moment reference at $x/c=0.25$; lift and drag coefficients are denoted $C_L$ and $C_D$. Nonzero steady drag is treated as a numerical load, not skin friction or shock-induced drag.

Each native steady solution is continued with equal initial BDF2 histories. The history combinations in Table~\ref{tab:naca-history-decomposition} are then formed on the target mesh and compared with its native continuation at the same physical step. State differences use the nondimensional volume-weighted norm $\|v\|_{M_B}=(v^TM_Bv)^{1/2}$ over the stored mean-flow components. Supplementary tests also report the unweighted Euclidean norm $\|v\|_2$; these norms weight the near-body and large farfield cells differently. Relative superposition defects are
\begin{equation}
 e_{\mathrm{sup}}^U=
 \frac{\|\delta_f-\delta_n-\delta_{n-1}\|_{M_B}}{\|\delta_f\|_{M_B}},
 \qquad
 e_{\mathrm{sup}}^{C_L}=
 \frac{|\Delta C_{L,f}-\Delta C_{L,n}-\Delta C_{L,n-1}|}{|\Delta C_{L,f}|}.
 \label{eq:history-superposition-error}
\end{equation}
State-norm ratios are nonnegative; force ratios retain their sign.

The solver reports $-\Phi_B$. Its initial residuals are compared before an update using two common-trial pairs: older-only minus native at $U_B^\star$, and latest-only minus both-histories at $\widehat U_B^\star$. Their differences in $\eta$ are $-\eps^\star/2$ and $+\eps^\star/2$, respectively. Double-precision residuals and a wall-normal/tangential split separate temporal forcing from slip-wall enforcement. Additional cancellation and amplitude tests are described in \ref{app:history-checks}.

A repeated-remeshing experiment perturbs vertices at wall distances between $0.01c$ and $20c$, while retaining the boundaries. NN2, GP2, and CV2 use matching deformation realizations, with 39 mesh replacements followed by evolution in a 400-step interval. Load differences are formed against the corresponding fixed-mesh trajectory. Slopes per 100 physical steps describe this finite window; they estimate neither long-time drift nor independent-sample uncertainty.

A separate single-transfer experiment perturbs narrow bands beginning at wall distances $0.05c$, $0.10c$, and $0.20c$. It uses GP2; CV2 repeats the longer $0.20c$-band calculation. Fixed pressure shells measure the root-mean-square (RMS) pressure difference from a common fixed-mesh reference, and force onset is defined by a fraction of the response maximum. For a shell at $r$ inward of a band at $d$, the low-Mach acoustic scale is $(d-r)/(a_\infty\Delta t)\simeq845(d-r)$ steps. Immediate disturbances extend up to approximately $0.08c$ inward of the displaced band; these shells are excluded from the threshold-dependent propagation fit.

\subsection{Fixed-body RANS and numerical sensitivity assessment}
\label{sec:rans-methodology}

The fixed-body RANS experiment is an application test of the assessment criteria of Secs.~\ref{sec:numerical-framework} and~\ref{sec:restart-consistency} under turbulence transport and viscous loads. It perturbs a nominally steady viscous state by repeated remeshing and asks whether transfer-level differences between methods exceed the numerical sensitivity of the receiving calculation; it neither validates the turbulence closures nor aims at an aerodynamic ranking. It retains $M_\infty=0.1$ and $\alpha=6^\circ$, with chord Reynolds number $Re_c=6\times10^6$ and the Spalart--Allmaras (SA) and shear-stress-transport (SST) closures~\cite{SpalartAllmaras1994,Menter1994}. The coarse mesh has 23,271 vertices, 9,686 quadrilaterals in the wall layer and wake region, 26,851 triangles, and 256 airfoil vertices. The medium and fine meshes contain 32,102 and 48,228 vertices. All three retain the same first off-wall spacing. The farfield is at $100c$; the first off-wall spacing is approximately $5\times10^{-6}c$, and the quadrilateral region extends to about $0.11c$.

The calculations use the Stanford University Unstructured (SU2) solver~\cite{EconomonEtAl2016SU2}. Mean-flow convection uses the Harten--Lax--van Leer contact (HLLC) flux with a monotonic upstream-centered scheme for conservation laws (MUSCL) reconstruction; turbulence transport uses scalar upwinding without MUSCL reconstruction. BDF2 physical steps are solved by dual-time iteration at a Courant--Friedrichs--Lewy (CFL) number of 6. Linear systems are solved with flexible generalized minimal residual (FGMRES) iterations and incomplete lower--upper (ILU) preconditioning. The nominal inner-iteration limit is 800 iterations. After ten iterations, the stopping test checks $\log_{10}\operatorname{rms}(r_\rho)\leq-12$, where $r_\rho$ is the nodal density-equation residual in the solver normalization. The dimensional physical step $\Delta t_0=10^{-3}$~s corresponds to the nondimensional value $\Delta t_0^*=0.347224$. A superscript $*$ denotes quantities normalized with the solver reference scales. With $U_\infty^*=0.1$, the 400-step interval spans approximately 13.9 chord flow-through times, where convective time is measured by $tU_\infty/c$. A first-order startup step is ten times smaller. Each closure has a no-remeshing reference continued for 800 steps from matching histories.

On the coarse mesh, the steady SST solution reaches the density-residual target, whereas the SA solution reaches its 20,000-iteration limit at $-11.2$, with lift and drag stationary to $10^{-7}$ over the final 2,000 iterations. The steady SST solution on the medium mesh stops at approximately $-11.1$. These residual levels and the reference continuations are distinguished from convergence of the remeshed physical steps.

The same prescribed mesh sequences are used for NN2, limited GP2, and limited CV2. At each replacement, 12,602 vertices may move and 10,669 are fixed. No vertex with solver wall distance below $0.01c$ moves; the geometric mask uses distance to the nearest airfoil vertex, and part of the outer quadrilateral layer can move. The nominal RANS deformation uses $s=4$, and a minimum corner-area ratio of 0.2 preserves valid cells. The boundaries remain fixed. Mesh replacement occurs every ten physical steps, independently of the evolving solution.

Both mean-flow and turbulence histories are transferred at the required time levels. SA transports the working variable $\widetilde\nu$. For SST, $k$ and $\omega$ denote turbulent kinetic energy and specific dissipation rate; their density-weighted values are transferred before primitive-variable positivity floors are applied. Wall distance and eddy viscosity are recomputed. Turbulence inventories change under their transport equations and are not physical invariants. The SST integral diagnostic precedes the final positivity floor, so it characterizes transfer at that stage rather than conservation of the fully processed restart.

The pressure and viscous contributions to total drag are denoted by $C_{D,p}$ and $C_{D,v}$, respectively. For an aerodynamic coefficient $C_Q$ and transfer method $m$, define the remeshing-induced response and paired method difference by
\begin{equation}
 D_{m,Q}(t)=C_{Q,m}(t)-C_{Q,\mathrm{ref}}(t),
 \qquad P_Q(t)=D_{\mathrm{NN2},Q}(t)-D_{\mathrm{GP2},Q}(t).
 \label{eq:rans-comparisons}
\end{equation}
Pressure and viscous contributions use identical surface quadrature for the remeshed and reference calculations. Over $N$ common physical sample times $t_i$, the RMS of a scalar history $q$ is $\mathcal R(q)=(N^{-1}\sum_i q(t_i)^2)^{1/2}$. For a modified numerical setting $c$, relative to the nominal setting denoted by subscript $0$, sensitivity is measured by
\begin{equation}
 S(q;c)=\frac{\mathcal R(q_c-q_0)}{\mathcal R(q_0)}.
 \label{eq:control-sensitivity}
\end{equation}
This measures the difference between histories, not the fractional change of their RMS amplitudes. The latter is $[\mathcal R(q_c)-\mathcal R(q_0)]/\mathcal R(q_0)$.

The sensitivity assessment uses a common 38-sample window containing three mesh replacements. Inner-iteration limits are increased to 1600 for both closures and to 6400 for SST. Time-step refinement uses $\Delta t_0/2$ for both closures and $\Delta t_0/4$ for SA, with identical displacement fields applied at matching physical times and a separate no-remeshing reference for each step size. The medium and fine steady meshes assess absolute-load sensitivity. A short SA remeshing comparison on the medium mesh uses the same displacement statistics and amplitude limit but an independent nodal deformation field, combining resolution and disturbance changes.

The steady viscous meshes show absolute-load sensitivity without establishing an asymptotic refinement sequence or bounding the paired NN2--GP2 error on a common mesh. The medium-mesh transient also changes the nodal disturbance, so refinement alone cannot explain its response. As a test of the criteria, the RANS results separate a resolved transfer-level property, smaller measured transfer-integral defects for GP2 and near-roundoff defects for CV2, including turbulence inventories, from an output comparison that the numerical sensitivity of the receiving calculation does not resolve: the aerodynamic advantage of either over NN2 remains unresolved at the tested settings.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

This study treats solution transfer before a multistep restart as an operator on every history used by the time integrator and assesses it by criteria that a latest-state interpolation norm or a global conservation defect does not provide. At a common trial state, the BDF2 history forcing of the transferred histories is exact, with no linearity or small-error assumption (Proposition~\ref{prop:history-forcing}). Static and repeated-remeshing counterexamples show that interpolation order, boundedness, integral preservation, and profile preservation are separate properties. The control-volume transfer, posed as a Petrov--Galerkin condition on the median-dual cells with a symmetric positive definite recovery matrix on two-dimensional and tetrahedral meshes, is globally conservative; its per-volume condition holds before limiting and away from the boundary trace, which is why conservation is assessed at stated processing stages.

The static inclusion test separates conservation from boundedness: unlimited CV2 preserves the lumped integral to roundoff while exceeding the source bounds, and unlimited NN2 returns an inadmissible state. Repeated Euler remeshing separates integral preservation from profile preservation: NN1+GC holds its signed cumulative density-integral defect at roundoff while the transition still broadens substantially, whereas CV2 holds the same integral at roundoff with at most 0.07 cell of broadening. At $s=4$, NN2, NN3, BAR3, GP2, GP3, and CV2 retain the profile far better than NN1. In the stronger-deformation comparison, GP2 and CV2 have the least broadening among NN1, NN1+GC, BAR2, GP2, and CV2.

The controlled restarts distinguish the contributions of the two BDF2 histories. Even with an exact latest history, an older-history discrepancy changes the first lift response, opposing the response to the same discrepancy in the latest history. The measured response ratios agree with the standard BDF2 coefficients to within the superposition error. The common-trial residual test confirms Proposition~\ref{prop:history-forcing} for every transfer, including the slip-wall rows, where the transferred histories already satisfy the slip condition.

The two Euler realizations both show slower initial drag accumulation with GP2 and CV2 than with NN2. The RANS application tests the criteria under turbulence transport and viscous loads. At the transfer level the comparison is resolved: GP2 preserves the monitored mean-flow and turbulence inventories at transfer more closely than NN2, and CV2 holds them near roundoff. At the output level it is not: the paired load difference between methods is smaller than either remeshing response, and its time-step sensitivity prevents an aerodynamic ordering at the tested settings; SST also remains incompletely converged.

The results suggest four requirements for assessing a multistep restart:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Measure discrepancies at every history level used by the time integrator (Sec.~\ref{sec:results-bdf2-new}).
\item Verify the history-forcing identity at a common trial state, with the receiving solver's boundary treatment applied to both the predicted and the measured residual forcing and boundary rows assessed separately (Sec.~\ref{sec:results-bdf2-new} and \ref{app:history-checks}).
\item Pair integral diagnostics with a measure of transition shape; a corrected total does not restore lost spatial structure (Table~\ref{tab:riemann-stress-new}).
\item Resolve the paired method difference under time-step and inner-iteration refinement before assigning an aerodynamic advantage; similar individual RMS amplitudes are insufficient (Table~\ref{tab:rans-temporal-main} and \ref{app:rans-controls}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Representation and reference-state identities}
\label{app:quadrature-mismatch}

GP3 reconstructs the source value at the midpoint of edge $(i,j)$ as
\begin{equation}
 \phi_{ij}=\tfrac12(\phi_i+\phi_j)
       -\tfrac18d_{ij}^T\overline H_{ij}d_{ij},
 \qquad d_{ij}=x_i-x_j,\qquad \overline H_{ij}=\tfrac12(H_i+H_j).
 \label{eq:gp3-edge-value}
\end{equation}
For a quadratic field, the curvature term equals the difference between the endpoint average and midpoint value. The complete quadratic projection can thus be exact while vertex restriction changes the solver integral. For example, $f(x)=x^2$ on a uniform partition of $[0,1]$ belongs to the quadratic finite-element space, but integrating only its vertex values with linear nodal weights gives
\begin{equation}
 Q_h(f)=\frac13+\frac{h^2}{6}.
 \label{eq:vertex-restriction-counterexample}
\end{equation}
This second-order discrepancy comes entirely from vertex restriction; the complete projection remains exact.

\subsection{Discrepancy relative to a native target state}
\label{app:reference-decomposition}

Let $\Pi_Mu^k$ represent a common exact field in the solver representation, with native error $e_M^k=U_M^k-\Pi_Mu^k$. For the complete, possibly nonlinear, transfer map,
\begin{align}
 \eps^k={}&\underbrace{\calI_{AB}(\Pi_Au^k)-\Pi_Bu^k}_{\text{transfer consistency}}\notag\\
 &+\underbrace{\calI_{AB}(U_A^k)-\calI_{AB}(\Pi_Au^k)-e_B^k}_{\text{source and target discretization contributions}}.
 \label{eq:defect-decomposition}
\end{align}
Only a linear map permits the second line to be written $\calI_{AB}e_A^k-e_B^k$. A load difference likewise separates as
\begin{align}
 G_B(\widehat U_B^k)-G_A(U_A^k)
 ={}&G_B(U_B^k)-G_A(U_A^k)\notag\\
 &+G_B(\widehat U_B^k)-G_B(U_B^k).
 \label{eq:observable-decomposition}
\end{align}
The first difference is native mesh sensitivity; the second is the target-referenced restart response. Neither alone is an independently known continuum error.

\subsection{Cancellation of the initial history forcing}

For an imposed target-history discrepancy $e$, setting $\eps^n=e/4$ and $\eps^{n-1}=e$ makes $\eta^n$ zero. At a common trial state, the two BDF2 equations are identical; an exact solve on the same local solution branch should return the same next state. The measurements distinguish force agreement from state agreement (Table~\ref{tab:naca-cancellation-app}). First-step lift differences are of order $10^{-9}$, whereas relative state differences are approximately $2\times10^{-4}$ in the Euclidean norm and 0.043--0.090 in the volume-weighted norm. Neither norm has an independently measured iterative floor, and the small lift difference leaves a finite state discrepancy.
