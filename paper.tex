\shorttitle{Mesh-to-Mesh Transfer Errors in Multistep Unsteady Flow Simulations}

\title [mode = title]{Mesh-to-Mesh Transfer Errors in Multistep Unsteady Flow Simulations}
\input{submission/author_details.tex}

\begin{abstract}
When an unsteady flow simulation changes its mesh, transferring the solution can introduce errors that affect later flow evolution and aerodynamic loads. Multistep time integrators also require solutions from earlier times, so remeshing must transfer several stored states. Transfer is often assessed by field accuracy, the absence of new extrema, and preservation of domain integrals. These measures do not, however, directly describe how the restarted calculation will respond. This study asks how errors in the latest and older stored states influence that response, and what must be checked to assess transfer reliably.

We compare nearest-neighbor Taylor interpolation, barycentric interpolation, Galerkin projection, and control-volume transfer using static tests, repeated remeshing, and airfoil calculations. The second-order backward differentiation formula (BDF2) uses both stored states with fixed coefficients, so an error in the older state changes the next step even when the latest state matches the reference. In controlled airfoil restarts, transferring only the older field changes lift in the opposite direction and by about one third of the change measured when both fields are transferred. Repeated remeshing of a Riemann problem shows that restoring total mass does not recover a smeared wave profile, and that preserving mass at each transfer does not prevent later mass drift. Corrections that redistribute mass across the domain disturb the far field, and the drift is consistent with altered open-boundary exchange. In a viscous airfoil test with the Spalart--Allmaras model, the root-mean-square drag departures from the fixed-mesh reference differ by less than 2\% between methods, and halving the physical time step changes the difference between two drag histories by more than its own magnitude, so the loads do not rank the methods. Assessing a transfer therefore requires checking every stored time level, conservation during the flow steps that follow it, wave profiles as well as integrals, and the time-step and iterative convergence of any load comparison.
\end{abstract}

\begin{highlights}
\item The older BDF2 field affects lift even when the latest matches the reference.

\item Global mass correction does not restore a smeared wave profile.

\end{highlights}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Mesh adaptation lets an unsteady flow calculation change its spatial resolution as the flow evolves, but every mesh change requires transferring the solution before time integration can continue. The transfer adds a source of numerical error to those of spatial discretization and time integration, and repeated transfers can accumulate it~\cite{AlauzetLoseilleOlivier2018,SauvageAlauzetDervieux2024,LevyMay2025}. For an aerodynamic calculation, what matters in practice is how this error affects the subsequent flow and the predicted forces.

Transfer quality is commonly described by three properties. Accuracy measures how closely the transferred field represents the source field, and the approximation order describes how that error decreases under mesh refinement for smooth data. Boundedness requires that transfer create no values outside the source range. Conservation requires that selected integrals or moments be preserved. Several families of methods address these properties. Common-refinement and Galerkin methods provide accurate conservative transfer between nonmatching meshes~\cite{JiaoHeath2004,FarrellMaddison2011}, and constrained formulations can also preserve norms and weak incompressibility~\cite{MaddisonHiester2017}. Conservative remapping integrates a reconstructed source field over target cells~\cite{DukowiczKodis1987,MargolinShashkov2003,AlauzetMehrenberger2010,Alauzet2016}, and conservative Galerkin transfer has been used in high-order implicit adaptation with bounds near discontinuities~\cite{LevyMay2024,LevyMay2025}.

A transfer can satisfy all three properties and still alter the subsequent flow. Preserving total mass does not determine where that mass is located, and suppressing new extrema can modify both accuracy and conservation. The subsequent flow also depends on the spatial discretization, the boundary conditions, and the time integrator. Shallow-water studies show, for example, that preserving a discrete equilibrium can suppress disturbances introduced by transfer~\cite{MaddisonCotterFarrell2011,MaddisonCotterFarrell2013}. This link between transfer and subsequent evolution motivates examining the complete restart, rather than only the field immediately after transfer.

Multistep time integration sharpens the distinction. A multistep scheme advances the latest solution using solutions stored from earlier times, which we call history states. After remeshing, these states have been transferred from another mesh instead of being computed by earlier steps on the target mesh. An error in an older state can therefore change the next time-step equation even when the latest state agrees with a target-mesh reference. Limiting, positivity corrections, and the reapplication of boundary conditions can change the stored states further.

This paper asks whether the conventional measures of transfer quality suffice to assess the subsequent unsteady flow and the aerodynamic loads. Specifically, we ask how errors in the latest and older history states contribute to the response after a restart, and which additional checks are needed before transfer methods can be compared. We therefore examine both the changes introduced during transfer and the flow computed afterwards. Controlled restarts separate the effects of errors in different history states. Repeated remeshing shows how transfer and flow evolution interact over many events. Time-step and iterative-convergence checks establish whether observed load differences can support a ranking of the methods.

We compare nearest-neighbor Taylor interpolation (NN), barycentric interpolation (BAR), continuous Galerkin projection (GP), and conservative control-volume transfer (CV) in a vertex-centered finite-volume solver. The unsteady calculations use the second-order backward differentiation formula (BDF2). The analysis connects the BDF2 coefficients of the stored states to the linear response of the next state and of an aerodynamic load. The CV transfer is formulated around the solver's vertex sum: its reconstructed field has the same domain integral, so the raw transfer preserves the quantity the solver computes. The numerical tests then examine why conservation across a transfer does not imply conservation during the flow steps that follow it. We repeat selected transfers with a global mass correction to measure its effect on wave shape.

The tests progress from static smooth and discontinuous fields to repeated remeshing of a Riemann problem, controlled airfoil restarts, and airfoil calculations with repeated remeshing. Unsteady Reynolds-averaged Navier--Stokes (RANS) calculations on a stationary airfoil with the Spalart--Allmaras (SA) model extend the assessment to turbulence transport and viscous loads. All tests use prescribed remeshing on fixed physical domains and independently computed reference solutions; the choice of an adaptive mesh is outside their scope. Section~\ref{sec:numerical-framework} describes the transfer methods, Section~\ref{sec:restart-consistency} connects history-state errors to the restart response, and Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the tests.

\section{Transferring a solution between meshes}
\label{sec:numerical-framework}

The four methods differ in how they compute values on the target mesh: NN and BAR interpolate from nearby source values, whereas GP and CV solve coupled systems built from integrals. Each method can then apply limiting, boundary conditions, and positivity corrections, and we distinguish the raw field from the corrected field passed to the flow solver. Method suffixes give the nominal order of accuracy, not the polynomial degree: BAR2 is second order, and GP2 and CV2 use piecewise-linear fields. For CV2 the order is nominal in a stronger sense: it is second order on triangles and parallelograms and first order on quadrilaterals that stay distorted under refinement (Section~\ref{sec:cv-transfer}).

\subsection{Stored variables and reconstructed derivatives}
\label{sec:derivative-reconstruction}

The source mesh $A$ and the target mesh $B$ cover the same discretized domain $\Omega$ in $d$ space dimensions. A mesh $M$ has $n_M$ solver vertices; vertex $i$ has position $\boldsymbol x_i^M$ and a median-dual control volume $C_i^M$ of positive measure $w_i^M=|C_i^M|$. The solver state $U_M$ stacks $n_U$ transported variables per vertex. For one scalar component $\phi$, the vector $\phi_M=(\phi_{M,1},\ldots,\phi_{M,n_M})$ collects the vertex values, and the solver computes its integral as
\begin{equation}
 Q_M(\phi_M)=\sum_{i=1}^{n_M}w_i^M\phi_{M,i}.
 \label{eq:fv-discrete-integral}
\end{equation}
For the full state, $Q_M(U_M)$ denotes the vector of these integrals, one per transported component. The numerical tests measure the change of an integral across transfer by the signed relative change $e_Q(\phi_A,\phi_B)=[Q_B(\phi_B)-Q_A(\phi_A)]/Q_A(\phi_A)$, defined for a nonzero source integral.

The mean-flow variables are density $\rho$, momentum density $\rho\boldsymbol u$, and total energy per unit volume $\rho E$, where $\boldsymbol u$ is the velocity, with Cartesian components $(u,v)$ in two dimensions, and $E$ is the total energy per unit mass. All transfer methods return values at the solver vertices.

The higher-order methods also need gradients and Hessians, which weighted least squares reconstructs from neighboring source values:
\begin{equation}
 \phi_j-\phi_i\simeq \boldsymbol g_i\cdot\boldsymbol r_{ij}
              +\tfrac12\boldsymbol r_{ij}^{T}\mathcal H_i\boldsymbol r_{ij},
 \qquad \boldsymbol r_{ij}=\boldsymbol x_j-\boldsymbol x_i.
 \label{eq:wls-polynomial}
\end{equation}
Here $\boldsymbol r_{ij}$ joins source vertices $i$ and $j$, and $\boldsymbol g_i$ and $\mathcal H_i$ approximate the gradient and Hessian of $\phi$ at $i$; a linear fit omits the Hessian. For smooth data on a well-conditioned, full-rank scaled stencil with characteristic local mesh spacing $h$, the linear fit has an $O(h)$ gradient error, and the quadratic fit has $O(h^2)$ gradient and $O(h)$ Hessian errors. Anisotropy, weighting, and poor conditioning can reduce this accuracy~\cite{BarthFrederickson1990,Barth1993,Mavriplis2003,DiskinThomas2008}. The third-order methods use the quadratic fit, and their achieved order is checked numerically.

\subsection{Nearest-neighbor and barycentric interpolation}
\label{sec:point-transfer}
\label{sec:barycentric-transfer}

We write $\calI_{AB}$ with a method label for the raw interpolation, before limiting and state corrections. Let $i_{\mathrm{NN}}(j)$ be the source vertex nearest to the target point $\boldsymbol x_j^B$, and let $\boldsymbol r_j=\boldsymbol x_j^B-\boldsymbol x_{i_{\mathrm{NN}}(j)}^A$ be the displacement between them. Nearest-neighbor Taylor transfer is
\begin{align}
 (\calI^{\mathrm{NN1}}_{AB}\phi_A)_j
 &=\phi_{A,i_{\mathrm{NN}}(j)},\label{eq:nn1}\\
 (\calI^{\mathrm{NN2}}_{AB}\phi_A)_j
 &=\phi_{A,i_{\mathrm{NN}}(j)}
       +\boldsymbol g_{i_{\mathrm{NN}}(j)}\cdot\boldsymbol r_j,\label{eq:nn2}\\
 (\calI^{\mathrm{NN3}}_{AB}\phi_A)_j
 &=\phi_{A,i_{\mathrm{NN}}(j)}
       +\boldsymbol g_{i_{\mathrm{NN}}(j)}\cdot\boldsymbol r_j
       +\tfrac12\boldsymbol r_j^T\mathcal H_{i_{\mathrm{NN}}(j)}\boldsymbol r_j.
 \label{eq:nn3}
\end{align}
NN1 copies the nearest source value, NN2 adds the local slope, and NN3 adds the curvature. For $\|\boldsymbol r_j\|_2=O(h)$, where $\|\cdot\|_2$ is the Euclidean norm, and sufficiently accurate derivatives, the smooth-field errors are $O(h)$, $O(h^2)$, and $O(h^3)$. Because the nearest source vertex changes abruptly with the target position, the interpolated value can be discontinuous.

Barycentric interpolation uses the source simplex (triangle or tetrahedron) that contains the target point; other element types are first subdivided into simplices without adding vertices. In dimension $d$, let the containing simplex have vertices $\boldsymbol x_{i_r}^A$, $r=1,\ldots,d+1$. The barycentric coordinates $\lambda_r$ of the target point are the nonnegative weights that satisfy
\begin{equation}
 \boldsymbol x_j^B=\sum_{r=1}^{d+1}\lambda_r \boldsymbol x_{i_r}^A,
 \qquad \sum_r\lambda_r=1,
 \qquad \lambda_r\geq0.
 \label{eq:barycentric-coordinates}
\end{equation}
They define BAR2 and its gradient-corrected extension BAR3:
\begin{align}
 (\calI^{\mathrm{BAR2}}_{AB}\phi_A)_j
 &=\sum_r\lambda_r\phi_{A,i_r},\label{eq:bar2}\\
 (\calI^{\mathrm{BAR3}}_{AB}\phi_A)_j
 &=\sum_r\lambda_r\left[\phi_{A,i_r}
       +\tfrac12\boldsymbol g_{i_r}\cdot(\boldsymbol x_j^B-\boldsymbol x_{i_r}^A)\right].\label{eq:bar3}
\end{align}
Because its weights are nonnegative and sum to one, BAR2 reproduces affine fields (a constant plus a linear function) and creates no maximum or minimum beyond the vertex values of the containing simplex. BAR3 adds half of each vertex gradient correction, which makes it third-order consistent, as shown next. Values and gradients are shared between neighboring simplices, so both interpolants are continuous across conforming simplex interfaces. Neither method constrains the source-to-target integral.

The factor of one half follows from a Taylor expansion. Let $q$ be a quadratic field with constant Hessian $\mathcal H_q$, let $\boldsymbol x=\boldsymbol x_j^B$, and set $\boldsymbol d_r=\boldsymbol x-\boldsymbol x_{i_r}^A$. Then
\begin{align*}
q(\boldsymbol x_{i_r}^A)&=q(\boldsymbol x)-\nabla q(\boldsymbol x)\cdot\boldsymbol d_r+\tfrac12\boldsymbol d_r^T\mathcal H_q\boldsymbol d_r,\\
\nabla q(\boldsymbol x_{i_r}^A)&=\nabla q(\boldsymbol x)-\mathcal H_q\boldsymbol d_r.
\end{align*}
Substituting these expressions into Eq.~\eqref{eq:bar3} gives $q(\boldsymbol x)-\tfrac12\nabla q(\boldsymbol x)\cdot\sum_r\lambda_r\boldsymbol d_r=q(\boldsymbol x)$, because $\sum_r\lambda_r\boldsymbol d_r=0$ by Eq.~\eqref{eq:barycentric-coordinates}. BAR3 is therefore exact for quadratic fields with exact vertex gradients. For a smooth field on a shape-regular simplex, the Taylor remainder is $O(h^3)$, and an $O(h^2)$ gradient error multiplied by the $O(h)$ displacement contributes at the same order.

\subsection{Galerkin projection and cross-mesh integration}
\label{sec:galerkin-projection}
\label{sec:simplicial-representation}

Galerkin projection chooses the target field closest to the reconstructed source field in the $L_2$ norm~\cite{FarrellMaddison2011,JiaoHeath2004}. The transfer uses finite-element basis functions, although the flow equations here remain finite volume, and it solves for all target values together instead of interpolating each vertex independently. Let $\phi_A^h$ be the piecewise-polynomial field built from the source vertex values and, for GP3, their reconstructed derivatives. Let $V_B^\ell$ be the space of continuous Lagrange functions of polynomial degree $\ell$ on the target simplicial mesh, and write $P^\ell$ for polynomials of total degree at most $\ell$. With the inner product $(f_1,f_2)_{L_2(\Omega)}=\int_\Omega f_1f_2\dd x$, the target field $\phi_B^h\in V_B^\ell$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
 (\phi_B^h-\phi_A^h,\xi_B)_{L_2(\Omega)}=0
 \quad\text{for every }\xi_B\in V_B^\ell.
 \label{eq:galerkin-orthogonality}
\end{equation}
The transfer error is thus orthogonal to every target test function, which makes $\phi_B^h$ the best $L_2$ approximation of $\phi_A^h$ in $V_B^\ell$~\cite{FarrellMaddison2011,JiaoHeath2004}. Expanding $\phi_B^h=\sum_jz_{B,j}N_j^B$ in the nodal basis functions $N_j^B$ of $V_B^\ell$ and taking $\xi_B=N_i^B$ gives the linear system
\begin{equation}
 H_Bz_B=b,\qquad
 (H_B)_{ij}=\int_\Omega N_i^BN_j^B\dd x,
 \qquad b_i=\int_\Omega N_i^B\phi_A^h\dd x.
 \label{eq:galerkin-system}
\end{equation}
The consistent Galerkin mass matrix $H_B$ is sparse and positive definite. It couples the basis functions that share a target element. The vector $b$ is the cross-mesh load vector. For GP2, the source field $\phi_A^h$ on each source triangle is the barycentric interpolation of its three vertex values. The target basis function $N_i^B$ is the hat function of target vertex $i$: it equals one at that vertex and decreases linearly to zero on the opposite edge of each triangle that contains it.

The load vector integrates the product of two fields that are piecewise polynomial on different meshes. A target element generally overlaps several source elements, across which the source polynomial changes, so the integral is assembled over the element intersections:
\begin{equation}
 b_i=\sum_{K_B\ni i}\sum_{K_A:\,|K_A\cap K_B|>0}
      \int_{K_A\cap K_B}N_i^B\phi_A^h\dd x.
 \label{eq:galerkin-load-intersections}
\end{equation}
Here $K_A$ is a source simplex, $K_B$ is a target simplex incident to $i$, and $|K_A\cap K_B|$ is the measure of their intersection. The intersections form a common refinement of the two meshes (Figure~\ref{fig:transfer-geometry}a) and must cover the domain without gaps or overlap. On each intersection both fields are single polynomials, so quadrature is exact: degree two suffices for $P^1$/$P^1$ transfer and degree four for $P^2$/$P^2$ transfer on affine simplices~\cite{Dunavant1985,Keast1986}. This geometric integration and the coupled solve distinguish GP from NN and BAR, which evaluate a source reconstruction at each target vertex independently.

\subsection{Conservative control-volume transfer}
\label{sec:cv-transfer}

The CV method matches the integrals of the source and target fields over each target control volume. This requires a field that can be integrated over parts of cells. The domain integral of that field must also equal the solver total, which is the sum of the vertex values multiplied by their control-volume measures. We construct such a field as follows. Both meshes represent the field as $\phi_M^h=\sum_k\phi_{M,k}\psi_k^M$. On triangles and tetrahedra, $\psi_k^M=N_k^M$ is the usual $P^1$ hat function. A quadrilateral is divided into triangles by joining its vertices to their average center (Figure~\ref{fig:transfer-geometry}b), and the value at that center is a weighted combination of the vertex values, not an independent unknown. Nonsimplicial three-dimensional elements use analogous centered subdivisions. The weights are chosen so that
\begin{equation}
 \int_K\psi_k^M\dd x=|C_k^M\cap K|
 \qquad\text{for every element $K$ and vertex $k$ of $M$,}
 \label{eq:cv-lumped-exact}
\end{equation}
where $C_k^M\cap K$ is the part of the control volume of vertex $k$ inside element $K$. For a quadrilateral, this gives vertex $k$ the center weight $|C_k^M\cap K|/|K|$. Multiplying Eq.~\eqref{eq:cv-lumped-exact} by the stored value $\phi_{M,k}$ and summing over the vertices of $K$ gives $\int_K\phi_M^h\dd x=\sum_{k\in K}|C_k^M\cap K|\phi_{M,k}$, and summing over all elements gives exactly the solver total $Q_M(\phi_M)$. The reconstructed field and the solver therefore have the same domain integral.

On parallelograms, the center weights give the arithmetic vertex average and reproduce affine fields. On any other quadrilateral they do not: the center value of an affine field is then in error by an amount proportional to the element size times the field gradient. The weights enforce Eq.~\eqref{eq:cv-lumped-exact} at the expense of affine reproduction.

The $n_B$ target vertex values are defined by requiring that the reconstructed target and source fields have the same integral over each target control volume:
\begin{equation}
 \int_{C_j^B}\left(\phi_B^h-\phi_A^h\right)\dd x=0,
 \qquad j=1,\ldots,n_B.
 \label{eq:cv-condition}
\end{equation}
A control volume usually overlaps several source elements, which supply its source contributions.

Conservative remapping can instead match integrals over whole target elements. The intersection-based $P^1$ methods of Alauzet and Mehrenberger~\cite{AlauzetMehrenberger2010} and Alauzet~\cite{Alauzet2016} take that route: they limit an element-wise linear reconstruction and recover vertex values by volume-weighted averaging over the incident elements. Because the flow solver here stores values at vertices and forms its total with median-dual weights, the CV construction uses the target control volumes directly. Each integral in Eq.~\eqref{eq:cv-condition} involves several target vertex values, which gives the coupled system
\begin{equation}
 \widetilde H_B\phi_B=\tilde b,\qquad
 (\widetilde H_B)_{jk}=\int_{C_j^B}\psi_k^B\dd x,
 \qquad \tilde b_j=\int_{C_j^B}\phi_A^h\dd x.
 \label{eq:cv-system}
\end{equation}
The functions $\psi_k^B$ sum to one, so the row sums are $w_j^B$, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:cv-lumped-exact} gives column sums $w_k^B$. The entries are nonnegative, depend only on the target geometry, are evaluated in closed form, and couple the same elements as $H_B$. On triangles, strictly convex quadrilaterals, and tetrahedra the matrix is symmetric and strictly diagonally dominant, and hence positive definite: vertex permutations exchange the median-dual pieces of a simplex, and on a quadrilateral each center weight is proportional to the corresponding median-dual piece. Three-dimensional mixed cells can have different matrix properties; the comparisons here are two-dimensional.

Like the Galerkin load vector, the right-hand side is assembled from intersections, here of source subelements with the median-dual pieces of the target elements:
\begin{equation}
 \tilde b_j=\sum_{K_B\ni j}\ \sum_{K_A:\,|K_A\cap K_B|>0}
      \int_{K_A\cap K_B\cap C_j^B}\phi_A^h\dd x,
 \label{eq:cv-load-intersections}
\end{equation}
where $K_B$ ranges over the target elements incident to $j$ and $K_A$ over the source subelements. The source mesh must cover every part of $C_j^B$, as it does in the numerical tests, where both meshes cover the same polygonal domain.

Summing Eq.~\eqref{eq:cv-condition} over the target control volumes, which tile $\Omega$, and using Eq.~\eqref{eq:cv-lumped-exact} gives
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 Q_B(\phi_B)&=\sum_{j=1}^{n_B}\int_{C_j^B}\phi_B^h\dd x
 =\sum_{j=1}^{n_B}\tilde b_j\\
 &=\int_\Omega\phi_A^h\dd x=Q_A(\phi_A)
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:cv-global}
\end{equation}
to within integration and solve tolerances. Equation~\eqref{eq:cv-condition} concerns the integral of the reconstructed field over each target control volume, $(\widetilde H_B\phi_B)_j$. This is not the product $w_j^B\phi_{B,j}$ that the solver attributes to vertex $j$, because integrating a continuous field over a control volume generally differs from multiplying that volume by the value at its vertex. The two quantities have the same sum over the domain but differ vertex by vertex. Clipping vertex values and redistributing the resulting change can preserve the domain sum while changing the individual integrals in Eq.~\eqref{eq:cv-condition}.

If $B=A$, the system has $\tilde b=\widetilde H_A\phi_A$ and recovers the source vertex values. If both reconstructions reproduce affine fields, uniqueness also makes transfer exact for those fields. This gives second-order consistency on triangles and parallelograms. For quadrilaterals that approach parallelograms under refinement, second-order consistency additionally requires the center-value error to decrease as $O(h^2)$. Where quadrilateral distortion does not vanish as the cells become smaller, the center-value error above makes the transfer first order.

\subsection{Corrections applied to the transferred state}
\label{sec:global-correction}
\label{sec:galerkin-limiter}
The methods above determine target vertex values, but those values must also be suitable for restarting the flow solver. Near a discontinuity, a reconstruction can create new extrema. Bounded values can still change a conserved integral or fail to satisfy the boundary conditions, and for compressible flow the conserved variables must give positive density and pressure. We therefore treat limiting, integral correction, boundary treatment, and a positivity fix as parts of the complete transfer. These operations interact: clipping or replacing boundary values changes an integral, and restoring that integral modifies local values. Their combined effect must therefore be assessed on the final state supplied to the solver.

NN and BAR control new extrema with the Barth--Jespersen (BJ) limiter~\cite{BarthJespersen1989}. At each source vertex $i$ it supplies a factor $0\leq\theta_i\leq1$ that reduces the linear reconstruction increment wherever the predicted values would leave the range of the neighboring source values. The same factor multiplies the Taylor correction in NN2 and NN3 and the gradient correction in BAR3, so $\theta_i=1$ retains the full correction and $\theta_i=0$ gives NN1 or BAR2. The smooth-field tests use $\theta_i=1$. Because the factor is computed from the linear slope, it does not bound the quadratic term of NN3, and extrema are therefore measured on the final transferred field.

GP and CV are limited after the solve. The bounds at each target vertex are the extrema of the overlapping source vertices. Hard limiting clips to these bounds; with relaxation, they are first widened by $\tau$ times the source range, where $\tau$ is a dimensionless tolerance. The change introduced by clipping is redistributed so as to preserve the lumped total on the simplicial mesh for GP, or $Q_B$ for CV, which can alter Galerkin orthogonality or the individual CV integrals. CV2 denotes the limited transfer and CV2-u the unlimited one. In the Euler and RANS simulations, standard GP2 and CV2 use $\tau=2\times10^{-3}$.

Bounding a field does not restore an integral that transfer has changed. Bounded global correction (GC) can enforce the integral in Eq.~\eqref{eq:fv-discrete-integral} for any base transfer. It lets us test whether restoring the domain integral improves a transfer that otherwise changes it. Paired NN and BAR tests compare the corrected and uncorrected results. Given an uncorrected target field $\phi_B^{\mathrm{pre}}$, GC adds a correction $\chi=(\chi_1,\ldots,\chi_{n_B})$ that satisfies
\begin{equation}
 \sum_jw_j^B\chi_j=Q_A(\phi_A)-Q_B(\phi_B^{\mathrm{pre}}),
 \qquad \phi_j^{\min}\leq\phi_{B,j}^{\mathrm{pre}}+\chi_j\leq \phi_j^{\max},
 \label{eq:global-correction-constraint}
\end{equation}
where $\phi_j^{\min}$ and $\phi_j^{\max}$ are the lower and upper bounds at target vertex $j$. The corrected field is $\phi_B^{\mathrm{GC}}=\phi_B^{\mathrm{pre}}+\chi$. The correction is distributed among the vertices that can change without leaving their bounds, and vertices already at a bound remain fixed~\cite{ShashkovWendroff2004,KucharikShashkovWendroff2003}. The requested integral must be attainable within the weighted bounds. A small total integral error does not imply that every vertex correction is small.

Integral preservation must also be reconciled with the boundary conditions. In the standard treatment, boundary values are interpolated from the source solution on the matching physical boundary; this replaces the unconstrained transfer at those vertices and can change the local GP or CV identities. The flow tests keep every boundary vertex, so the transferred wall state already satisfies the target wall condition. A changed surface discretization would add a mismatch that is not examined here.

Finally, the conserved variables must form a physical gas state. For the ideal gas used here, the pressure is $p=(\gamma-1)[\rho E-|\rho\boldsymbol u|^2/(2\rho)]$, with heat-capacity ratio $\gamma>1$. Positive density and positive internal energy are therefore coupled requirements: separate bounds on the conserved components do not guarantee positive pressure.

These requirements determine the final treatment before the solver restarts. The standard GP, CV, and globally corrected transfers apply boundary values and scalar lower bounds, and then redistribute the resulting integral change. A positivity fix is applied to any remaining state with nonpositive density or pressure, while preserving the totals of the affected patch. The final state can consequently differ from the solution of the original GP or CV equations. We therefore measure conservation and bounds on the state passed to the flow solver. Global redistribution can reach any vertex with room inside its relaxed bounds, including vertices in a uniform far field; Section~\ref{sec:results-local-conservation} measures the consequence.

\section{Effect of remeshing on multistep time integration}
\label{sec:restart-consistency}

BDF2 uses two stored solutions. If either changes during transfer, the next time-step equation changes. To quantify this change, we compare two calculations on the same target mesh $B$. The \emph{reference run} continues from states computed on $B$. The \emph{restarted run} continues from states transferred from the source mesh $A$. Both runs use the same spatial discretization, so they differ only through their stored states. At each physical step, the implicit BDF2 equation is solved iteratively while the stored states are held fixed; the calculations in this paper use dual-time stepping, whose pseudo-time iterations are called inner iterations below. An iterative solve stops at a finite tolerance, and the analysis keeps the residual that it leaves.

\subsection{Time discretization and stored-state errors}
\label{sec:unsteady-discretization}
\label{sec:native-transfer-decomposition}

Let $V_M=\operatorname{diag}(w_i^M)\otimes I_{n_U}$ be the diagonal matrix of control volumes, where $I_{n_U}$ is the identity on the state components and $\otimes$ denotes the Kronecker product: each vertex weight is repeated for its $n_U$ components. This finite-volume matrix differs from the coupled Galerkin matrix $H_B$. Between remeshing events, the semi-discrete equations are
\begin{equation}
 V_M\dot U_M+F_M(U_M,t)=0.
 \label{eq:semidiscrete}
\end{equation}
Here $t$ is physical time, $\dot U_M=\mathrm dU_M/\mathrm dt$, and $F_M$ is the assembled spatial residual, including fluxes, source terms, and boundary contributions. The state includes the RANS turbulence variables when they are advanced in time.

At constant time step, BDF2 determines the new state $U_B^{n+1}$ on the target mesh from
\begin{equation}
 \frac{V_B}{\Delta t}\left(\tfrac32U_B^{n+1}-2U_B^n+\tfrac12U_B^{n-1}\right)
   +F_B(U_B^{n+1},t^{n+1})=0.
 \label{eq:bdf2}
\end{equation}
We denote the left-hand side by $\Phi_B(U_B^{n+1};U_B^n,U_B^{n-1})$ and call it the BDF2 residual. Its first argument is the new state. The other two are the stored states, which are fixed during the step. Here $n$ is the physical time-step index, $t^n$ the corresponding time, and $\Delta t=t^n-t^{n-1}>0$ the constant step size. Each mesh is stationary during a time step. Continuously moving meshes would require compatible geometric histories and a discrete geometric conservation law~\cite{KoobusFarhat1999,GeuzaineGrandmontFarhat2003}; Eq.~\eqref{eq:bdf2} does not cover that case.

Let $U_A^k$ and $U_B^k$ be the reference solutions computed on meshes $A$ and $B$ for the same problem at time $t^k$. A hat identifies the restarted run. At each time level $k$, the difference between the two runs on $B$ is
\begin{equation}
\eps^k=\widehat U_B^k-U_B^k.
 \label{eq:history-defects}
\end{equation}
At the two stored times $k=n-1,n$, the restarted run receives $\widehat U_B^k=\mathcal T_{AB}(U_A^k)$. The complete map $\mathcal T_{AB}$ includes raw transfer, limiting, boundary treatment, and positivity corrections. The source solution was computed on another mesh. The stored-state error $\eps^k$ therefore contains the difference between the two discrete solutions as well as the transfer error. Static analytic-field tests measure transfer accuracy alone, without solving the flow equations.

\subsection{Response of the next time step to stored-state errors}
\label{sec:state-response}

Both runs solve Eq.~\eqref{eq:bdf2} iteratively, each with its own stored states. When the iterations stop, the computed new states leave the residuals
\[
 \mathfrak r_B^{n+1}=\Phi_B(U_B^{n+1};U_B^n,U_B^{n-1}),\qquad
 \widehat{\mathfrak r}_B^{n+1}=\Phi_B(\widehat U_B^{n+1};\widehat U_B^n,\widehat U_B^{n-1})
\]
in the reference run and in the restarted run, respectively. Both residuals are zero when the equations are solved exactly. Subtracting the first definition from the second gives, without approximation,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 &\frac{3V_B}{2\Delta t}\eps^{n+1}
   +F_B(\widehat U_B^{n+1},t^{n+1})-F_B(U_B^{n+1},t^{n+1})
 =\frac{V_B}{\Delta t}\eta^n
   +\widehat{\mathfrak r}_B^{n+1}-\mathfrak r_B^{n+1},\\
 &\eta^n=2\eps^n-\tfrac12\eps^{n-1}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:nonlinear-bdf2-response}
\end{equation}
The stored states appear only in the time-derivative term of Eq.~\eqref{eq:bdf2}, with coefficients $-2$ and $1/2$. They do not appear in $F_B$, which depends on the new state alone. The two stored-state errors therefore enter Eq.~\eqref{eq:nonlinear-bdf2-response} only through the weighted history error $\eta^n$, and the difference of the two spatial residuals stays on the left-hand side. When both equations are solved exactly, $\mathfrak r_B^{n+1}=\widehat{\mathfrak r}_B^{n+1}=0$, and the history term $V_B\eta^n/\Delta t$ is balanced by the change in the new state and in its spatial residual.

\paragraph{Linear response.}
Let $J_B=\partial_U F_B(U_B^{n+1},t^{n+1})$ be the spatial Jacobian at the reference new state, where $\partial_U$ denotes differentiation with respect to the state vector. Define the remainder $\mathcal N_B$ as the part of the spatial-residual difference that is not linear in $\eps^{n+1}$:
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal N_B(\eps^{n+1})
 =F_B(\widehat U_B^{n+1},t^{n+1})-F_B(U_B^{n+1},t^{n+1})-J_B\eps^{n+1}.
\label{eq:spatial-remainder}
\end{equation}
This is a definition and involves no approximation. Appendix~\ref{app:remainders} derives an integral expression for $\mathcal N_B$ and a bound on its size. Substituting Eq.~\eqref{eq:spatial-remainder} into Eq.~\eqref{eq:nonlinear-bdf2-response} and multiplying by $\Delta t$ gives
\begin{equation}
 A_B\eps^{n+1}
 =V_B\eta^n+\Delta t\bigl(\widehat{\mathfrak r}_B^{n+1}-\mathfrak r_B^{n+1}\bigr)
                 -\Delta t\,\mathcal N_B(\eps^{n+1}),
 \qquad A_B=\tfrac32V_B+\Delta t J_B.
 \label{eq:linearized-bdf2-response}
\end{equation}
The matrix $A_B$ is the Jacobian of the BDF2 residual with respect to the new state, multiplied by $\Delta t$. If $F_B$ is continuously differentiable and $A_B$ is invertible, the implicit function theorem~\cite{OrtegaRheinboldt2000} gives a restarted solution that tends to the reference as $\eta^n$ tends to zero. If the Jacobian of $F_B$ is also Lipschitz continuous near the reference, the remainder is quadratic (Appendix~\ref{app:remainders}), and for exact solves
\begin{equation}
 \eps^{n+1}=R_B\eta^n+O(\Delta t\|\eta^n\|_2^2),
 \qquad R_B=A_B^{-1}V_B.
 \label{eq:state-response}
\end{equation}
The response matrix $R_B$ converts the weighted history error into a leading prediction of the next-state difference. Proportional history errors therefore give proportional state differences. Section~\ref{sec:naca-fixed-methodology} uses this property to test the relative effects of the two stored states. When $\|\Delta t V_B^{-1}J_B\|_2$ tends to zero, $R_B$ tends to $\tfrac23I$ and the leading term becomes $\tfrac43\eps^n-\tfrac13\eps^{n-1}$. Otherwise the Jacobian term changes both the size and the spatial distribution of the response.

The Lipschitz condition is an assumption on the spatial discretization, not a consequence of BDF2. Slope limiters and upwind switches are not differentiable where they change branch, so the quadratic estimate can fail there. When the iterative solves stop early, the term $\Delta t A_B^{-1}(\widehat{\mathfrak r}_B^{n+1}-\mathfrak r_B^{n+1})$ must also be retained; Eq.~\eqref{eq:linearized-bdf2-response} contains it.

\subsection{Response of an aerodynamic load}
\label{sec:delayed-response}

This subsection relates the state difference $\eps^{n+1}$ to the difference in an aerodynamic load, such as the lift or drag coefficient. On a fixed airfoil in inviscid flow, the load coefficients are weighted sums of wall pressure, with weights fixed by geometry, force direction, and reference quantities. In viscous flow, wall shear also contributes, through velocity gradients reconstructed near the wall. An error located away from the wall therefore does not appear in these formulas. It changes a load only after the flow equations have transmitted it to the wall, so its effect on the load is delayed with respect to the transfer.

Let $G_B$ be one scalar load evaluated on mesh $B$, and let $\eps_G^{n+1}=G_B(\widehat U_B^{n+1})-G_B(U_B^{n+1})$ be its difference between the two runs. Let $\nabla_U G_B$ be the gradient of the load with respect to the state, evaluated at $U_B^{n+1}$. As in Eq.~\eqref{eq:spatial-remainder}, define the load remainder $\mathcal N_G^{n+1}$ as the part of the load difference that is not linear in $\eps^{n+1}$:
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal N_G^{n+1}=\eps_G^{n+1}-(\nabla_U G_B)^T\eps^{n+1}.
 \label{eq:output-remainder}
\end{equation}
The remainder is quadratic in $\eps^{n+1}$ when the load gradient is Lipschitz continuous (Appendix~\ref{app:remainders}). Substituting Eq.~\eqref{eq:state-response} then gives
\begin{equation}
 \eps_G^{n+1}=(\nabla_U G_B)^TR_B\eta^n+O(\|\eta^n\|_2^2).
 \label{eq:force-response}
\end{equation}
The first-step load difference is thus, to leading order, a weighted sum of the entries of $\eta^n$, with weights given by the row vector $(\nabla_U G_B)^TR_B$. The tests use only the resulting proportionality between $\eta^n$ and $\eps_G^{n+1}$; the weights themselves are not computed.

Equation~\eqref{eq:force-response} also shows why preserving a global integral does not by itself preserve a load. Consider mass at a stored time level $k=n-1$ or $n$. Let $\rho_A^k$ and $\rho_B^k$ be the independently computed reference densities, and $\widehat\rho_B^k$ the transferred density. A mass-preserving transfer satisfies $Q_B(\widehat\rho_B^k)=Q_A(\rho_A^k)$. Subtracting the target-reference mass gives
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{j=1}^{n_B} w_j^B(\widehat\rho_{B,j}^k-\rho_{B,j}^k)
 =Q_A(\rho_A^k)-Q_B(\rho_B^k).
 \label{eq:gc-native-mismatch}
\end{equation}
Preserving the source mass therefore need not give zero mass difference from the target reference. Even when the two reference masses agree, mass can be added at one vertex and removed at another. These changes cancel in the mass sum. Their contributions to a load carry different weights in Eq.~\eqref{eq:force-response}, however, and need not cancel. The tests therefore measure integral changes and load changes separately.

Finally, the first-step load difference need not capture the later response. At the following step the stored-state errors are $\eps^{n+1}$ and $\eps^n$, so the weighted history error is $\eta^{n+1}=2\eps^{n+1}-\eps^n/2$. The newly computed error thus enters the subsequent steps, during which the flow can carry a disturbance toward the wall. Section~\ref{sec:delayed-airfoil-test} follows pressure and lift after one transfer away from the wall to determine how much of the response occurs after the first step.

\subsection{Response of total mass}
\label{sec:mass-response}

Total mass is one quantity whose response is known exactly. Let $\delta^k=Q_B(\widehat\rho_B^k)-Q_B(\rho_B^k)$ be the mass difference between the two runs at time level $k$. Suppose that the spatial discretization is conservative and that the boundary mass flux does not depend on the state, as for slip walls. The density components of $F_B$ then sum over the mesh to the same value for every state. Summing the density components of Eq.~\eqref{eq:nonlinear-bdf2-response} over the mesh, with exact solves, therefore leaves only the time-derivative terms, $\tfrac32\delta^{n+1}=2\delta^n-\tfrac12\delta^{n-1}$, or
\begin{equation}
 \delta^{n+1}=\tfrac43\delta^n-\tfrac13\delta^{n-1}.
 \label{eq:mass-first-step}
\end{equation}

\textcolor{red}{(How can you prove these statements?)}
No linearization is involved. The same relation holds at every later step in which the stored states are not transferred again. It leaves $\delta^{k+1}-\delta^k/3$ unchanged from one step to the next, while $\delta^{k+1}-\delta^k$ decreases by a factor of three per step. The mass difference therefore tends to
\begin{equation}
 \delta^{\infty}=\tfrac32\delta^n-\tfrac12\delta^{n-1}.
 \label{eq:mass-long-run}
\end{equation}
Equal mass errors $\delta$ in both stored states thus shift the later mass by $\delta$, not by $2\delta$. An error in the older state alone shifts it with the opposite sign. The unweighted sum of the two transfer changes can therefore misrepresent the subsequent mass difference. \end{equation}

Pressure and viscous drag contributions are $C_{D,p}$ and $C_{D,v}$, with $C_D=C_{D,p}+C_{D,v}$. The label $Q$ selects a monitored coefficient $C_Q$: lift, total drag, pressure drag, or viscous drag. \begin{equation}
 \Delta C_{Q,m}(t)=C_{Q,m}(t)-C_{Q,\mathrm{ref}}(t),
 \qquad
 \delta C_Q(t)=\Delta C_{Q,\mathrm{NN2}}(t)-\Delta C_{Q,\mathrm{GP2}}(t).
 \label{eq:rans-comparisons}
\end{equation}
Thus $\Delta C_{D,\mathrm{GP2}}$ measures how far the GP2 drag history departs from its fixed-mesh reference, and $\delta C_D$ measures the difference between the NN2 and GP2 drag histories on the same mesh sequence. Remeshed and reference calculations use identical surface quadrature. For any scalar history $q(t)$ sampled at $N_s$ common physical times $t_i$, define $\mathcal R(q)=(N_s^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N_s}q(t_i)^2)^{1/2}$. Write $q_{\mathrm{nom}}$ and $q_{\mathrm{test}}$ for this history at the nominal and modified numerical settings. The sensitivity is
\begin{equation}
 S_{\mathrm{test}}(q)=
 \frac{\mathcal R(q_{\mathrm{test}}-q_{\mathrm{nom}})}
      {\mathcal R(q_{\mathrm{nom}})}.
 \label{eq:control-sensitivity}
\end{equation}
Subtracting the two histories at each common sample time makes $S_{\mathrm{test}}$ sensitive to changes in both amplitude and timing.

\FloatBarrier
\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

In the airfoil restarts, placing the same discrepancy in the older BDF2 field alone reverses the sign of the first-step lift change and gives about one third of its magnitude relative to transferring both fields. All four second-order transfer methods reproduce this prediction of the linearized load response. For total mass there is a separate exact result. With conservative spatial fluxes, state-independent boundary mass flux and exact inner solves, equal mass errors in both stored fields shift the later mass by that same amount.

In the shock tube, global mass correction leaves a smeared contact even when it restores the total mass. GP2 and CV2 preserve the wave profiles more closely than NN1 and BAR2. With relaxed bounds, however, their global redistribution also reaches the uniform far field. For CV2, mass changes during the subsequent flow steps despite near-roundoff conservation across transfers. Both open-boundary exchange and incomplete inner convergence can contribute to the measured drift.

In the SA airfoil restarts, the RMS drag departures of the three methods differ by less than 2\% while the NN2 and GP2 histories differ by 12--16\% of the GP2 departure. The ordering of the departures depends on the mesh sequence, and halving the physical time step changes the NN2--GP2 drag difference by more than its nominal RMS. CV2 produces the smallest changes in the monitored integrals; all are small relative to whole-domain totals, which does not by itself establish their aerodynamic significance. The viscous calculations therefore do not rank the methods.

For an unsteady restart, the time integrator determines which stored states must be transferred and how their errors enter the next step. In flows with shocks or contacts, comparing profiles reveals local smearing that a mass balance cannot detect. Measuring the mass immediately before and after each transfer, and again after the flow step, identifies when a change occurs. For aerodynamic loads, the observation interval must allow disturbances from the remeshed region to reach the wall. A ranking then requires load differences large enough to resolve under time-step refinement and tighter iterative convergence.

\section{Remainder bounds for the linear response}
\label{app:remainders}

This appendix derives integral expressions for the remainders $\mathcal N_B$ and $\mathcal N_G^{n+1}$ defined in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:spatial-remainder} and \eqref{eq:output-remainder}, and gives the conditions under which they are quadratic. Norms are the Euclidean vector norm $\|\cdot\|_2$ and its induced matrix norm, applied to nondimensional states.

\paragraph{Spatial remainder.}
Join the reference and restarted new states by the straight path
\[
 U(\sigma)=U_B^{n+1}+\sigma\eps^{n+1},\qquad 0\leq\sigma\leq1,
\]
so that $U(0)=U_B^{n+1}$ and $U(1)=\widehat U_B^{n+1}$. Consider the spatial residual along this path, $f(\sigma)=F_B(U(\sigma),t^{n+1})$. Since $\mathrm dU/\mathrm d\sigma=\eps^{n+1}$, the chain rule gives
\[
 \frac{\mathrm df}{\mathrm d\sigma}=\partial_U F_B(U(\sigma),t^{n+1})\,\eps^{n+1}.
\]
Integrating from $\sigma=0$ to $\sigma=1$, and using $f(1)-f(0)=F_B(\widehat U_B^{n+1},t^{n+1})-F_B(U_B^{n+1},t^{n+1})$, gives
\[
 F_B(\widehat U_B^{n+1},t^{n+1})-F_B(U_B^{n+1},t^{n+1})
 =\int_0^1\partial_U F_B(U(\sigma),t^{n+1})\,\eps^{n+1}\dd\sigma.
\]
The linear part can be written in the same form, because $J_B$ and $\eps^{n+1}$ do not depend on $\sigma$:
\[
 J_B\eps^{n+1}=\int_0^1J_B\eps^{n+1}\dd\sigma.
\]
Subtracting the second expression from the first and using the definition in Eq.~\eqref{eq:spatial-remainder} gives
\[
 \mathcal N_B(\eps^{n+1})
 =\int_0^1\big[\partial_U F_B(U(\sigma),t^{n+1})-J_B\big]\eps^{n+1}\dd\sigma.
\]
The integrand vanishes at $\sigma=0$, where the two Jacobians coincide. Assume that the Jacobian is Lipschitz continuous along the path, with a finite constant $L_F$. Since $\|U(\sigma)-U_B^{n+1}\|_2=\sigma\|\eps^{n+1}\|_2$, this means
\begin{equation}
 \|\partial_U F_B(U(\sigma),t^{n+1})-J_B\|_2
 \leq L_F\sigma\|\eps^{n+1}\|_2,
 \quad 0\leq\sigma\leq1.
 \label{eq:jacobian-variation-bound}
\end{equation}
The norm of an integral is at most the integral of the norm, so
\begin{equation}
 \|\mathcal N_B(\eps^{n+1})\|_2
 \leq\int_0^1\|\partial_U F_B(U(\sigma),t^{n+1})-J_B\|_2\,\|\eps^{n+1}\|_2\dd\sigma
 \leq L_F\|\eps^{n+1}\|_2^2\int_0^1\sigma\dd\sigma
 =\tfrac12 L_F\|\eps^{n+1}\|_2^2.
 \label{eq:spatial-remainder-bound}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{State response.}
For exact solves and invertible $A_B$, multiplying Eq.~\eqref{eq:linearized-bdf2-response} by $A_B^{-1}$ gives
\[
 \eps^{n+1}=R_B\eta^n-\Delta t A_B^{-1}\mathcal N_B(\eps^{n+1}).
\]
Taking norms and using Eq.~\eqref{eq:spatial-remainder-bound},
\[
 \|\eps^{n+1}\|_2
 \leq\|R_B\|_2\|\eta^n\|_2
 +\tfrac12\Delta t\|A_B^{-1}\|_2L_F\|\eps^{n+1}\|_2^2.
\]
Suppose that the state difference is small enough that $\tfrac12\Delta t\|A_B^{-1}\|_2L_F\|\eps^{n+1}\|_2\leq\tfrac12$. The last term is then at most $\|\eps^{n+1}\|_2/2$, and moving it to the left-hand side gives
\[
 \|\eps^{n+1}\|_2\leq2\|R_B\|_2\|\eta^n\|_2.
\]
Inserting this bound into the remainder term gives
\[
 \|\Delta t A_B^{-1}\mathcal N_B(\eps^{n+1})\|_2
 \leq\tfrac12\Delta t\|A_B^{-1}\|_2L_F\|\eps^{n+1}\|_2^2
 \leq2\Delta t\|A_B^{-1}\|_2L_F\|R_B\|_2^2\|\eta^n\|_2^2,
\]
which is the $O(\Delta t\|\eta^n\|_2^2)$ term in Eq.~\eqref{eq:state-response}. The constant contains $\|A_B^{-1}\|_2$ and $\|R_B\|_2$ and therefore depends on the mesh and the time step.

\paragraph{Load remainder.}
The same steps apply to a load. Along the path, let $g(\sigma)=G_B(U(\sigma))$. The chain rule gives $\mathrm dg/\mathrm d\sigma=\nabla_U G_B(U(\sigma))^T\eps^{n+1}$, and integrating from zero to one gives
\[
 \eps_G^{n+1}=G_B(\widehat U_B^{n+1})-G_B(U_B^{n+1})
 =\int_0^1\nabla_U G_B(U(\sigma))^T\eps^{n+1}\dd\sigma.
\]
Subtracting $(\nabla_U G_B)^T\eps^{n+1}=\int_0^1(\nabla_U G_B)^T\eps^{n+1}\dd\sigma$ and using the definition in Eq.~\eqref{eq:output-remainder} gives
\[
 \mathcal N_G^{n+1}=\int_0^1
 [\nabla_U G_B(U(\sigma))-\nabla_U G_B]^T\eps^{n+1}\dd\sigma.
\]
If the load gradient is Lipschitz continuous along the path, with a finite constant $L_G$,
\begin{equation}
 \|\nabla_U G_B(U(\sigma))-\nabla_U G_B\|_2
 \leq L_G\sigma\|\eps^{n+1}\|_2,
 \quad 0\leq\sigma\leq1,
 \label{eq:output-gradient-variation-bound}
\end{equation}
then, as for the spatial remainder, $|\mathcal N_G^{n+1}|\leq\tfrac12L_G\|\eps^{n+1}\|_2^2\leq2L_G\|R_B\|_2^2\|\eta^n\|_2^2$. Together with the state-response bound, this gives the $O(\|\eta^n\|_2^2)$ term in Eq.~\eqref{eq:force-response}. Ideal-gas wall pressure, for example, is a differentiable function of the conserved variables for positive density, and a finite bound requires the density to stay away from zero over the states considered. A viscous load also depends on the wall-gradient reconstruction and the constitutive model, which must satisfy the same bound.
