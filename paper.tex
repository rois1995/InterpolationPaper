\title{Mesh-to-Mesh Transfer Errors in BDF2 Unsteady Flow Simulations}
\author{}
\date{}
\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Discrete remeshing in an unsteady CFD calculation creates two coupled disturbances: the spatial discretization changes, and the histories required by a multistep integrator must be transferred to the new mesh. This study compares nearest-neighbor Taylor transfer, barycentric interpolation, and continuous Galerkin projection in BDF2 Euler and RANS calculations on fixed physical domains. Manufactured fields verify the nominal first- to third-order behavior and separate interpolation accuracy, boundedness, and integral preservation. For BDF2, the target-referenced history forcing is $2\varepsilon^n-\varepsilon^{n-1}/2$. A controlled Euler NACA~0012 restart shows why both histories matter: perturbing only the older state changes the lift even though the latest state is exact, and the measured response ratios agree with the BDF2 coefficients to within 0.3\% in norm. Repeated Euler remeshing further shows that restoring a global integral can leave a spatial transport error essentially unchanged. The fixed-airfoil RANS extension adds SA and SST turbulence histories and separates pressure and friction loads. Galerkin projection preserves the monitored turbulence integrals much more closely than second-order nearest-neighbor transfer, but the two methods produce similar aggregate drag errors over the short two-seed comparison. Near-wall turbulence discrepancies also develop on vertices whose coordinates never move. Together, the results show that transfer accuracy, multistep-history consistency, and global conservation are distinct requirements; none alone provides a universal ranking of remeshing methods.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Unsteady Reynolds-averaged Navier--Stokes (RANS) calculations use mesh adaptation to follow evolving flow structures without maintaining fine resolution throughout the domain. When adaptation regenerates connectivity, the flow and turbulence histories must be transferred to the new discretization before time integration can continue. The resulting calculation combines spatial approximation, solution transfer, and temporal integration; improving one component does not necessarily reduce their combined error. Time-accurate anisotropic adaptation and space--time adaptation for implicit RANS calculations illustrate this coupling~\cite{AlauzetLoseilleOlivier2018,SauvageAlauzetDervieux2024}.

Two questions arise at each mesh change. Does the transfer represent the source field accurately on the target mesh, and does the transferred state permit a consistent continuation of the target discrete evolution? Polynomial reproduction and integral conservation address the first question only partially. A transfer can preserve a total integral while redistributing the field in a way that changes a residual balance or an aerodynamic load. Conversely, an accurate pointwise interpolant can introduce a small global imbalance. Repeated transfers may accumulate either effect, depending on the error's spatial structure and its interaction with the evolving flow.

The distinction between interpolation accuracy and preservation of a discrete state is well established. Common-refinement Galerkin projection provides an integral-preserving, best-$L_2$ transfer between finite-element spaces~\cite{FarrellMaddison2011,JiaoHeath2004}, while constrained variants are needed when additional discrete properties must be preserved~\cite{MaddisonHiester2017}. Balance-preserving transfer in shallow-water models provides a related example: an accurate interpolant need not preserve a steady discrete balance~\cite{MaddisonCotterFarrell2011,MaddisonCotterFarrell2013}. Here these ideas are examined in the specific setting of a vertex-centered finite-volume BDF2 restart and its subsequent aerodynamic response.

The comparison uses three established constructions: nearest-neighbor Taylor transfer, barycentric interpolation on a simplicial representation of the source mesh, and continuous Galerkin projection. Their nominal orders range from one to three. A bounded global correction is also applied to the pointwise methods, allowing the effect of enforcing a total integral to be assessed without replacing the underlying interpolation rule. The analysis distinguishes the consistent finite-element integral from the nodal integral actually used by the receiving solver, particularly when a quadratic projected field is reduced to vertex-only output.

The numerical argument proceeds from static transfer verification to accumulated Euler transport and controlled aerodynamic restarts. A two-dimensional Riemann calculation tests transfer frequency and deformation amplitude. An Euler NACA~0012 case isolates the two-history response on independent meshes and examines repeated load disturbances. The final application retains the fixed airfoil but adds SA and SST turbulence transport and viscous loads. Its nominally steady baseline makes remeshing-induced transients observable without imposed body motion. The scope is discrete replacement of stationary meshes in time-accurate calculations, not continuous mesh motion, a physically periodic RANS response, or a comparison of turbulence-model accuracy.

\section{Solution-transfer operators}
\label{sec:numerical-framework}

\subsection{Representation and reconstructed derivatives}
\label{sec:derivative-reconstruction}

The source and target meshes, $A$ and $B$, cover the same discretized domain $\Omega$. Their nodal values are $U_A$ and $U_B$, and $M_M=\operatorname{diag}(w_i^M)\otimes I$ contains the positive control-volume weights of the vertex-centered flow solver on mesh $M$. For one component $\phi$, the solver's integral is
\begin{equation}
 Q_M(\phi_M)=\sum_i w_i^M\phi_{M,i}.
 \label{eq:fv-discrete-integral}
\end{equation}
All compared transfers return values at the solver vertices. Density, momentum, and total energy are transferred in conserved-variable form; nonlinear products are not reconstructed from separately interpolated primitive variables. Nodal sampling and control-volume averaging are different representation operators, even when the same weights are used to monitor an integral.

Nearest-neighbor corrections and the quadratic source representation require derivatives recovered from surrounding nodal data. A weighted least-squares fit determines the coefficients in
\begin{equation}
 \phi_j-\phi_i\simeq g_i\cdot r_{ij}
       +\tfrac12r_{ij}^{T}H_i r_{ij},\qquad r_{ij}=x_j-x_i.
 \label{eq:wls-polynomial}
\end{equation}
The degree-one fit omits the Hessian; the degree-two fit retains it. With a full-rank, suitably scaled stencil and smooth data, polynomial reproduction gives an $O(h)$ gradient error for the linear fit and $O(h^2)$ gradient and $O(h)$ Hessian errors for the quadratic fit. These are conditional estimates: irregularity, anisotropy, rank deficiency, and weighting can degrade derivative recovery~\cite{BarthFrederickson1990,Barth1993,Mavriplis2003,DiskinThomas2008}. The third-order variants therefore use degree-two reconstruction. Their accuracy is checked numerically rather than inferred from the requested polynomial degree alone.

\subsection{Nearest-neighbor and barycentric interpolation}
\label{sec:point-transfer}

Nearest-neighbor transfer selects the closest source vertex $n(j)$ to each target vertex and evaluates a Taylor expansion there. With $r_j=x_j^B-x_{n(j)}^A$, the three variants are
\begin{align}
 (\calI^{\mathrm{NN1}}_{AB}\phi_A)_j&=\phi_{A,n(j)},\label{eq:nn1}\\
 (\calI^{\mathrm{NN2}}_{AB}\phi_A)_j&=\phi_{A,n(j)}+g_{n(j)}\cdot r_j,\label{eq:nn2}\\
 (\calI^{\mathrm{NN3}}_{AB}\phi_A)_j&=\phi_{A,n(j)}+g_{n(j)}\cdot r_j
                 +\tfrac12r_j^TH_{n(j)}r_j.\label{eq:nn3}
\end{align}
Thus NN1 copies a value, NN2 accounts for local slope, and NN3 also accounts for curvature. No containing-element search is required. For $|r_j|=O(h)$ and accurate derivatives, their smooth-field errors are respectively $O(h)$, $O(h^2)$, and $O(h^3)$. The selected donor changes discontinuously across source-node Voronoi boundaries, so a smooth displacement need not produce a smooth change in the interpolation error.

Barycentric interpolation instead locates the source simplex containing the target point. A conforming triangulated or tetrahedralized view of the original mesh supplies this search geometry, using original vertices rather than an augmented control-volume point set. If the containing simplex has vertices $x_{i_r}^A$, its barycentric coordinates satisfy
\begin{equation}
 x_j^B=\sum_{r=1}^{d+1}\lambda_r x_{i_r}^A,
 \qquad \sum_r\lambda_r=1,\qquad \lambda_r\geq0.
 \label{eq:barycentric-coordinates}
\end{equation}
They express the target point as a geometric weighted average of the simplex vertices. Applying the same weights to the field gives
\begin{align}
 (\calI^{\mathrm{BAR2}}_{AB}\phi_A)_j
 &=\sum_r\lambda_r\phi_{A,i_r},\label{eq:bar2}\\
 (\calI^{\mathrm{BAR3}}_{AB}\phi_A)_j
 &=\sum_r\lambda_r\left[\phi_{A,i_r}
       +\tfrac12 g_{i_r}\cdot(x_j^B-x_{i_r}^A)\right].\label{eq:bar3}
\end{align}
\label{sec:barycentric-transfer}
BAR2 is affine-exact and already second order. For a quadratic field with exact vertex gradients, the half-gradient term in BAR3 cancels the quadratic interpolation remainder, yielding third-order consistency. The factor $1/2$ follows from this cancellation rather than from calibration. Both expressions are continuous across a conforming simplex interface when shared vertices have unique values and gradients. Neither enforces Eq.~\eqref{eq:fv-discrete-integral} between meshes.

Limiting is separate from nominal order. A factor $0\leq\Phi_i\leq1$ may scale both derivative terms in NN3, so $\Phi_i=0$ recovers NN1; in BAR3 it scales the gradient correction and recovers BAR2, not a piecewise-constant interpolant. The smooth verification uses unlimited reconstruction. The discontinuous tests compare unlimited and Barth--Jespersen-limited transfer~\cite{BarthJespersen1989}; differentiable and higher-order limiter constructions provide related alternatives~\cite{Venkatakrishnan1995,MichalakOllivierGooch2009,Nishikawa2022}. Damping a quadratic with a factor chosen from linear increments alone does not establish boundedness at every evaluation point. The measured extrema and the final thermodynamic admissibility of the state remain necessary diagnostics.

\subsection{Galerkin projection and common-refinement integration}
\label{sec:galerkin-projection}

Galerkin projection determines a coupled target field rather than evaluating each target point independently. Let $V_B^p$ be the continuous degree-$p$ Lagrange space on the target simplicial mesh. The projection of a source representation $\phi_A^h$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
 (\phi_B^h-\phi_A^h,v_B)_{L_2(\Omega)}=0
 \quad\forall v_B\in V_B^p,
 \label{eq:galerkin-orthogonality}
\end{equation}
and minimizes the $L_2$ distance to that source representation~\cite{FarrellMaddison2011,JiaoHeath2004}. For $\phi_B^h=\sum_jv_jN_j^B$, this gives
\begin{equation}
 H_Bv=b,\qquad
 (H_B)_{ij}=\int_\Omega N_i^BN_j^B\dd x,\qquad
 b_i=\int_\Omega N_i^B\phi_A^h\dd x.
 \label{eq:galerkin-system}
\end{equation}
The consistent mass matrix $H_B$ is sparse and positive definite. Its off-diagonal entries couple basis functions sharing target elements; solving the system determines all coefficients simultaneously. It is distinct from the diagonal finite-volume matrix $M_B$ used in time integration.

The load vector is the cross-mesh operation. On one target element, $N_i^B$ has a fixed polynomial expression, but the source field may cross several source-element boundaries and change polynomial expression within that element. Consequently,
\begin{equation}
 b_i=\sum_{K_B}\sum_{K_A:\,|K_A\cap K_B|>0}
       \int_{K_A\cap K_B}N_i^B\phi_A^h\dd x.
 \label{eq:galerkin-load-intersections}
\end{equation}
Each intersection fixes both polynomial expressions and permits exact polynomial quadrature. These intersections form the source--target common refinement; they may be integrated locally without storing a complete additional mesh. Coverage must be complete and nonoverlapping. Products require degree-two quadrature for $P^1$/$P^1$ transfer and degree-four quadrature for $P^2$/$P^2$ transfer on affine simplices~\cite{Dunavant1985,Keast1986}. This geometric integration and the mass-matrix solve distinguish projection from the point searches in NN and BAR.

GP2 uses $P^1$ source and target spaces. GP3 uses $P^2$ spaces, introducing edge coefficients internally. With $d_{ij}=x_i-x_j$ and $\overline H_{ij}=(H_i+H_j)/2$, the source midpoint value is reconstructed as
\begin{equation}
 \phi_{ij}=\tfrac12(\phi_i+\phi_j)
                 -\tfrac18d_{ij}^T\overline H_{ij}d_{ij}.
 \label{eq:gp3-edge-value}
\end{equation}
For a quadratic, the subtracted curvature term is exactly the difference between the endpoint average and the midpoint value. GP3 then solves for the complete quadratic target field, but only its original-vertex values are supplied to the flow solver. Nominal $L_2$ approximation orders are two and three under the usual smoothness and mesh-stability assumptions; nodal accuracy after this complete pipeline is assessed separately.

\label{sec:simplicial-representation}
On mixed-element grids, BAR and GP use conforming subdivisions based on a deterministic shared-face diagonal rule. The approximation is $P^p$ on the simplices, not $Q^p$ on the native quadrilaterals or hexahedra. A valid conforming split and the chosen control-volume weights must therefore be retained consistently in geometric and integral checks.

\subsection{Which integral is conserved?}
\label{sec:global-correction}
\label{sec:galerkin-vertex-output}

For an unconstrained target space containing constants, Eq.~\eqref{eq:galerkin-orthogonality} preserves the integral of the complete finite-element field. Coordinate functions also belong to continuous $P^1$, and quadratic coordinates belong to $P^2$. Exact projection therefore preserves the corresponding consistent moments before constraints, sampling, or limiting. This is not finite-volume conservation over arbitrary target cells: their indicator functions generally do not belong to the continuous test space. Nor is the inverse mass matrix a local transfer operator. Equality of source and target geometry on one subregion does not by itself make the projection the identity there.

For GP2, the consistent and solver-lumped integrals coincide when $w_i^M=\int N_i^M\dd x$ on both meshes. For GP3, source lifting and target vertex restriction each introduce a representation-dependent difference. In particular, a third-order projected field does not imply an $O(h^3)$ lumped-integral defect. Appendix~\ref{app:quadrature-mismatch} gives a quadratic counterexample with an $O(h^2)$ discrepancy. The consistent projection, the stored restart field, and any repaired field are consequently distinguished throughout the comparison.

A global correction can enforce a chosen solver integral independently of the transfer construction. For a raw nodal field $\phi_B^r$, it seeks a correction $c$ such that
\begin{equation}
 \sum_j w_j^B c_j=Q_A(\phi_A)-Q_B(\phi_B^r),
 \qquad \ell_j\leq\phi_{B,j}^r+c_j\leq u_j.
 \label{eq:global-correction-constraint}
\end{equation}
The implemented repair redistributes the defect over nodes with available capacity, freezing saturated nodes~\cite{ShashkovWendroff2004,KucharikShashkovWendroff2003}. Feasibility requires the requested integral to lie between the weighted lower and upper bounds, with fixed boundary values included. Source-range bounds on individual conserved components do not alone guarantee positive pressure. Accuracy is retained only if the correction is sufficiently small in the accuracy norm; a small total defect does not itself bound every nodal increment.

\label{sec:galerkin-limiter}
The optional NN/BAR correction is denoted by the suffix GC. GP additionally uses a projection-level limiter: source-vertex values in overlapping elements define $[\ell_j,u_j]$, widened by $\tau$ times the global source range. Clipping is followed by redistribution preserving a selected lumped total. Hard limiting sets $\tau=0$; soft limiting allows a controlled excursion. Once active, this nonlinear post-processing need not preserve Galerkin orthogonality, first moments, or the best-approximation property. For GP2, preserving its lumped total preserves its consistent mass only under the weight compatibility above. For vertex-only GP3, no such implication holds automatically. These qualifications matter when interpreting the method labels in the numerical results.

\section{BDF2 response to a mesh change}
\label{sec:restart-consistency}

\subsection{Reference trajectories and history mismatch}
\label{sec:unsteady-discretization}
\label{sec:native-transfer-decomposition}

Between remeshing events, the mesh is fixed and the semi-discrete equations are
\begin{equation}
 M_M\dot U_M+F_M(U_M,t)=0.
 \label{eq:semidiscrete}
\end{equation}
The residual can contain Euler, viscous, or RANS terms; the state must include every transported variable required by the time integrator. The constant-step BDF2 equation on the target mesh is
\begin{equation}
 \Phi_B(Y;V,W)=\frac{M_B}{\Delta t}
       \left(\tfrac32Y-2V+\tfrac12W\right)+F_B(Y,t^{n+1})=0.
 \label{eq:bdf2}
\end{equation}
This form is not a general moving-volume ALE formula. Continuously moving meshes require consistent geometric history terms and a discrete geometric conservation law~\cite{KoobusFarhat1999,GeuzaineGrandmontFarhat2003}; those are outside the fixed-mesh-between-transfers derivation.

Let $U_A^k$ and $U_B^k$ be native trajectories for the same physical problem, computed on the two meshes. The transferred histories and their target-referenced discrepancies are
\begin{equation}
 \widehat U_B^k=\calI_{AB}(U_A^k),\qquad
 \eps^k=\widehat U_B^k-U_B^k,\qquad k=n,n-1.
 \label{eq:history-defects}
\end{equation}
Here $\calI_{AB}$ includes all limiting, correction, and restart-field processing. Let $\Pi_Mu^k$ represent a common exact field in the solver's chosen data representation, and $e_M^k=U_M^k-\Pi_Mu^k$. An exact decomposition, valid also for nonlinear transfer, is
\begin{align}
 \eps^k={}&\underbrace{\calI_{AB}(\Pi_Au^k)-\Pi_Bu^k}_{\text{transfer consistency}}\notag\\
 &+\underbrace{\calI_{AB}(U_A^k)-\calI_{AB}(\Pi_Au^k)-e_B^k}_{\text{propagated source error and target error}}.
 \label{eq:defect-decomposition}
\end{align}
Only for a linear transfer may the second line be replaced by $\calI_{AB}e_A^k-e_B^k$.

Thus a native-target reference does not make $\eps^k$ a pure interpolation error. It puts both states into the same target evolution problem while retaining their pre-existing discretization mismatch. For an output $G$, the decomposition
\begin{align}
 G_B(\widehat U_B^k)-G_A(U_A^k)
 ={}&[G_B(U_B^k)-G_A(U_A^k)]\notag\\
 &+[G_B(\widehat U_B^k)-G_B(U_B^k)]
 \label{eq:observable-decomposition}
\end{align}
separates native mesh sensitivity from the target-referenced restart discrepancy, not from an independently known consistency error. Comparisons of different transfers from the same source histories to the same target provide the controlled method comparison.

\subsection{Exact history forcing and local state response}

At any common trial state $Y$, changing only the histories gives the exact identity
\begin{equation}
 \Phi_B(Y;U_B^n+\eps^n,U_B^{n-1}+\eps^{n-1})
 -\Phi_B(Y;U_B^n,U_B^{n-1})
 =-\frac{M_B}{\Delta t}\eta^n,
 \quad \eta^n=2\eps^n-\tfrac12\eps^{n-1}.
 \label{eq:history-residual-identity}
\end{equation}
This identity does not require a linear spatial residual or small transfer errors. It tests history assembly provided both residual evaluations use exactly the same trial state and residual convention.

For the solved states, define $\delta^{n+1}=\widehat U_B^{n+1}-U_B^{n+1}$. Let $r$ and $\widehat r$ be the remaining BDF2 algebraic residuals. Subtraction and expansion about the native target state give
\begin{equation}
 A_B\delta^{n+1}=M_B\eta^n+\Delta t(\widehat r-r)
                -\Delta t\,\mathcal N_B(\delta^{n+1}),\qquad
 A_B=\tfrac32M_B+\Delta t J_B,
 \label{eq:linearized-bdf2-response}
\end{equation}
where $J_B=\partial F_B/\partial U$ and $\mathcal N_B$ is the nonlinear Taylor remainder. With a locally Lipschitz Jacobian, $\mathcal N_B(\delta)=O(\|\delta\|^2)$. If $A_B$ is locally invertible, the perturbations are small, and algebraic errors are negligible,
\begin{equation}
 \delta^{n+1}=R_B\eta^n+O(\Delta t\|\eta^n\|^2),
 \qquad R_B=A_B^{-1}M_B.
 \label{eq:state-response}
\end{equation}
The constants are local to the mesh, state, time step, and norm; they are not assumed uniform over arbitrary anisotropic mesh families. Active limiter switches or insufficient inner convergence may invalidate this smooth-response approximation, but not Eq.~\eqref{eq:history-residual-identity}.

The steady experiment uses one spatial discrepancy $\eps^\star=\calI_{AB}U_A^\star-U_B^\star$ and assigns it independently to the two histories:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
History configuration & $\eps^n$ & $\eps^{n-1}$ & $\eta^n$\\
\midrule
Native & $0$ & $0$ & $0$\\
Full & $\eps^\star$ & $\eps^\star$ & $3\eps^\star/2$\\
Latest only & $\eps^\star$ & $0$ & $2\eps^\star$\\
Older only & $0$ & $\eps^\star$ & $-\eps^\star/2$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\label{eq:naca-history-decomposition}
These diagnostic restarts predict
\begin{equation}
 \delta_n\simeq\tfrac43\delta_{\mathrm{full}},\qquad
 \delta_{n-1}\simeq-\tfrac13\delta_{\mathrm{full}},\qquad
 \delta_{\mathrm{full}}\simeq\delta_n+\delta_{n-1}.
 \label{eq:naca-history-ratios}
\end{equation}
The older-only case distinguishes the two-history forcing from a latest-state diagnostic. All three perturbations nevertheless use the same spatial direction: recovering their ratios is a test of directional response, not a complete validation of $R_B$ for arbitrary history fields.

\subsection{Aerodynamic response and propagation}
\label{sec:delayed-response}

For a differentiable aerodynamic functional with gradient $g_B^{n+1}$, the first-step discrepancy is
\begin{equation}
 \Delta G^{n+1}=(g_B^{n+1})^T R_B\eta^n+O(\|\eta^n\|^2).
 \label{eq:force-response}
\end{equation}
Equivalently, $A_B^Tz_B=g_B^{n+1}$ gives $\Delta G^{n+1}=z_B^TM_B\eta^n$ to first order~\cite{GilesPierce2000}. The error is weighted by the target dynamics and the output sensitivity, not by a constant conservation test. Conservation of the source integral constrains only
\begin{equation}
 \bm1^TM_B\eps^k=Q_A(U_A^k)-Q_B(U_B^k),
 \label{eq:gc-native-mismatch}
\end{equation}
which need not vanish because the native integrals may differ.

A slope discrepancy must include the output difference already present at the transfer time:
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\Delta G^{n+1}-\Delta G^n}{\Delta t}
 \simeq\frac{(g_B^{n+1})^TR_B\eta^n-(g_B^n)^T\eps^n}{\Delta t}.
 \label{eq:force-slope-diagnostic}
\end{equation}
Dividing the first-step output difference alone by $\Delta t$ is generally not this quantity. Cancellation between the two terms also prevents a universal force-kink scaling from being inferred from interpolation order alone.

For later steps, linearization along the native target trajectory gives
\begin{equation}
 \begin{bmatrix}\delta^{k+1}\\\delta^k\end{bmatrix}
 \simeq
 \begin{bmatrix}2R_B^{k+1}&-R_B^{k+1}/2\\I&0\end{bmatrix}
 \begin{bmatrix}\delta^k\\\delta^{k-1}\end{bmatrix}.
 \label{eq:multistep-propagation}
\end{equation}
Repeated application connects the initial histories to a delayed output. This explains why a first-step force difference need not bound the later excursion. A dominant disturbance originating away from the airfoil may become measurable after acoustic or convective transport, but neither the inverse mass matrix of projection nor an implicit time step has compact support. Distance-dependent arrival is therefore measured by amplitude thresholds, not by a claim of strict first-nonzero causality.

\section{Numerical experiments and assessment}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{Static transfer and repeated Euler transport}
\label{sec:static-verification}
\label{sec:riemann-methodology}

The static tests separate transfer error from flow-solver error. The asymmetric smooth density is
\begin{equation}
 \phi=\rho_\infty\left[2+0.3e^{0.8x/L_x+0.5y/L_y}
              \sin(2\pi x/L_x)\sin(2\pi y/L_y)\right].
 \label{eq:asymmetric-manufactured-field}
\end{equation}
The field is transferred to structured quadrilateral and independently generated mixed triangular/quadrilateral target meshes at nominal resolutions 64, 128, and 256; the source mesh has half the target nominal resolution. Errors are relative nodal density $L_2$ differences from the analytic value at the target points. The effective spacing is defined as $h_{\mathrm{eff}}=N_{\mathrm{nodes}}^{-1/2}$; on this fixed domain it is used only to define refinement ratios. The discontinuous test replaces the smooth field with a circular inclusion of radius $0.25\min(L_x,L_y)$ and density levels 1.2 and 3.6. Because the curved interface is not mesh-aligned, unlimited and limited transfers can be compared on a common unresolved discontinuity.

Consecutive and fitted orders are obtained from
\begin{equation}
 p_{k,k+1}=\frac{\log(E_k/E_{k+1})}{\log(h_k/h_{k+1})},
 \qquad \log E\simeq p_{\mathrm{fit}}\log h+b.
 \label{eq:pairwise-observed-order}
\end{equation}
The three-level sequences are used to check consistency with the nominal orders, not to estimate mesh-independent asymptotic constants. A second smooth test on the structured Riemann mesh family keeps the mean displacement-to-spacing ratio near 0.057 and is reported in Appendix~\ref{app:static-details}.

The unsteady Riemann calculation solves two-dimensional Euler flow on $[0,5]\times[0,1]$, with a uniform $501\times101$-node quadrilateral mesh, $h=0.01$, $\Delta t=10^{-3}$, and 400 physical steps. At each event, the original mesh receives a new bounded random displacement in $x$, with boundary vertices fixed. Distortion is not accumulated geometrically from one event to the next. The bound is the minimum incident corner-triangle altitude divided by SafeFactor, denoted SF. Two transfers per block give 80 transfers for a ten-step interval or 400 for a two-step interval. SF4 is the baseline; SF2 doubles the displacement bound. A no-remeshing calculation supplies a fixed-mesh reference. This experiment measures the combined effect of changing spatial discretization and transferring the solution, not a pure interpolation error relative to an exact flow.

Interpolation-only integral changes are sampled immediately before and after each transfer and summed separately from physical evolution. Density and energy are used because their reference integrals are away from zero. Spatial transport is assessed with two distinct diagnostics: maximum-gradient location and jump/maximum-gradient thickness, or the 50\% crossing and 10--90\% width of a sampled $y$-averaged density profile. Their reference positions, 2.6984 and 2.3993, are not interchangeable. The latter tracker is used for the frequency/amplitude comparisons. The transition is broad; these are operational profile diagnostics rather than independently validated sharp-shock positions. Only one random sequence is available, and the NN1 crossing is sensitive to the sampling grid.

\subsection{NACA~0012: independent meshes and controlled histories}
\label{sec:naca-fixed-methodology}

The aerodynamic case is Euler flow over NACA~0012 at $M_\infty=0.1$ and $\alpha=6^\circ$, with $p_\infty=\rho_\infty=T_\infty=1$, $a_\infty=\sqrt{1.4}=1.18322$, and $U_\infty=0.118322$. BDF2 dual-time integration uses $\Delta t=10^{-3}$. Forces are pressure-only, with unit reference chord and area and moment reference $x/c=0.25$. The nonzero steady computed drag is treated as a numerical load, not as skin friction or shock-induced wave drag; absolute coefficient changes are used.

The independent source and target meshes contain 16,255/32,077 and 22,508/44,583 nodes/triangles, respectively. Both share the same 370 airfoil vertices and 63-node farfield polygon at 100 chords. The surface geometry is therefore unchanged although the interior meshes differ. Each native steady solution is advanced with equal BDF2 histories to create its own reference trajectory. Transfers from $A$ to $B$ then populate the history configurations of Eq.~\eqref{eq:naca-history-ratios}. All comparisons of their solved responses use the native target trajectory at the same physical step.

State discrepancies use the nondimensional volume-weighted norm $\|v\|_M=(v^TMv)^{1/2}$, summed over the stored flow components. Because the farfield occupies most of the domain, this norm emphasizes the global state and is complemented by near-wall and force diagnostics. Vector superposition is assessed by
\begin{equation}
 e_{\mathrm{sup}}=
 \frac{\|\delta_{\mathrm{full}}-\delta_n-\delta_{n-1}\|_{M_B}}
      {\|\delta_{\mathrm{full}}\|_{M_B}},
 \label{eq:history-superposition-error}
\end{equation}
with the analogous scalar expression for lift. Signed force ratios and positive state-norm ratios are kept distinct.

The solver exports $-\Phi_B$, including the temporal residual. Initial residuals are compared before the first update and only for cases sharing a trial state: older-only versus native at $U_B^\star$, and latest-only versus full at $\widehat U_B^\star$. Their $\Delta\eta$ values are respectively $-\eps^\star/2$ and $+\eps^\star/2$. The sign is converted to the convention of Eq.~\eqref{eq:history-residual-identity}. Single-precision exports limit the interpretation of the smallest residual differences. First-step forces and subsequent 50-step summaries provide separate assessments of immediate and later response.

\subsection{Repeated airfoil remeshing and localized disturbances}

The repeated-remeshing experiment perturbs the source mesh between wall distances 0.01 and 20 chords while keeping the boundaries fixed. Thirty-nine mesh replacements occur, one every ten physical steps in the 400-step window. NN2 and GP2 are evaluated on the same two random deformation sequences, and force differences are formed relative to the no-remeshing trajectory. Least-squares slopes are quoted per 100 physical steps as descriptive summaries; the associated regression standard errors refer to each individual correlated time series and are not ensemble uncertainties.

A separate one-event experiment perturbs narrow wall-distance bands centered at $d/c=0.05$, 0.10, and 0.20, then continues without further adaptation. Force onset is the first crossing of a selected fraction of that case's peak response. Fixed pressure shells provide a less spatially integrated diagnostic through their root-mean-square pressure difference. A shell a distance $d-r$ inside the launch band has the approximate acoustic lag $(d-r)/(a_\infty\Delta t)\simeq845(d-r)$. This is a low-Mach scale estimate; actual characteristics depend on local velocity, sound speed, and propagation direction.

The no-remeshing trajectory is the reference for this localized-event experiment, whereas the mixed-history study uses an independently evolved target-mesh reference. Even though the meshes coincide outside the displaced band, the transfer is not guaranteed to remain strictly local. An immediate response is observed up to about $0.08c$ inward of the band; those shells are excluded from the propagation fit. Projection coupling, limiter redistribution, the changed spatial operator, and the implicit update can all contribute to this near-instantaneous component.

\subsection{Fixed-body RANS extension}
\label{sec:rans-methodology}

The RANS extension uses the fixed NACA~0012 at $M_\infty=0.1$, $\alpha=6^\circ$, and $Re_c=6\times10^6$, with Spalart--Allmaras (SA) and shear-stress-transport (SST) closures~\cite{SpalartAllmaras1994,Menter1994}. The objective is to measure remeshing-induced transients about a nominally steady viscous solution, including turbulence histories and skin-friction loads. Both closures use the same hybrid mesh with 29,956 vertices, 14,070 quadrilaterals, 31,339 triangles, 370 airfoil vertices, and a farfield at $100c$. The first off-wall spacing is approximately $5\times10^{-6}c$, and the quadrilateral region extends to about $0.053c$. Surface values reach $y^+\simeq\RansMaxYplus$; the mesh is therefore treated as the resolution used for this comparison, not as an independently grid-converged wall treatment.

Mean-flow fluxes use HLLC with MUSCL reconstruction and a Venkatakrishnan limiter. Turbulence convection uses scalar upwinding without MUSCL reconstruction. The second-order transfer label therefore does not describe the order of every term in the receiving RANS discretization. BDF2 uses $\Delta t=10^{-3}$, 50 inner iterations per physical step, CFL~6, and FGMRES with ILU preconditioning and a 60-iteration linear limit. Here the velocity reference is the freestream sound speed: $a_\infty=1$ and $U_\infty=0.1$, unlike the Euler normalization in Sec.~\ref{sec:naca-fixed-methodology}. The 400-step comparison spans only $tU_\infty/c=0.04$. Each closure has its own 800-step no-transfer reference, initialized with the same steady and startup histories as its perturbed calculations. References are subtracted at matching steps; their startup relaxation is not treated as statistical noise.

The repeated-remeshing comparison uses NN2 and limited GP2 with two prescribed deformation sequences and one mesh replacement every ten physical steps. Surface vertices and the 12,233 vertices with $d_w<0.01c$ are fixed; nodes farther from the wall may move out to $20c$. SF4 sets the baseline displacement limit and a mesh-quality floor of 0.2 is enforced. For each seed, NN2 and GP2 see exactly the same mesh sequence. Thirty-nine replacements are followed by at least one physical step in the analyzed window; the terminal history export is excluded because no subsequent force sample is available. A separately converged solution on the first displaced mesh quantifies the change in the native discrete equilibrium. The one-event GP2 cases perturb the bands $[0.05,0.10]c$, $[0.10,0.15]c$, and $[0.20,0.25]c$, with selected SF2 and longer-window variants.

Density, momentum, energy, and both BDF2 histories of the turbulence variables are transferred. Conservation is monitored for density-weighted $\widetilde\nu$ in SA and for density-weighted $k$ and $\omega$ in SST. After transfer, SST enforces the primitive-variable floors $k\geq10^{-10}$ and $\omega\geq10^{-4}$; SA has no analogous post-pass. Wall distance and eddy viscosity are recomputed on the receiving mesh. The comparison therefore concerns the complete NN2 and GP2 restart pipelines, including limiting and positivity processing. It does not isolate turbulence-history error from the simultaneous mean-flow transfer or test a separate RANS global-correction variant.

Total loads are taken from the force histories. Pressure and friction contributions are additionally integrated from the exported surface fields using the same quadrature for case and reference. These differences resolve changes hidden by the six-decimal force-breakdown output, but the underlying surface fields remain single precision. Surface records duplicated at identical physical steps are counted once. The corresponding script, precision limitations, event accounting, and supporting diagnostics are documented in Appendix~\ref{app:rans-details}.

\subsection{Evidence and reproducibility limits}
\label{sec:evidence-limits}

The convergence orders in the static tests can be recomputed directly from the stored error tables. The Riemann and Euler NACA datasets are mostly reduced summaries, while the RANS dataset also includes force histories, surface fields, and transfer-integral diagnostics. Raw restart states, complete residual vectors, case configurations, and full inner-iteration histories are not included. Numerical settings are therefore treated as run conditions, and conclusions that would require those missing quantities are kept outside the claims of the paper.

\subsection{Accumulated transport under repeated remeshing}
\label{sec:riemann-stress}
\label{sec:riemann-multicycle}
\label{sec:riemann-transition}

After 80 transfers in the Riemann case, NN1 changes the density and energy integrals by $+0.0203\%$ and $+0.0211\%$ of their initial values. The remaining methods stay within $0.0023\%$ in net density change, and GP2 has the smallest median absolute per-transfer density defect, $1.07\times10^{-7}$ compared with $1.11\times10^{-5}$ for NN1. Signed accumulation can contain cancellation, so it should not be read as a statistical drift rate. Boundedness gives a different picture: NN1 and BAR2 introduce no new density extrema, whereas the unlimited derivative-based variants overshoot at every sampled transfer. The complete accumulation and profile results are in Appendix~\ref{app:riemann-stress-data}.

Figure~\ref{fig:riemann-stress} compares the final transition position and width as the remeshing becomes more frequent and more severe. With 400 transfers and the larger SF2 deformation, the corresponding errors are 25.78 and 26.43 cells, whereas GP2 remains at 0.09 cell in the tabulated extraction. The absolute NN1 crossing is sensitive to the profile-sampling procedure, so these values establish the qualitative amplification seen in this extraction rather than a universal rate.

\label{sec:riemann-moment-interpretation}
The moment and limiter diagnostics in Appendix~\ref{app:riemann-moment-data} offer a possible interpretation, but not a causal identification. GP2 has smaller measured lumped centroid changes than BAR2 or NN1, consistent with the moment constraints of the unmodified projection. A whole-domain density centroid is not the 50\%-crossing position, however. Similarly, the unlimited GP2 excursion rises from $7.6\times10^{-6}$ to $3.6\times10^{-3}$ between the two displacement settings, suggesting stronger limiter activity. A global excursion alone does not determine activation of every local bound, and no resolved limiter-on/off width budget is available. The observed width increase cannot therefore be partitioned between projection, limiter repair, spatial discretization, and profile sampling.

The second- and third-order discrepancy norms range from $7.040\times10^{-4}$ to $7.243\times10^{-4}$, a spread below 3\%; NN1 gives $1.474\times10^{-3}$. This clustering is consistent with a shared native mesh mismatch, but does not separate that mismatch quantitatively from transfer error. The lowest tabulated norm belongs to BAR2, but the small spread does not establish a general ranking. Near-wall discrepancies can remain important despite relative whole-domain norms near $1.5\times10^{-6}$, because the normalization includes the large farfield.

The common-trial residual pairs satisfy Eq.~\eqref{eq:history-residual-identity} with relative vector discrepancies of $2.4\times10^{-8}$--$4.2\times10^{-8}$ for NN/BAR, $4.4\times10^{-6}$ for GP2, and $1.3\times10^{-6}$ for GP3. This confirms the implemented BDF2 history signs and coefficients at the precision of the exported fields. The check concerns residual assembly; it does not test the accuracy of the subsequently converged nonlinear response.

Figure~\ref{fig:naca-first-step-cl} provides the direct physical consequence of the older history. With $\eps^n=0$, the older-only lift difference is still $1.83\times10^{-3}$--$2.55\times10^{-3}$ for the five methods with complete first-step force summaries, and its sign is opposite to the corresponding full-history response. These magnitudes exceed the native target window's lift span, but that comparison is descriptive rather than a significance test. More importantly, a diagnostic based only on the latest history would assign zero perturbation to these cases, whereas the BDF2 forcing does not.

Global correction provides a second controlled test. The repeated-remeshing Euler airfoil case reveals behavior that a one-step norm cannot capture. The other loads constrain a symmetry-based interpretation. GP2's lift trend changes sign across seeds, whereas both NN2 lift slopes are positive and differ substantially in magnitude. \section{Discussion and conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

The evidence distinguishes three requirements of a remeshed time-accurate calculation: adequate spatial transfer, appropriate multistep histories, and sufficiently accurate evolution on the receiving mesh. Global integral preservation addresses only part of the first requirement. Its insufficiency is demonstrated by paired Riemann raw/GC comparisons; the importance of the older history is isolated by the Euler NACA restart. The exact common-trial residual identity and approximate BDF2 coefficient ratios support the history analysis without constituting a full validation of the nonlinear resolvent or aerodynamic adjoint.

The fixed-body RANS cases add the two ingredients absent from the Euler tests: transported turbulence histories and viscous loads. GP2 preserves the monitored turbulence integrals much more closely than NN2, yet their total-drag RMS errors differ by less than 1\% for each paired deformation sequence and show no consistent ordering. Both methods also produce a small reduction in friction drag and comparable eddy-viscosity changes inside the protected near-wall vertex set. Integral preservation is therefore a useful transfer diagnostic, but it does not by itself rank the aerodynamic response. The behavior of the complete post-processed restart state is what matters to the receiving RANS calculation.

Reference choice remains part of that evaluation. Independently converged grids have different discrete equilibria, and comparison with a no-remeshing trajectory includes changes in the spatial operator as well as transfer. Identical mesh sequences provide a controlled method comparison, not a pure interpolation-error measurement. Likewise, a spatially restricted mesh change need not produce a strictly localized field perturbation. The Euler pressure records support a dominant acoustic delay, whereas the RANS pressure precision, early nonlocal response, and window-dependent peaks do not establish a propagation law.

The conclusions are limited to fixed physical domains and short remeshing-induced transients about nominally steady airfoil flows. The RANS extension is sufficient to assess turbulence-history transfer and viscous loads within that scope; body motion is a separate problem. The present 50-inner-iteration budget, short time horizon, protected near-wall region, and two deformation sequences are not sufficient for a long-time accuracy ranking or a production recommendation. Those stronger claims would require targeted physical-step and time-step checks, complete run configurations, and transfer-cost measurements. Continuous moving meshes, separated periodic flows, and a higher-order RANS method ranking are left outside the scope of this paper.

\section{Projection verification and vertex-lumping accuracy}
\label{app:naca-gp3-verification}

The NACA GP3 assembly used for the reported results passes constant and affine patch tests to $3.9\times10^{-11}$ and $1.1\times10^{-10}$, respectively. A mesh-specific over-coverage defect identified during verification is documented in the accompanying diagnostic table; it is not evidence of a defect in Galerkin projection itself. In two dimensions, a signed polygon traversal requires signed fan-triangle contributions. Replacing them by absolute areas can overcount self-cancelling portions of the traversal and over-assemble the load vector while leaving the target mass matrix unchanged. This convention is not transferred to disjoint three-dimensional clipped tetrahedra, whose positive measures add. Global bounded repair cannot replace coverage and polynomial-reproduction tests, because it can redistribute a local assembly error into a bounded global bias.

\subsection{A quadratic counterexample to an automatic third-order lumped defect}
\label{app:quadrature-mismatch}

Consider $f(x)=x^2$ on $[0,1]$. Its exact $P^2$ projection onto any conforming one-dimensional mesh is itself, so there is no projection error. On a uniform mesh with spacing $h$, retain only the vertices and use the $P^1$ nodal integration weights. The resulting composite trapezoidal integral is
\begin{equation}
 Q_h(f)=h\left[\frac{f(0)+f(1)}{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{1/h-1}f(ih)\right]
       =\frac13+\frac{h^2}{6}.
 \label{eq:quadratic-counterexample}
\end{equation}
The difference from the consistent quadratic integral is second order despite exact nodal values and exact projection. The same result holds for $f(x,y)=x^2$ on a uniformly triangulated unit square obtained by splitting Cartesian cells; integrating the vertex-linear field in $y$ leaves the same composite rule in $x$. Two meshes can consequently have a source--target lumped difference $(h_B^2-h_A^2)/6$ even when the transferred quadratic is exact. The example is a mathematical check, not an additional CFD measurement. It rules out a general claim that vertex restriction of GP3 necessarily leaves an $O(h^3)$ lumped defect or that repairing that defect automatically retains third-order pointwise accuracy.

\section{Data provenance and automated checks}
\label{app:automated-postprocessing}

The 39 pre-existing input/generated CSVs and 14 RANS source CSVs retain their names, relative locations, and contents. Figures and tables use \texttt{pgfplots} and \texttt{pgfplotstable}; the build tracks their dependencies. The static builder recomputes convergence orders. The RANS builder creates derived views, rejects conflicting duplicate physical keys, removes only exact repeated snapshots, aligns event windows, and recomputes descriptive statistics. Its hash manifest distinguishes original data from derived products and detects later intentional source updates. Neither builder runs CFD.

The original reports are retained. Earlier Riemann/Euler NACA CSVs contain report-transcribed summaries; the new RANS files contain reduced post-processing exports, including actual sampled force histories. Continuous curves are drawn only where time samples exist. Report-only floor statistics are labeled separately. The earlier residual-summary CSV's cosines above one remain preserved but excluded from validation. The accompanying scientific assessment records the remaining source/precision and solver-control limitations and the bibliographic additions. Reproduction from these reduced data is not a substitute for archiving the configurations, meshes, restart states, and extraction scripts required to reproduce the simulations themselves.
