\begin{abstract}
Changing the mesh in an unsteady flow calculation requires transferring the solution and the previous states used by the time integrator. We compare nearest-neighbor Taylor interpolation, barycentric interpolation, Galerkin projection, and control-volume transfer in static tests, repeated remeshing, and airfoil calculations. The tests examine field accuracy, bounds, integral preservation, and the subsequent flow response. Frequent remeshing smears the Riemann waves with first-order nearest-neighbor and linear barycentric interpolation. Higher-order point interpolation and projection preserve the profiles more closely. Correcting total mass does not restore a smeared profile. Even mass-preserving transfer can change mass during later flow steps: global limiter redistribution disturbs the far field and changes open-boundary fluxes. Local flux correction keeps final mass and energy drift near roundoff and modestly reduces density error, but allows small extrema within relaxed bounds and leaves some correction unapplied. Controlled restarts with the second-order backward differentiation formula (BDF2) show that an error in the older stored state changes next-step lift even when the latest state matches the reference. In fixed-body Reynolds-averaged Navier--Stokes calculations, smaller transfer-integral errors do not establish better aerodynamic accuracy: load differences remain sensitive to the time step and iterative convergence. Transfer must therefore be assessed together with the subsequent time integration.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Mesh adaptation changes where an unsteady flow solution is stored. Before the calculation can continue, the solution must be transferred from the old mesh to the new one. Repeating this operation can accumulate errors, alongside those from spatial discretization and time integration~\cite{AlauzetLoseilleOlivier2018,SauvageAlauzetDervieux2024,LevyMay2025}. A multistep integrator also needs solutions from previous time levels. We call these stored solutions history states. This paper examines what transfer preserves, what it changes, and how errors in the stored states affect the flow after remeshing.

Three common measures of transfer quality answer different questions. Approximation order describes how the error decreases under mesh refinement for a smooth field. Boundedness asks whether transfer introduces values outside the source range. Conservation asks whether selected integrals or moments are preserved. Common-refinement and Galerkin methods provide accurate conservative transfer between nonmatching meshes~\cite{JiaoHeath2004,FarrellMaddison2011}. Constrained formulations can also preserve norms and weak incompressibility~\cite{MaddisonHiester2017}. Conservative remapping integrates a reconstructed source field over target cells~\cite{DukowiczKodis1987,MargolinShashkov2003,AlauzetMehrenberger2010,Alauzet2016}, while conservative Galerkin transfer has been used in high-order implicit adaptation with bounds near discontinuities~\cite{LevyMay2024,LevyMay2025}. These properties must be checked separately: the correct total mass does not determine its spatial distribution, and limiting new extrema can change the accuracy and conservation of an unconstrained transfer.

A single transferred field does not describe everything the time integrator uses. After remeshing, the stored states were transferred from another mesh rather than computed by earlier time steps on the new mesh. An error in the older state can therefore change the next residual even if the latest state agrees with a reference solution on the target mesh. Limiters, positivity corrections, changes in field representation, and boundary conditions can introduce further changes before time integration resumes. Related shallow-water studies show that preserving a discrete equilibrium can suppress transfer-induced disturbances~\cite{MaddisonCotterFarrell2011,MaddisonCotterFarrell2013}. Here we study stored states in compressible flow and the resulting aerodynamic loads.

We compare four transfer families in a vertex-centered finite-volume solver using the second-order backward differentiation formula (BDF2): nearest-neighbor Taylor interpolation (NN), barycentric interpolation (BAR), continuous Galerkin projection (GP), and conservative control-volume transfer (CV). A numerical suffix gives the nominal smooth-field accuracy order, as in NN1 or GP2. A bounded global correction (GC) is used separately to test the effect of restoring a domain integral. The CV method matches integrals over the target median-dual control volumes; Galerkin projection instead matches integrals weighted by continuous test functions.

The experiments progress from isolated transfers to flow calculations. Smooth and discontinuous fields reveal accuracy, new extrema, and integral errors without flow evolution. A Riemann problem shows how these changes accumulate under repeated remeshing. Controlled airfoil restarts vary the latest and older BDF2 states separately, allowing an exact residual identity to be checked against the computed state and lift response. Third-order variants are included in these verification tests. Repeated-remeshing aerodynamic comparisons use methods of the same nominal second order.

Fixed-body Reynolds-averaged Navier--Stokes (RANS) calculations then examine turbulence transport and viscous loads, with checks on time-step and iterative sensitivity. All tests use prescribed remeshing on fixed physical domains and independently computed reference solutions. The Riemann comparisons use three random mesh-perturbation realizations per amplitude; the repeated-remeshing airfoil comparisons use two, and the RANS sensitivity tests use one. Local flux correction is tested in the Riemann problem. Each experimental subsection presents its setup, results, and interpretation together.


\section{Transferring a solution between meshes}
\label{sec:numerical-framework}

The transfer methods differ in how they compute values on the target mesh. NN and BAR interpolate from nearby source values; GP and CV solve coupled systems based on integrals. Each method can then apply limiting, boundary conditions, and corrections for positive density and internal energy. We distinguish the field before these operations from the field passed to the flow solver. Method suffixes give nominal accuracy order rather than polynomial degree: BAR2 is second order, and GP2 and CV2 use piecewise-linear fields. Global correction adjusts a domain integral after transfer.

\subsection{Stored variables and reconstructed derivatives}
\label{sec:derivative-reconstruction}

The source mesh $A$ and target mesh $B$ cover the same discretized domain $\Omega$. On mesh $M$, the solver stores a state vector $U_M$ at the vertices. Each vertex $i$ has a surrounding median-dual control volume $C_i^M$ of positive measure $w_i^M=|C_i^M|$. The finite-volume mass matrix is $M_M=\operatorname{diag}(w_i^M)\otimes I$, where $I$ acts on the state components and $\otimes$ is the Kronecker product. For one scalar component $\phi$, the solver's discrete integral is
\begin{equation}
 Q_M(\phi_M)=\sum_i w_i^M\phi_{M,i}.
 \label{eq:fv-discrete-integral}
\end{equation}
The transferred variables are density, momentum density, and total energy per unit volume. With $E$ denoting specific total energy, the energy variable is $\rho E$. All methods return values at the solver vertices, not control-volume averages. This distinction matters when comparing the integral of a reconstructed field with Eq.~\eqref{eq:fv-discrete-integral}.

Higher-order methods also need local slopes and curvature. These are reconstructed from neighboring source values by weighted least squares:
\begin{equation}
 \phi_j-\phi_i\simeq g_i\cdot r_{ij}+\tfrac12r_{ij}^{T}H_i r_{ij},
 \qquad r_{ij}=x_j-x_i.
 \label{eq:wls-polynomial}
\end{equation}
Here $g_i$ and $H_i$ approximate the gradient and Hessian at source vertex $i$. A linear fit omits the Hessian. For smooth data on a well-conditioned, full-rank scaled stencil with controlled mesh geometry, the linear fit has an $O(h)$ gradient error; the quadratic fit has $O(h^2)$ gradient and $O(h)$ Hessian errors. Anisotropy, weighting, and poor stencil conditioning can reduce this accuracy~\cite{BarthFrederickson1990,Barth1993,Mavriplis2003,DiskinThomas2008}. The third-order methods use quadratic reconstruction, and their achieved order is checked numerically.

\subsection{Nearest-neighbor and barycentric interpolation}
\label{sec:point-transfer}
\label{sec:barycentric-transfer}

Let $n(j)$ be the source vertex closest to target point $x_j^B$, and $r_j=x_j^B-x_{n(j)}^A$. Nearest-neighbor Taylor transfer gives
\begin{align}
 (\calI^{\mathrm{NN1}}_{AB}\phi_A)_j&=\phi_{A,n(j)},\label{eq:nn1}\\
 (\calI^{\mathrm{NN2}}_{AB}\phi_A)_j&=\phi_{A,n(j)}+g_{n(j)}\cdot r_j,\label{eq:nn2}\\
 (\calI^{\mathrm{NN3}}_{AB}\phi_A)_j&=\phi_{A,n(j)}+g_{n(j)}\cdot r_j
              +\tfrac12r_j^TH_{n(j)}r_j.\label{eq:nn3}
\end{align}
NN1 copies the nearest source value, NN2 adds its local slope, and NN3 also includes curvature. For $|r_j|=O(h)$ and sufficiently accurate derivatives, the smooth-field errors are $O(h)$, $O(h^2)$, and $O(h^3)$. As the target point moves, its nearest source vertex can change. The interpolated value can therefore be discontinuous as a function of target position.

Barycentric interpolation uses the source triangle or tetrahedron containing the target point. A conforming simplicial subdivision supplies this search geometry on other element types without adding control-volume vertices. In dimension $d$, the containing simplex has vertices $x_{i_r}^A$, $r=1,\ldots,d+1$. Its barycentric coordinates are nonnegative weights that express the target position:
\begin{equation}
 x_j^B=\sum_{r=1}^{d+1}\lambda_r x_{i_r}^A,
 \qquad \sum_r\lambda_r=1,
 \qquad \lambda_r\geq0.
 \label{eq:barycentric-coordinates}
\end{equation}
The same weights define BAR2 and its gradient-corrected extension:
\begin{align}
 (\calI^{\mathrm{BAR2}}_{AB}\phi_A)_j
 &=\sum_r\lambda_r\phi_{A,i_r},\label{eq:bar2}\\
 (\calI^{\mathrm{BAR3}}_{AB}\phi_A)_j
 &=\sum_r\lambda_r\left[\phi_{A,i_r}
       +\tfrac12g_{i_r}\cdot(x_j^B-x_{i_r}^A)\right].\label{eq:bar3}
\end{align}
BAR2 reproduces affine fields, meaning a linear function plus a constant, and stays within the values at the containing-simplex vertices. For a quadratic field with exact vertex gradients, the half-gradient term cancels the interpolation remainder, giving BAR3 third-order consistency. Shared values and gradients make the interpolant continuous across conforming simplex interfaces. Neither method constrains the source-to-target integral.

Limiting reduces reconstructed slopes or curvature to control new extrema. Multiplying the Taylor corrections by $0\leq\Phi_i\leq1$ reduces NN2 or NN3 to NN1 when $\Phi_i=0$, but reduces BAR3 to BAR2. Smooth-field verification uses no limiter. Discontinuous-field tests also use Barth--Jespersen (BJ) limiting~\cite{BarthJespersen1989}. A limiter based on linear increments need not bound a quadratic reconstruction everywhere, so bounds are checked on the final transferred values.

\subsection{Galerkin projection and cross-mesh integration}
\label{sec:galerkin-projection}
\label{sec:simplicial-representation}

Galerkin projection chooses the target field that best matches the reconstructed source field in the $L_2$ norm. It solves for the target values together rather than interpolating each vertex independently. Let $\phi_A^h$ be the piecewise-polynomial source field and $V_B^p$ the space of continuous degree-$p$ Lagrange functions on the target simplicial mesh. The target field $\phi_B^h\in V_B^p$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
 (\phi_B^h-\phi_A^h,v_B)_{L_2(\Omega)}=0
 \quad\text{for every }v_B\in V_B^p.
 \label{eq:galerkin-orthogonality}
\end{equation}
Thus the transfer error has zero integral when weighted by any target test function. This condition gives the best $L_2$ approximation of $\phi_A^h$ in $V_B^p$~\cite{FarrellMaddison2011,JiaoHeath2004}. Expanding the target field as $\phi_B^h=\sum_jv_jN_j^B$, with nodal basis functions $N_j^B$ and coefficients $v_j$, gives
\begin{equation}
 H_Bv=b,\qquad
 (H_B)_{ij}=\int_\Omega N_i^BN_j^B\dd x,
 \qquad b_i=\int_\Omega N_i^B\phi_A^h\dd x.
 \label{eq:galerkin-system}
\end{equation}
The sparse positive-definite matrix $H_B$ couples basis functions that share target elements. It is different from the diagonal finite-volume matrix $M_B$ used in time integration.

The two meshes generally do not share element boundaries. A target element can overlap several source elements, across which the source polynomial changes. Integration must account for these intersections:
\begin{equation}
 b_i=\sum_{K_B}\sum_{K_A:\,|K_A\cap K_B|>0}
      \int_{K_A\cap K_B}N_i^B\phi_A^h\dd x.
 \label{eq:galerkin-load-intersections}
\end{equation}
Here $K_A$ and $K_B$ are source and target simplices, and $|K_A\cap K_B|$ is their intersection measure. Their intersections form a common refinement. Both fields have one polynomial expression on each intersection, allowing exact polynomial quadrature: degree two for $P^1$/$P^1$ transfer and degree four for $P^2$/$P^2$ transfer on affine simplices~\cite{Dunavant1985,Keast1986}. The intersections must cover the domain without gaps or overlap. This geometric integration and the coupled solve distinguish GP from point interpolation.

GP2 uses piecewise-linear ($P^1$) source and target fields. GP3 reconstructs a quadratic ($P^2$) source field, including internal edge values, and solves for a complete quadratic target field. The solver receives only the original-vertex values. Restricting the quadratic field in this way can change its finite-volume integral, as illustrated in \ref{app:quadrature-mismatch}. Mixed-element meshes use $P^p$ fields on conforming simplicial subdivisions, rather than $Q^p$ fields on the original elements. Under smoothness and mesh-stability assumptions, the nominal orders are two and three; the accuracy of the vertex values is tested separately.

\subsection{Conservative control-volume transfer}
\label{sec:cv-transfer}

The CV method matches source and target field integrals over each target control volume. Let $C_i^M$ surround vertex $i$ on mesh $M$, with $w_i^M=|C_i^M|$, and let $C_i^M\cap K$ be its part in element $K$. Both meshes represent the field as $\phi_M^h=\sum_k\phi_{M,k}\psi_k^M$. On triangles and tetrahedra, $\psi_k^M=N_k^M$ is the usual $P^1$ hat function. A quadrilateral is divided by joining its edges to the vertex-average center. The value at that center is a weighted combination of the original nodal values, rather than an independent unknown. Nonsimplicial three-dimensional elements use analogous centered subdivisions. These dependent values are chosen so that
\begin{equation}
 \int_K\psi_k^M\dd x=|C_k^M\cap K|
 \qquad\text{for every element $K$ and vertex $k$ of $M$.}
 \label{eq:cv-lumped-exact}
\end{equation}
For a quadrilateral, vertex $k$ has center weight $|C_k^M\cap K|/|K|$. Summing over the elements gives $\int_\Omega\psi_k^M\dd x=w_k^M$ and therefore $\int_\Omega\phi_M^h\dd x=Q_M(\phi_M)$. The reconstructed field and the solver thus have the same domain integral on every supported element type.

The source field interpolates nodal solver values continuously; it is not reconstructed from source-cell averages. Its domain integral equals $Q_A$, but its integral over an individual source control volume need not equal $w_i^A\phi_{A,i}$. Before limiting and boundary treatment, CV preserves the source integral over the whole domain and matches the reconstructed field integral over each target control volume. It does not preserve each source nodal content.

On parallelograms, the center weights give the arithmetic vertex average and reproduce affine fields. On quadrilaterals that remain distorted, they do not: the weights enforce Eq.~\eqref{eq:cv-lumped-exact} at the expense of exact affine reproduction.

The $n_B$ target vertex values are defined by
\begin{equation}
 \int_{C_j^B}\left(\phi_B^h-\phi_A^h\right)\dd x=0,
 \qquad j=1,\ldots,n_B.
 \label{eq:cv-condition}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:cv-condition} determines the target nodal values by matching one integral per target control volume. In finite-element terms, it is a Petrov--Galerkin condition: the trial functions are $\psi_k^B$, while the test functions equal one inside a target control volume and zero outside. Intersection-based $P^1$-conservative interpolation~\cite{AlauzetMehrenberger2010,Alauzet2016} instead preserves integrals over target elements, limits an element-wise linear reconstruction, and obtains vertex values by explicit volume-weighted averaging over incident elements. Matching the solver control volumes directly requires a sparse solve:
\begin{equation}
 \widetilde H_B\phi_B=\tilde b,\qquad
 (\widetilde H_B)_{jk}=\int_{C_j^B}\psi_k^B\dd x,
 \qquad \tilde b_j=\int_{C_j^B}\phi_A^h\dd x.
 \label{eq:cv-system}
\end{equation}
The functions $\psi_k^B$ sum to one, giving row sums $w_j^B$; Eq.~\eqref{eq:cv-lumped-exact} gives column sums $w_k^B$. The entries are nonnegative and couple the same elements as $H_B$. They depend only on the target geometry and are evaluated in closed form. The matrix is symmetric on triangles, strictly convex quadrilaterals, and tetrahedra. Vertex permutations exchange the median-dual pieces of a simplex; on a quadrilateral, each center weight is proportional to the corresponding median-dual piece.

The right-hand side integrates the piecewise-linear source field over each target control volume. It is assembled from intersections of source subelements and target median-dual pieces:
\begin{equation}
 \tilde b_j=\sum_{K_B\ni j}\ \sum_{K_A:\,|K_A\cap K_B|>0}
      \int_{K_A\cap K_B\cap C_j^B}\phi_A^h\dd x,
 \label{eq:cv-load-intersections}
\end{equation}
Here $K_B$ ranges over target elements incident to $j$, and $K_A$ over source subelements. In these calculations, the meshes cover the same polygonal domain and the uncovered measure is below $10^{-20}$ of the domain. Equation~\eqref{eq:cv-system} is solved to a relative residual tolerance of $10^{-15}$. Its unknowns are nodal values, not the control-volume averages $\tilde b_j/w_j^B$.

Summing Eq.~\eqref{eq:cv-condition} over the target control volumes, which tile $\Omega$, and using the column sums of $\widetilde H_B$ gives
\begin{equation}
 Q_B(\phi_B)=\bm1^T\widetilde H_B\phi_B=\bm1^T\tilde b
   =\int_\Omega\phi_A^h\dd x=Q_A(\phi_A),
 \label{eq:cv-global}
\end{equation}
to within integration and solve tolerances. Locally, Eq.~\eqref{eq:cv-condition} matches $\int_{C_j^B}\phi_B^h\dd x$ to the source field integral over $C_j^B$. This integral differs from the nodal content $w_j^B\phi_{B,j}$ by $(\widetilde H_B\phi_B)_j-w_j^B\phi_{B,j}$. These differences sum to zero but need not vanish individually. Transferring back to the same mesh, $B=A$, gives $\tilde b=\widetilde H_A\phi_A$ and recovers $\phi_A$. If both representations reproduce affine fields, uniqueness of Eq.~\eqref{eq:cv-system} also makes their transfer exact. CV2 is therefore nominally second order on simplicial meshes and quadrilaterals that remain parallelograms under refinement. Persistent quadrilateral distortion reduces the attainable order. No higher-order CV method is used.

On simplicial meshes, CV2 and GP2 use the same continuous $P^1$ source and target fields. Their test functions differ. GP2 weights the error by continuous hat functions, giving the best $L_2$ approximation and preserving the global field integral and coordinate moments. CV2 matches integrals over target control volumes and preserves the global finite-volume integral before limiting and boundary treatment. It generally preserves neither coordinate moments nor the minimum $L_2$ error. Both require a coupled solve: changing the mesh in one region can alter transferred values elsewhere. On quadrilaterals, their field representations also differ. GP2 triangulates without added vertices; CV2 uses a centered subdivision with dependent center values.

The CV recovery matrix is symmetric positive definite on triangular, strictly convex quadrilateral, and tetrahedral meshes. Symmetry follows from the median-dual construction, and strict diagonal dominance gives positive definiteness on these element types. These properties do not generally extend to mixed three-dimensional cells. All numerical comparisons here are two-dimensional; persistent quadrilateral distortion is the main limitation on CV accuracy.

CV2 denotes the limited transfer, and CV2-u the unlimited construction. Flow comparisons use the same bound-relaxation tolerance as GP2, $\tau=2\times10^{-3}$. Limiting, boundary conditions, and positivity corrections can change the unlimited field. Its conservation properties must therefore be checked again in the field actually used by the solver.

\subsection{Conservation and bounds in the field used by the solver}
\label{sec:global-correction}
\label{sec:galerkin-vertex-output}
\label{sec:galerkin-limiter}

Galerkin projection preserves the integral of the complete finite-element field if the unconstrained target space contains constants. It also preserves coordinate moments represented in the test space. These statements follow from Eq.~\eqref{eq:galerkin-orthogonality}, but do not imply conservation over arbitrary finite-volume cells: a function that is one in a cell and zero elsewhere generally lies outside a continuous finite-element space. CV2 uses precisely these cell indicators in Eq.~\eqref{eq:cv-condition} and preserves the finite-volume total through Eq.~\eqref{eq:cv-global}. Neither the Galerkin mass-matrix inverse nor $\widetilde H_B^{-1}$ is local. Unchanged mesh geometry in one region does not guarantee unchanged transferred values there.

For GP2, the field integral and the finite-volume integral agree when $w_i^M=\int_\Omega N_i^M\dd x$ on both meshes. This holds on simplicial meshes. It generally fails when a quadrilateral mesh is triangulated, because the hat-function integrals differ from the median-dual measures. CV2 enforces the corresponding equality through Eq.~\eqref{eq:cv-lumped-exact} on every supported element type. GP3 adds another difference: reconstructing a quadratic source field and then retaining only target vertices changes the representation. Even an exact quadratic projection can then have a second-order finite-volume integral error (\ref{app:quadrature-mismatch}). Third-order point-value accuracy does not imply third-order accuracy of the solver's integral.

The bounded global correction can enforce Eq.~\eqref{eq:fv-discrete-integral} for any base transfer. For an uncorrected target field $\phi_B^r$, the correction $c$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
 \sum_jw_j^Bc_j=Q_A(\phi_A)-Q_B(\phi_B^r),
 \qquad \ell_j\leq\phi_{B,j}^r+c_j\leq u_j.
 \label{eq:global-correction-constraint}
\end{equation}
Here $\ell_j$ and $u_j$ are nodal bounds. The correction is distributed among vertices that can change without exceeding those bounds; vertices already at a bound remain fixed~\cite{ShashkovWendroff2004,KucharikShashkovWendroff2003}. The requested integral must be feasible within the weighted bounds. A small total integral error does not require every nodal correction to be small, and separate bounds on conserved components do not guarantee positive pressure.

GP and CV limiters use the extrema of overlapping source vertices as target bounds. Hard limiting uses these extrema directly; soft limiting widens the range by $\tau$ times the source range. Values outside the bounds are clipped, then the resulting integral change is redistributed to preserve a selected total: the simplicial lumped total for GP and $Q_B$ for CV. This operation can remove Galerkin orthogonality and the best-approximation property. For CV, it changes individual target-volume integrals while preserving their sum.

Boundary values are interpolated from the source solution on the matching physical boundary. This replaces the unconstrained transfer at those vertices and can change the local GP or CV identities. Coincident source and target wall vertices retain the source wall condition. If the discretized surface changes, its new wall orientation can instead introduce a mismatch. The flow tests retain all boundary vertices, excluding this geometric effect.

For GP, CV, and globally corrected transfers, changes from boundary treatment and scalar lower bounds are followed by integral-preserving redistribution. Any remaining states with nonpositive density or internal energy are repaired while preserving the affected patch totals. These steps preserve the selected global integral but can change local constraints. Global redistribution can also alter a uniform far-field region: any vertex with room within its relaxed bounds can receive part of the correction. A transfer can therefore preserve the domain integral while changing the fluxes through open boundaries during the next flow step.

\subsection{Locally conservative flux correction}
\label{sec:local-flux-correction}

The Riemann tests include an alternative that redistributes corrections only between neighboring target volumes. This locally conservative (LC) flux correction replaces each method's usual limiting, global correction, and boundary-state recovery. Let $\tilde b_j$ be the source field integral over target control volume $j$, as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:cv-system}. The corrected nodal content is
\begin{equation}
 w_j^B\phi_j^{\mathrm{LC}}=\tilde b_j+\sum_k\alpha_{jk}F_{jk},
 \qquad F_{jk}=-F_{kj},\quad \alpha_{jk}=\alpha_{kj}\in[0,1].
 \label{eq:local-flux-correction}
\end{equation}
The fluxes connect vertices sharing a target element. Applied in full, they recover the integral-corrected unlimited transfer. Each exchange adds to one volume what it removes from its neighbor, so the domain sum is unchanged even when limiting permits only part of a flux. Local conservation here allows neighboring volumes to exchange content; it does not require each nodal content to remain equal to $\tilde b_j$.

The starting low-order state, $\tilde b_j/w_j^B$, is bounded. Iterative flux-corrected transport~\cite{Zalesak1979} adds as much of the correction as the bounds allow, with a relaxation of $2\times10^{-3}$ of the source range. At most 12 limiting passes are used. Paired fluxes ensure conservation, but the pass limit can leave part of the unlimited correction unapplied. Only the Riemann calculations use LC; the airfoil calculations use the standard treatment above.

\section{Effect of remeshing on multistep time integration}
\label{sec:restart-consistency}

BDF2 uses two stored solutions. If either changes during transfer, the next time-step equation changes. We compare a transferred restart with a reference continuation on the same target mesh, so both use the same spatial discretization. The BDF2 coefficients then give the exact change in the residual. Predicting the resulting state or load change also requires the spatial operator and the accuracy of the nonlinear solve.

\subsection{Stored states and the target-mesh reference}
\label{sec:unsteady-discretization}
\label{sec:native-transfer-decomposition}

Between remeshing events, the semi-discrete equations are
\begin{equation}
 M_M\dot U_M+F_M(U_M,t)=0.
 \label{eq:semidiscrete}
\end{equation}
Here $t$ is physical time and $F_M$ is the assembled spatial residual, including fluxes, source terms, and boundary treatment. The state contains every variable used by the time integrator, including RANS turbulence variables. Boundary constraints must be applied consistently to the stored states and the equations. At constant time step, BDF2 on the target mesh is
\begin{equation}
 \Phi_B(Y;V,W)=\frac{M_B}{\Delta t}
        \left(\tfrac32Y-2V+\tfrac12W\right)+F_B(Y,t^{n+1})=0.
 \label{eq:bdf2}
\end{equation}
The unknown is the next state $Y$; $V$ and $W$ are the stored states at $t^n$ and $t^{n-1}$. Each mesh is stationary during a time step. Continuously moving meshes would require compatible geometric histories and a discrete geometric conservation law~\cite{KoobusFarhat1999,GeuzaineGrandmontFarhat2003}; Eq.~\eqref{eq:bdf2} does not cover that case.

Let $U_A^k$ and $U_B^k$ be independently computed reference solutions for the same problem on meshes $A$ and $B$. Transfer produces the target states
\begin{equation}
 \widehat U_B^k=\calI_{AB}(U_A^k),\qquad
 \eps^k=\widehat U_B^k-U_B^k,\qquad k=n,n-1.
 \label{eq:history-defects}
\end{equation}
The map $\calI_{AB}$ includes corrections for physical admissibility. The difference $\eps^k$ therefore includes both transfer error and the difference between the solutions computed on the two meshes. The target reference $U_B^k$ fixes the discretization for the comparison; it is not an exact continuum solution. \ref{app:reference-decomposition} separates these contributions.

\subsection{How errors in the stored states change the next step}

Evaluate the reference and transferred BDF2 equations at the same trial state. The spatial terms are then identical, leaving only the contributions from the two stored-state errors:

\begin{proposition}[Residual perturbation from transferred states]
\label{prop:history-forcing}
Let $\Phi_B$ be the constant-step BDF2 residual of Eq.~\eqref{eq:bdf2}, and let the transferred histories differ from the target-mesh reference states by $\eps^n$ and $\eps^{n-1}$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:history-defects}. Then, for every trial state $Y$,
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 &\Phi_B(Y;U_B^n+\eps^n,U_B^{n-1}+\eps^{n-1})
       -\Phi_B(Y;U_B^n,U_B^{n-1})\\
 &\hspace{2cm}=-\frac{M_B}{\Delta t}\eta^n,
 \qquad \eta^n=2\eps^n-\tfrac12\eps^{n-1}.
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:history-residual-identity}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
At the common trial state and time, the spatial residual and the term $3M_BY/(2\Delta t)$ cancel. The remaining difference is $(M_B/\Delta t)(-2\eps^n+\eps^{n-1}/2)$.\qed
\end{proof}

The latest and older errors enter with different weights and opposite signs. Equation~\eqref{eq:history-residual-identity} is exact for finite errors and nonlinear $F_B$, because BDF2 depends linearly on the stored states. If boundary conditions modify a residual equation, they must also modify its predicted perturbation. This identity describes a change in the equation, not yet the change in its computed solution.

To relate that residual change to the solution, define $\delta^{n+1}=\widehat U_B^{n+1}-U_B^{n+1}$. Let $r$ and $\widehat r$ be the algebraic residuals left by the reference and perturbed solves. Subtracting their equations and expanding about the reference next state gives
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 A_B\delta^{n+1}&=M_B\eta^n+\Delta t(\widehat r-r)
                 -\Delta t\,\mathcal N_B(\delta^{n+1})\\
 A_B&=\tfrac32M_B+\Delta t J_B,
 \qquad J_B=\frac{\partial F_B}{\partial U}.
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:linearized-bdf2-response}
\end{equation}
Here $J_B$ is evaluated at the reference solution at $t^{n+1}$, and $\mathcal N_B$ contains the nonlinear Taylor remainder. A locally Lipschitz Jacobian gives $\mathcal N_B(\delta)=O(\|\delta\|^2)$. If the step is locally invertible, the errors are small, and the solves are sufficiently converged, then
\begin{equation}
 \delta^{n+1}=R_B\eta^n+O(\Delta t\|\eta^n\|^2),
 \qquad R_B=A_B^{-1}M_B.
 \label{eq:state-response}
\end{equation}
The response matrix $R_B$ describes how the target time step converts stored-state errors into a solution change. The approximation applies locally at the chosen mesh, state, time step, and norm. Limiter switching or incomplete convergence can invalidate it while leaving the exact residual identity valid.

A superscript $\star$ denotes a computed steady reference state. Table~\ref{tab:naca-history-decomposition} assigns the same spatial discrepancy $\eps^\star=\calI_{AB}U_A^\star-U_B^\star$ to the two stored states independently.

Writing the corresponding responses as $\delta_f$, $\delta_n$, and $\delta_{n-1}$, local linearity predicts
\begin{equation}
 \delta_n\simeq\tfrac43\delta_f,\qquad
 \delta_{n-1}\simeq-\tfrac13\delta_f,\qquad
 \delta_f\simeq\delta_n+\delta_{n-1}.
 \label{eq:naca-history-ratios}
\end{equation}
The older-only case reveals an error that checking the latest state alone would miss. All imposed errors are multiples of the same field, so these ratios test the linear response to that field. They do not characterize the action of $R_B$ on arbitrary errors.

\subsection{Aerodynamic response}
\label{sec:delayed-response}

For a differentiable target-mesh output $G_B$, define $\Delta G^{n+1}=G_B(\widehat U_B^{n+1})-G_B(U_B^{n+1})$. With $g_B^{n+1}$ the gradient of $G_B$ with respect to the state at the target-mesh reference solution, Eq.~\eqref{eq:state-response} yields
\begin{equation}
 \Delta G^{n+1}=(g_B^{n+1})^TR_B\eta^n+O(\|\eta^n\|^2).
 \label{eq:force-response}
\end{equation}
The load response therefore depends on where the error occurs and how the target dynamics carry it into the output. A domain integral alone cannot describe this weighting. Preserving the source integral imposes, componentwise,
\begin{equation}
 \bm1^TM_B\eps^k=Q_A(U_A^k)-Q_B(U_B^k).
 \label{eq:gc-native-mismatch}
\end{equation}
The right-hand side need not be zero: the independently computed reference solutions can have different integrals. Matching the source integral need not bring the transferred solution, or its loads, closer to the target-mesh reference.

Later steps use both the newly computed state and a retained older state. Their errors can continue to influence the flow, so the first load difference need not be the largest. A disturbance away from the airfoil can produce a delayed acoustic or convective response. Projection and implicit time integration can also introduce small immediate changes outside the perturbed region. Arrival times are therefore measured with finite-amplitude thresholds.


\section{Numerical experiments and results}
\label{sec:methodology}
\label{sec:results}

The methods included in each test follow its purpose. NN3, BAR3, and GP3 test third-order accuracy in smooth fields and its relation to bounds and integral preservation in discontinuous fields and the Riemann problem. Third-order variants are also included in the controlled BDF2 restarts. The repeated-remeshing Euler airfoil and RANS comparisons use NN2, GP2, and CV2 at a common nominal second order, which is the order of the CV formulation studied here. Those aerodynamic tests do not compare second- and third-order transfer.


\subsection{Single-transfer tests}
\label{sec:static-verification}

\subsubsection{Smooth-field accuracy}

A static test measures transfer alone, without evolving the flow. We first transfer the smooth asymmetric field
\begin{equation}
 \phi=\rho_\infty\left[2+0.3e^{0.8x/L_x+0.5y/L_y}
              \sin(2\pi x/L_x)\sin(2\pi y/L_y)\right].
 \label{eq:asymmetric-manufactured-field}
\end{equation}
Here $\rho_\infty$ is reference density, and $L_x$ and $L_y$ are coordinate length scales. Target meshes are either structured quadrilaterals or independently generated mixed triangles and quadrilaterals, with nominal resolutions 64, 128, and 256. Source resolution is half the target resolution.

We measure the relative nodal density error as $E_2=[\sum_j(\rho_{B,j}-\rho(x_j^B))^2/\sum_j\rho(x_j^B)^2]^{1/2}$. Equal vertex weights make this a point-value measure, rather than a volume-weighted field error. Refinement ratios use the effective spacing $h_{\mathrm{eff}}=N_{\mathrm{nodes}}^{-1/2}$ on the fixed domain. The order between successive levels is
\begin{equation}
 p_{k,k+1}=\frac{\log(E_k/E_{k+1})}{\log(h_k/h_{k+1})}.
 \label{eq:pairwise-observed-order}
\end{equation}
Here $E_k$ and $h_k$ are the error and effective spacing at level $k$. A least-squares fit of $\log E$ against $\log h$ also gives an order over all three levels.
\label{sec:results-asymmetric}

The NN, BAR, and GP methods recover their nominal smooth-field orders (Figure~\ref{fig:asym-l2-convergence}). NN1 is first order; NN2, BAR2, and GP2 are consistent with second order; NN3 is consistent with third order. BAR3 and GP3 have slopes slightly above three, approaching approximately 3.2 on the finest pair. On the finest mixed mesh, the relative errors are approximately $2.6\times10^{-7}$ for BAR3, $3.4\times10^{-7}$ for GP3, and $2.6\times10^{-6}$ for NN3.

CV2 depends more strongly on element geometry. Its fitted order is 2.03 on the structured mesh, but 1.49 on the mixed mesh and 1.35 on its finest pair. This agrees with the loss of affine reproduction on quadrilaterals that remain distorted under refinement (Sec.~\ref{sec:cv-transfer}). CV2 preserves discrete mass to within $4\times10^{-15}$ on both mesh families. The finest-mesh GP2 mass errors are $4.3\times10^{-12}$ (structured) and $2.5\times10^{-7}$ (mixed).

\subsection{Repeated remeshing of a Riemann problem}

\subsubsection{Setup and error measures}
\label{sec:riemann-methodology}

The Riemann problem tests whether the behavior of one transfer predicts what happens after many transfers. We solve the two-dimensional Euler equations on $[0,5]\times[0,1]$, starting from a uniform reference mesh with $501\times101$ vertices and spacing $h=0.01$. The ideal gas has heat-capacity ratio $\gamma=1.4$ and unit gas constant. Density, pressure, and temperature are $\rho$, $p$, and $T$; the Cartesian velocities are $u$ and $v$. Initially,
\begin{equation}
 (\rho,u,v,p)=
 \begin{cases}
 (1,0,0,1), & x<2,\\
 (0.125,0,0,0.1), & x\geq2,
 \end{cases},
 \label{eq:riemann-initial-state}
\end{equation}
giving left and right temperatures of 1 and 0.8. Upper and lower boundaries are inviscid slip walls. Characteristic conditions at $x=0$ and $x=5$ prescribe total pressure and temperature $(p_0,T_0)=(1,1)$ and $(0.1,0.8)$, respectively, with direction $(1,0)$ at both ends.

The calculation runs for 400 physical steps with $\Delta t=10^{-3}$. At each remeshing event, interior vertices receive fresh random streamwise displacements relative to the original mesh. Boundary vertices remain fixed, and geometric distortion does not accumulate from mesh to mesh. If $a_{i,\min}$ is the smallest altitude of the corner triangles incident to vertex $i$, its maximum displacement is $a_{i,\min}/s$. We use deformation parameters $s=4$ and $s=2$; the latter allows twice the displacement.

Remeshing every ten or two physical steps compares less and more frequent transfer. Both BDF2 states are transferred at every mesh change. The lower-frequency comparison has 80 transfer evaluations; the higher-frequency integral record has 398. A fixed-mesh calculation with the same initial and boundary conditions supplies a numerical reference. Differences from it include both transfer error and the changed spatial discretization.

The density profile is averaged in $y$. A first width measure uses the window $W_1=[2.40,3.00]$ and estimates plateau densities at its ends. Position is the 50\% crossing; width is the distance between the 10\% and 90\% crossings. Their differences from the fixed-mesh reference, $\Delta x$ and $\Delta w$, are expressed in cells of spacing $h=0.01$. After many transfers, the smeared contact can enter the left plateau interval $[2.40,2.46]$. The measured width then combines contact and shock distortion. A second, shock-only measure uses $[2.55,2.85]$ to exclude the contact tail. The wide-window tables use 16 sampling lines and 800 probes; the shock-only measure uses the stored eight-line mean profile at the same streamwise resolution. Both are evaluated at step 399 against their corresponding fixed-mesh references.

We measure mass changes in two ways. First, the change immediately across a transfer is $\Delta I_\rho$, target minus source, with $I_\rho=Q_M(\rho_M)$. The normalized signed sum $\sum\Delta I_\rho/I_{\rho,0}$ includes transfer alone. Second, final mass drift is $[I_\rho(t)-I_{\rho,0}]/I_{\rho,0}$, which also includes changes during flow evolution. Here $I_{\rho,0}$ is initial mass. The exact mass and energy for this Sod problem are 2.375 and 5.75, since no physical wave reaches an open boundary by $t=0.399$. Density accuracy is measured by the median-dual-volume-weighted mean absolute error against the exact Sod solution~\cite{LeVeque2002} at that time.

The LC comparison contains 36 matched pairs covering NN1--NN3, BAR2--BAR3, GP2--GP3, and CV2. All methods use intervals of ten and two steps at $s=4$. With the shorter interval, NN1, BAR2, GP2, and CV2 also use three realizations at both $s=4$ and $s=2$. Each standard/LC pair follows the same mesh sequence and ends on the same mesh. NN1+GC has no LC counterpart. Percentage changes in density error are computed within each pair before averaging. Final mass and energy drift are measured from the double-precision restart after the last transfer.

\subsubsection{Mass balance and local flux correction}
\label{sec:results-local-conservation}

Final mass drift reveals changes that the transfer-only sum misses (Figure~\ref{fig:riemann-mass-error}a). Standard CV2 gives final relative mass drift of approximately $3.0\times10^{-7}$--$4.1\times10^{-7}$ at $s=4$ and $5.5\times10^{-6}$--$6.4\times10^{-6}$ at $s=2$, despite preserving mass during transfer to near roundoff. GP2 also has finite final drift, reaching about $2\times10^{-6}$ at $s=4$. The final-step budgets place the CV2 change in the flow step: at $s=2$, realization 1, step 399 changes mass by $2.7\times10^{-8}$ of the initial mass, and the following transfer change is negligible. Supplementary Section S5 gives representative budgets.

The standard GP/CV limiter clips values to its bounds and redistributes the integral change globally, according to the remaining capacity at each vertex. With bound relaxation $2\times10^{-3}$, even a constant far-field region can receive a correction. Measured density disturbances reach $1.4\times10^{-4}$ for $x<1$, and momentum density at the open boundaries reaches approximately $2\times10^{-4}$. NN and BAR retain the constant far-field states to roundoff. The measurements connect global redistribution with far-field disturbances and later boundary mass exchange. A separate experiment with the limiter disabled was not performed, so its effect has not been isolated in that way.

LC keeps final relative mass drift below $5\times10^{-13}$ and energy drift below $7\times10^{-13}$ in all 36 matched cases. Its effect on density error is smaller (Figure~\ref{fig:riemann-mass-error}b). Mean changes relative to standard transfer are $-3.3\%$ for GP and $-2.9\%$ for CV, compared with $+0.2\%$ for NN and $-0.3\%$ for BAR. At 398 transfers and $s=2$, GP2 errors decrease by 7.0--7.9\% and CV2 errors by 5.1--5.4\% across the three realizations. Their wide-window position errors fall from $+0.17$--$+0.20$ cell to $0.00$--$+0.02$ cell. Shock-only broadening changes little (Supplementary Section S5). Local flux correction therefore improves mass balance and some solution errors without removing the diffusion of the underlying transfer.

\subsection{Controlled BDF2 restarts over an airfoil}

\subsubsection{Setup and error measures}
\label{sec:naca-fixed-methodology}

Controlled airfoil restarts separate the effects of the latest and older stored BDF2 states. The Euler flow is over the National Advisory Committee for Aeronautics (NACA) 0012 airfoil at freestream Mach number $M_\infty=0.1$ and angle of attack $\alpha=6^\circ$, with $p_\infty=\rho_\infty=T_\infty=1$, $a_\infty=\sqrt{1.4}$, $U_\infty=0.118322$, and $\Delta t=10^{-3}$. The subscript $\infty$ denotes freestream values, $a_\infty$ is sound speed, $U_\infty$ is flow speed, and $c$ is airfoil chord.

The source and independent target meshes have 16,255 and 22,508 vertices, with 32,077 and 44,583 triangles. They share 370 airfoil vertices and a 63-vertex farfield polygon at $100c$, keeping the discretized boundary unchanged. Forces use pressure only, unit reference chord and area, and a moment reference at $x/c=0.25$. Lift and drag coefficients are $C_L$ and $C_D$. Any nonzero steady drag is numerical, rather than skin friction or shock drag.

Each steady reference is continued with equal initial BDF2 states. We form the combinations in Table~\ref{tab:naca-history-decomposition} on the target mesh, then compare each restart with the reference continuation at the same physical step. State differences use the nondimensional volume-weighted norm $\|v\|_{M_B}=(v^TM_Bv)^{1/2}$ over stored mean-flow components. Supplementary tests also use the unweighted Euclidean norm $\|v\|_2$; the two norms give different weight to small near-body cells and large far-field cells. To measure departure from the linear superposition in Eq.~\eqref{eq:naca-history-ratios}, we define
\begin{equation}
 e_{\mathrm{sup}}^U=
 \frac{\|\delta_f-\delta_n-\delta_{n-1}\|_{M_B}}{\|\delta_f\|_{M_B}},
 \qquad
 e_{\mathrm{sup}}^{C_L}=
 \frac{|\Delta C_{L,f}-\Delta C_{L,n}-\Delta C_{L,n-1}|}{|\Delta C_{L,f}|}.
 \label{eq:history-superposition-error}
\end{equation}
State-norm ratios are nonnegative; force ratios retain their sign.

The solver reports $-\Phi_B$. We compare residuals before any update, using the same trial state within each pair. Older-only and reference cases use $U_B^\star$; latest-only and both-states cases use $\widehat U_B^\star$. The corresponding differences in $\eta$ are $-\eps^\star/2$ and $+\eps^\star/2$. Double-precision residuals are split into wall-normal and tangential components to distinguish the temporal terms from slip-wall enforcement. \ref{app:history-checks} gives additional cancellation and amplitude tests.

\subsubsection{Effects of the latest and older stored states}
\label{sec:results-bdf2-new}

For every method in Figure~\ref{fig:naca-history-new}, its sign is opposite to that obtained by perturbing both states. GP2 gives lift differences of $+2.453\times10^{-3}$ and $-7.315\times10^{-3}$, respectively; CV2 gives $+2.439\times10^{-3}$ and $-7.272\times10^{-3}$.

\subsection{Airfoil loads after remeshing}
\label{sec:results-euler-airfoil-new}

The following Euler tests use the flow conditions of Sec.~\ref{sec:naca-fixed-methodology}.

\subsubsection{Initial load growth under repeated remeshing}

To examine accumulation in the airfoil calculation, we perturb vertices at wall distances between $0.01c$ and $20c$, keeping boundary vertices fixed. NN2, GP2, and CV2 use matching random deformations. A 400-step interval contains 39 remeshing events followed by flow evolution. Load differences are measured against the corresponding fixed-mesh trajectory. Fitted slopes per 100 physical steps describe this window; they are neither long-time drift estimates nor estimates of independent-sample uncertainty.

GP2 and CV2 give slower initial drag growth than NN2 in both realizations. GP2 slopes are $6.31\times10^{-6}$ and $5.89\times10^{-6}$ per 100 physical steps; CV2 gives $5.92\times10^{-6}$ and $5.82\times10^{-6}$, compared with $3.95\times10^{-5}$ and $3.84\times10^{-5}$ for NN2. The mean NN2 slope is approximately 6.4 times the GP2 mean and 6.6 times the CV2 mean. However, 400 steps span only about 0.047 convective and 0.47 acoustic chord times. The result describes early growth, and lift trends vary more between realizations.

\subsubsection{Delayed response to a disturbance away from the wall}

A separate experiment isolates one transfer in a narrow band away from the wall. The bands begin at wall distances $0.05c$, $0.10c$, and $0.20c$. GP2 is used in all three, and CV2 repeats the longer $0.20c$-band calculation. Fixed sampling shells measure the root-mean-square (RMS) pressure difference from a common fixed-mesh reference. Force onset is defined by a fraction of the response maximum. For a shell at wall distance $r$ inside a band beginning at $d$, the low-Mach acoustic travel time is approximately $(d-r)/(a_\infty\Delta t)\simeq845(d-r)$ steps. Transfer already produces immediate changes up to approximately $0.08c$ inward of the displaced band. These shells are excluded when fitting propagation times from amplitude thresholds.

Beyond the region changed immediately by transfer, the pressure response is delayed (Figure~\ref{fig:euler-pressure}). For the longer calculation with the band beginning at $0.20c$, estimated propagation speeds are 1.09--1.27 with GP2 and 1.10--1.30 with CV2, using 10--75\% amplitude thresholds. Both ranges include the sound speed $a_\infty=1.183$. Their spread shows the threshold sensitivity of a single event, rather than variability across independent events.

A separate restart test also shows why the first load difference is insufficient. The NN1 lift difference peaks three steps after the first post-transfer sample, at 4.66 times its initial value. The sampled second- and third-order responses peak at the first step and then decay.

\subsection{Fixed-body RANS calculations}

\subsubsection{Setup and numerical sensitivity tests}
\label{sec:rans-methodology}

The RANS tests ask whether the transfer differences persist with turbulence transport and viscous loads. Prescribed remeshing disturbs a nominally steady solution; we compare load changes between methods and check their sensitivity to numerical settings. The conditions are $M_\infty=0.1$, $\alpha=6^\circ$, and chord Reynolds number $Re_c=6\times10^6$, using Spalart--Allmaras (SA) and shear-stress-transport (SST) models~\cite{SpalartAllmaras1994,Menter1994}.

The coarse mesh has 23,271 vertices, 9,686 boundary-layer and wake quadrilaterals, 26,851 triangles, and 256 airfoil vertices. Medium and fine meshes have 32,102 and 48,228 vertices. All retain a first off-wall spacing of approximately $5\times10^{-6}c$. The farfield is at $100c$, and the quadrilateral region extends to about $0.11c$.

We use the Stanford University Unstructured (SU2) solver~\cite{EconomonEtAl2016SU2}. Mean-flow convection uses the Harten--Lax--van Leer contact (HLLC) approximate Riemann solver with a monotonic upstream-centered scheme for conservation laws (MUSCL) reconstruction. Turbulence transport uses scalar upwinding without MUSCL. BDF2 is solved by dual-time stepping at a pseudo-time Courant--Friedrichs--Lewy (CFL) number of 6. The nominal limit is 800 pseudo-time iterations per physical step. After ten iterations, the solver stops if $\log_{10}\operatorname{rms}(r_\rho)\leq-12$, with $r_\rho$ the nodal density-equation residual in solver normalization.

The dimensional physical step $\Delta t_0=10^{-3}$~s corresponds to $\Delta t_0^*=0.347224$. A superscript $*$ denotes normalization by solver reference scales. With $U_\infty^*=0.1$, the 400-step window spans approximately 13.9 chord flow-through times, measured by $tU_\infty/c$. Startup uses a first-order step ten times smaller. Each turbulence model has a fixed-mesh reference continued for 800 steps from matching stored states.

The coarse steady SST solution reaches the density-residual target. SA instead reaches its 20,000-iteration limit at $-11.2$, with lift and drag stationary to $10^{-7}$ over the last 2,000 iterations. The medium-mesh steady SST solution stops at approximately $-11.1$. These are steady-solution checks; convergence during the remeshed physical steps is assessed separately.

NN2, limited GP2, and limited CV2 follow the same prescribed mesh sequences. At each event, 12,602 vertices may move and 10,669 remain fixed. No vertex with solver wall distance below $0.01c$ moves. The geometric mask uses distance to the nearest airfoil vertex, allowing part of the outer quadrilateral region to move. The deformation parameter is $s=4$; a minimum corner-area ratio of 0.2 maintains valid cells. Boundary vertices remain fixed. Remeshing occurs every ten physical steps, independently of the solution.

Both stored states of the mean flow and turbulence variables are transferred. SA transports the working variable $\widetilde\nu$. SST transports turbulent kinetic energy $k$ and specific dissipation rate $\omega$; their density-weighted values are transferred before lower bounds are applied to the primitive variables. Wall distance and eddy viscosity are recomputed. The relative integral change for a scalar $\phi$ is $[Q_B(\phi_B)-Q_A(\phi_A)]/Q_A(\phi_A)$; all monitored source integrals are nonzero. These are transfer diagnostics. Turbulence-variable integrals need not be conserved during flow evolution because their equations have source terms. SST integrals are measured before the final lower bounds, so they do not describe the fully corrected state.

Pressure and viscous drag contributions are $C_{D,p}$ and $C_{D,v}$. For coefficient $C_Q$ and method $m$, the remeshing response is its difference from the fixed-mesh reference. We also compare NN2 directly with GP2:
\begin{equation}
 D_{m,Q}(t)=C_{Q,m}(t)-C_{Q,\mathrm{ref}}(t),
 \qquad P_Q(t)=D_{\mathrm{NN2},Q}(t)-D_{\mathrm{GP2},Q}(t).
 \label{eq:rans-comparisons}
\end{equation}
Remeshed and reference calculations use identical surface quadrature. At $N$ common physical times $t_i$, a scalar history $q$ has RMS $\mathcal R(q)=(N^{-1}\sum_i q(t_i)^2)^{1/2}$. We compare a modified numerical setting $c$ with the nominal setting, subscript $0$, through
\begin{equation}
 S(q;c)=\frac{\mathcal R(q_c-q_0)}{\mathcal R(q_0)}.
 \label{eq:control-sensitivity}
\end{equation}
This measures the RMS difference between the histories relative to the nominal RMS. It differs from the fractional change in RMS amplitude, $[\mathcal R(q_c)-\mathcal R(q_0)]/\mathcal R(q_0)$: two curves can have similar amplitudes but differ in timing or shape.

Sensitivity tests share a 38-sample window containing three remeshing events. Inner-iteration limits are raised to 1600 for both models and to 6400 for SST. Time-step tests use $\Delta t_0/2$ for both models and $\Delta t_0/4$ for SA. The same displacement fields are applied at matching physical times, and each step size has its own fixed-mesh reference. Medium and fine steady meshes assess absolute-load sensitivity. A short medium-mesh SA transient uses the same displacement statistics and amplitude limit, but a different nodal deformation field; its differences therefore combine mesh-resolution and disturbance changes.

\subsection{Interpreting the comparisons}
\label{sec:discussion}

Spatial profiles, integrals, state norms, and loads measure different aspects of transfer error. Differences from a target-mesh reference also include differences between the independently computed mesh solutions. These distinctions agree with studies that separate integral preservation from other discrete properties~\cite{MaddisonHiester2017}, and preserve discrete equilibria to suppress transfer-induced disturbances~\cite{MaddisonCotterFarrell2011,MaddisonCotterFarrell2013}.

CV2 preserves the global finite-volume integral but loses affine reproduction on persistently distorted quadrilaterals. Limiting and boundary treatment can then change its local constraints. LC replaces global redistribution with exchanges between neighboring volumes. This treatment is tested only in the Riemann problem; the standard-transfer airfoil tests establish no LC aerodynamic benefit.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

Transfer in an unsteady calculation must supply every state used by the time integrator. In the controlled BDF2 restarts, an error in the older state changes next-step lift even when the latest state matches the target-mesh reference. The opposite signs of the latest-only and older-only responses follow from the BDF2 coefficients. The residual change is exact; converting it into a state or load prediction also requires assumptions about the spatial operator and solve convergence.

The Riemann results distinguish preservation of mass, wave shape, and mass balance during later flow evolution. Global mass correction does not restore a smeared profile. At high transfer counts, a wide-window 10--90\% width also includes contact smearing, so it must be separated from shock-only broadening. GP2 and CV2 preserve the tested profiles more closely than NN1 and BAR2, but standard global limiting can disturb the far field and allow mass changes during subsequent flow steps.

Local flux correction keeps final mass and energy drift near roundoff and modestly reduces GP/CV density error without materially changing shock broadening. It allows small extrema within relaxed bounds and can leave part of the unlimited correction unapplied. Exact wall compatibility immediately after transfer is not guaranteed either.

In fixed-body RANS, smaller transfer-integral errors do not establish smaller aerodynamic errors. The differences between methods remain sensitive to time-step refinement, with additional iterative uncertainty for SST. The slower initial Euler drag growth of GP2 and CV2 therefore cannot be extended to a RANS ranking. Transfer accuracy must be assessed through both the transferred fields and the flow evolution that follows, including all stored time levels.

\section{Field integrals and reference solutions}
\label{app:quadrature-mismatch}

A projection can preserve a field exactly and still give a different integral when only its vertex values are used. For example, $f(x)=x^2$ belongs to the quadratic finite-element space on a uniform partition of $[0,1]$. Integrating its vertex values with linear nodal weights gives
\begin{equation}
 Q_h(f)=\frac13+\frac{h^2}{6}.
 \label{eq:vertex-restriction-counterexample}
\end{equation}
The complete quadratic projection is exact. The second-order integral error comes solely from discarding its nonvertex values, as occurs when GP3 supplies only solver vertices.

\subsection{Separating transfer error from mesh dependence}
\label{app:reference-decomposition}

A discrepancy from a target-mesh reference solution contains both transfer error and the difference between the mesh discretizations. To distinguish these contributions, let $\Pi_Mu^k$ represent a common exact field in the solver representation, with discretization error $e_M^k=U_M^k-\Pi_Mu^k$. For a possibly nonlinear transfer map,
\begin{align}
 \eps^k={}&\underbrace{\calI_{AB}(\Pi_Au^k)-\Pi_Bu^k}_{\text{transfer consistency}}\notag\\
 &+\underbrace{\calI_{AB}(U_A^k)-\calI_{AB}(\Pi_Au^k)-e_B^k}_{\text{source and target discretization contributions}}.
 \label{eq:defect-decomposition}
\end{align}
Only a linear map permits the second line to be written $\calI_{AB}e_A^k-e_B^k$. A load difference likewise separates as
\begin{align}
 G_B(\widehat U_B^k)-G_A(U_A^k)
 ={}&G_B(U_B^k)-G_A(U_A^k)\notag\\
 &+G_B(\widehat U_B^k)-G_B(U_B^k).
 \label{eq:observable-decomposition}
\end{align}
The first difference is dependence of the reference solution on the mesh; the second is the restart response relative to the target-mesh reference. Neither term alone measures the error relative to the exact continuum solution.

Computed lift differences are of order $10^{-9}$, but volume-weighted state differences remain 4.3--9.0\% of the imposed error (Table~\ref{tab:naca-cancellation-app}). Agreement in lift therefore does not imply agreement of the states. Iterative error has not been independently measured, so this test does not verify cancellation of the computed state response. \subsection{Remeshing response on a different mesh}

The medium-mesh SA test changes both resolution and the imposed nodal disturbance. Its short remeshing interval gives RMS lift responses near $1.07\times10^{-4}$ and drag responses near $5.39\times10^{-5}$ for individual methods. Differences between methods are $8.77\times10^{-6}$ and $9.94\times10^{-7}$. The methods remain close, but the changed random displacement field prevents attributing the smaller response to resolution alone. Five to seven of the 38 physical steps reach the nominal inner-iteration limit.
